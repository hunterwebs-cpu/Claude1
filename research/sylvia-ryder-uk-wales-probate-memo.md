## 2. Issues and Summary Conclusions

**Issue 1 — Timeline:** Given that probate administration for Ms. Ryder's estate has been ongoing since at least November 2025, is 9–10 months without a Grant, or without meaningful communication, normal, slow, or a red flag?

*Short answer:* It depends on which stage the estate is at. Obtaining the Grant of Probate itself should now typically take on the order of 2–4 months for a straightforward online application (though real-world experience in 2025–2026 varies widely, with some cases still taking considerably longer). Full administration and distribution of a straightforward estate is customarily expected to be substantially complete within the "executor's year" (12 months from death), though this is a convention, not a hard statutory deadline, and complex estates legitimately run 12–24 months. **Nine to ten months of silence, as opposed to nine to ten months of active-but-slow administration, is not normal and is a legitimate trigger for the escalation steps in Section 6**, regardless of which stage the estate is at.

**Issue 2 — What does "foreign" probate mean here, and does it change the timeline?** *Short answer:* If nothing about this estate actually crosses a border (deceased domiciled in Wales, all assets in the UK, executor and beneficiaries not needing a foreign grant recognized), there is no formal "foreign probate" process in play, and Wales/England procedure applies in full. If there genuinely is a cross-border element (e.g., a grant obtained in another country needs to be resealed in England & Wales, or vice versa, or UK assets need a separate ancillary grant), that does add real time and complexity — see Section 5.

**Issue 3 — What does the solicitor/personal representative owe the beneficiaries, and is silence for this long a breach?** *Short answer:* A personal representative (executor) has a personal, statutory duty to collect in and administer the estate "according to law" and to account for it. A solicitor retained by the executor is professionally regulated and, while the beneficiaries are not that solicitor's client, the solicitor still owes duties not to mislead or take unfair advantage of them, and the firm must operate a complaints process available to anyone affected by its service. Persistent, unexplained non-communication with a beneficiary for the better part of a year is capable of being both a breach of the executor's own duty and a service failure by the firm — see Section 6 for what follows from that.

**Issue 4 — What can a beneficiary actually do?** *Short answer:* There is a clear escalation ladder, from a written request for an account, through the firm's internal complaints procedure and the Legal Ombudsman (against the solicitor), to a High Court application under s.50 Administration of Justice Act 1985 to remove or add a personal representative in the worst cases. None of these require the beneficiary to already have a lawyer of their own, though for the court-application end of the ladder they would be well advised to get one.

## 4. Discussion / Analysis

### 4.1 The ordinary England & Wales probate timeline (Issue 1)

**Rule/framework.** Probate in England & Wales runs through two largely separate phases:

1. **Getting the Grant of Probate** (or Letters of Administration if there's no will) — the court document, issued by HM Courts & Tribunals Service (HMCTS) via the Probate Registry, that legally authorizes the executor to deal with the estate's assets. The application is made online via GOV.UK or by post using form PA1P (where there is a will). GOV.UK's own guidance states applicants will "usually get probate within 12 weeks of submitting your application," with the caveat that it "can take longer if you need to provide additional information."
2. **Administering and distributing the estate** — collecting assets, paying debts and any Inheritance Tax, and distributing to beneficiaries. This has no fixed statutory deadline; the informal industry benchmark is the "executor's year," i.e., a general expectation (not a legal requirement) that a straightforward estate is wound up within 12 months of death.

**Application to real-world 2025–2026 conditions.** Independent practitioner reporting (multiple firms; consistent across sources, though these are practitioner/marketing sources rather than official statistics and are treated here as corroborating color, not primary authority) describes a system still working through a well-documented backlog:
- Straightforward **online** applications have been reported running roughly 4–8+ weeks in mid-2026, against an HMCTS internal target of around 8 weeks that multiple sources report is "rarely met" in practice.
- **Paper** applications are consistently reported as much slower — commonly 16–20+ weeks.
- If HMCTS "stops" an application to request more information, that alone commonly adds another 4–12 weeks.
- The UK Parliament's Justice Committee opened an inquiry into probate delays after average online waits peaked at 15.8 weeks in November 2023; government figures since claim improvement for simple digital cases, but reporting also flags that the number of applications taking *nearly two years* rose sharply (cited as up from 88 cases in 2020/21 to 203 in 2024/25) — i.e., a shrinking median alongside a real, growing tail of very long outliers.

**Conclusion on Issue 1.** Nine to ten months is within the outer range of what a delayed-but-progressing Grant application, or a mid-administration complex estate, can plausibly still look like — it is not so long as to be impossible on its face. But it is *not* a period in which a beneficiary should be getting nothing: HMCTS delay explains why the Grant itself is slow; it does not explain, let alone excuse, a solicitor going silent on a beneficiary for months. Those are two different problems and should be diagnosed separately (see Recommendations).

### 4.2 What "foreign" probate would actually mean, and whether it applies here (Issue 2)

Wales and England share one body of succession law (there is no separate "Welsh probate law"); the Probate Registry that would typically handle a Wales-domiciled estate is HMCTS's Wales & Cardiff registry, but the substantive rules, forms (PA1P), and Non-Contentious Probate Rules 1987 are identical to an English case. So "foreign," in a legally meaningful sense, would only be in play here if there is an actual cross-border fact — most commonly one of:

- The deceased was domiciled outside England & Wales, or held assets abroad requiring a separate foreign grant.
- A grant already obtained in another country needs to be **resealed** in England & Wales (or vice versa) before it can be used here. Resealing is available only for a defined list of roughly 70 mostly-Commonwealth jurisdictions under the Colonial Probates Act Application Order 1965, is discretionary (the English court is not automatically bound to recognize the foreign grant), and requires lodging a certified copy of the foreign grant and will. This process is layered *on top of* ordinary Grant timelines and commonly adds meaningful extra weeks-to-months, particularly for document authentication and translation where relevant.
- One or both beneficiaries live outside the UK — this does **not**, by itself, change the English administration process; it mainly affects how funds are eventually transferred to them (currency conversion, foreign tax reporting) rather than the probate procedure itself.

**Conclusion on Issue 2.** Unless one of the above cross-border facts is actually present, this is procedurally a standard England & Wales administration, and the "foreign" framing should not be assumed to explain the delay. **This is the single biggest factual gap in the memo — confirm with the beneficiary which of these (if any) actually applies**, because it changes both the expected timeline and which registry/process is relevant.

### 4.3 Duties owed to beneficiaries, and whether prolonged silence breaches them (Issue 3)

**Rule.** Section 25 of the Administration of Estates Act 1925 (as substituted by s.9 Administration of Estates Act 1971) imposes a personal statutory duty on the personal representative (executor) to "collect and get in the real and personal estate of the deceased and administer it according to law," and, when required by the court, to "exhibit on oath... a full inventory of the estate" and "render an account of the administration of the estate." This duty runs to the estate and is enforceable by the court; it is the foundation for the general practitioner consensus (see multiple firm sources cited below, consistent with each other) that beneficiaries can properly request, and are ordinarily entitled to see, **estate accounts** showing the assets, debts, and distributions of the estate, and that unreasonable, unexplained delay can amount to a breach of duty (and, for a *professional* executor such as a solicitor acting as executor, potentially professional negligence).

Where a **solicitor is acting for the executor** rather than being the executor themself, the solicitor's direct professional-conduct client is the executor/administrator, **not** the beneficiary. This matters practically: a beneficiary cannot invoke solicitor–client duties (e.g., a duty to keep "the client" updated under the SRA Code of Conduct) as if they were the client. However, two things still constrain the solicitor's conduct toward a non-client beneficiary:

- **SRA Code of Conduct for Solicitors, RELs and RFLs**, paragraph 1.2: a solicitor must not "abuse your position by taking unfair advantage of clients or others" (i.e., others, not just clients). Paragraph 3.2 requires that "the service you provide to clients is competent and delivered in a timely manner" (client-facing, but relevant to the underlying administration a beneficiary is affected by). Paragraph 8.2 requires the firm to "establish and maintain, or participate in, a procedure for handling complaints," and firms must tell people how to complain — this complaints route is generally open to anyone affected by the firm's service, including a non-client beneficiary, not just the executor-client.
- **The Legal Ombudsman's own published position on probate is explicit and directly on point**: "In all probate cases, the solicitor's client is the executor or administrator. The Legal Ombudsman will do its best to help with these types of complaints even if the handling of the estate is not finished" — i.e., LeO treats beneficiary complaints in probate matters as within its remit even though the beneficiary isn't technically "the client," precisely because probate is a recognized exception where non-client beneficiaries are foreseeably and directly affected by the solicitor's service to the executor.

**Conclusion on Issue 3.** The unresponsive beneficiary is not without standing here. If the unresponsive party is the **solicitor**, the beneficiary can use the firm's own complaints procedure and, if unsatisfied, the Legal Ombudsman. If the unresponsive party is actually the **executor personally** (a lay person, or a solicitor acting as executor rather than merely as the executor's solicitor), the primary lever is s.25 AEA 1925 duty enforcement and, in serious cases, the s.50 AJA 1985 removal route (Section 4.4) — SRA/LeO routes would not apply to a lay executor who isn't a regulated solicitor.

### 4.4 Escalation: from a request for information to removing a personal representative

**Tier 1 — Request for an account / update, in writing.** No special legal form is required. A short letter or email to the executor (or, if a solicitor is handling it, the firm) asking for: (a) confirmation of what stage the estate is at (Grant applied for/pending/issued; assets being collected; IHT position; anticipated distribution date), and (b) a copy of the estate accounts to date. This tracks directly from the s.25 AEA 1925 duty to account and is the normal, low-conflict first step every practitioner source consulted recommends.

**Tier 2 — Larke v Nugus request (only relevant if there's a *validity* concern, not simply delay).** A Larke v Nugus request (named for the 1979 Court of Appeal case, reported at (1979) 123 Solicitors Journal 337 / [2000] WTLR 1033 — **note: I was not able to independently read the judgment text itself**; see NOT VERIFIED below) is a formal letter to the *solicitor who drafted the will* asking for their file (attendance notes, capacity assessment, instructions) where there is a genuine concern about the will's validity — undue influence, lack of capacity, lack of knowledge/approval. Multiple independent secondary sources agree the drafting solicitor is under **no strict legal duty** to comply but that Law Society guidance directs solicitors to cooperate to head off costly litigation, and that in practice disclosure of the file is now the norm following the case. **This tool is specifically for validity disputes — it is the wrong tool if the concern here is purely "I can't get updates," and the wrong solicitor to send it to if "probate counsel" is administering, not the one who drafted the will.** Flagging it because it's commonly confused with a general information request.

**Tier 3 — Complaint to the firm, then the Legal Ombudsman (if a solicitor is involved).** The firm must be given the chance to resolve the complaint first — LeO's published rule is the provider gets **eight weeks** to respond before LeO will open an investigation (and the beneficiary can go straight to LeO if eight weeks pass with no response at all). After a firm's final response (or the eight-week point), the complaint must reach LeO within **6 months** of that final response, and in any case no more than **1 year** from the act/omission complained of (or 1 year from when the beneficiary reasonably ought to have known there was a cause for complaint). If the firm is not solicitor-regulated (some probate practitioners are licensed by ICAEW or CILEx instead of the SRA), LeO can still often take the complaint, but the underlying professional regulator differs — worth confirming which regulator applies once the firm's status is known.

**Tier 4 — SRA (conduct, not service, complaints).** The SRA is the regulator for serious *conduct* issues (dishonesty, holding client money improperly, systemic failures) rather than the general "poor service/slow response" complaints that LeO is built for. A single unresponsive matter is usually a LeO-track complaint; a pattern suggesting dishonesty or a firm ignoring its regulatory obligations altogether would be reportable to the SRA.

**Tier 5 — Court application to compel or remove the personal representative.** If informal and regulatory routes fail and the underlying problem is the **executor** (not merely their solicitor) unreasonably failing to administer the estate, a beneficiary can apply to the Chancery Division. Two distinct routes commonly discussed by practitioners:
- An application for an order requiring the executor to carry out specific administration acts within a set timeframe.
- An application under **s.50 Administration of Justice Act 1985** for the court to appoint a substitute or additional personal representative, or to terminate the appointment of an existing one, on application by an existing personal representative *or a beneficiary*. I read this section directly (legislation.gov.uk): it confirms the court's discretion to appoint a replacement (who becomes executor if appointed alongside an executor, or administrator otherwise) and to authorize that replacement to charge for their services; a "beneficiary" for these purposes is defined as anyone with a beneficial interest in the estate, whether under the will or on intestacy. This is a real, but comparatively serious, step — courts do not remove executors lightly, and it would ordinarily follow, not replace, Tiers 1–4, and would benefit from the beneficiary's own solicitor.

### 4.5 Getting the will and Grant independently (a beneficiary is not dependent on the solicitor for this)

Once a Grant of Probate has actually issued, the will and the grant become public documents in England & Wales. Any member of the public — not just a beneficiary — can search HMCTS probate records via the GOV.UK "Search probate records" service and order a copy of the will and the grant for a small fee (multiple sources give this as approximately £1.50 per document as of the current fee schedule). **Practical upshot for this matter: the beneficiary does not need the unresponsive solicitor's cooperation just to confirm whether a Grant has issued yet, and to whom** — that alone is a useful, independent way to check whether the estate is actually moving. If a Grant has *not yet* issued, it won't appear in the search, which is itself informative.

## 5. Conclusion

On the law and the facts as given: nine to ten months of active-but-slow administration is not, by itself, outside the range of what a genuinely backlogged 2025–2026 Probate Registry (plus an ordinarily complex estate) can produce, so delay alone doesn't establish wrongdoing. But **prolonged, unexplained refusal to answer a beneficiary is a separate problem from registry delay, and it is not something the beneficiary has to simply accept.** They have a real, low-cost escalation path available to them starting today (Tier 1–2 below), regardless of whether the ultimate cause turns out to be a genuinely backlogged Registry, an overstretched firm, or something more concerning. My confidence in the general framework (statutory duty to account, the LeO complaints route and its stated timelines, the s.50 AJA 1985 removal power) is high, since the operative provisions were read directly from primary sources. My confidence in the specific timeline benchmarks (weeks-to-Grant, "executor's year") is moderate — those numbers come from consistent, independent practitioner reporting rather than an official HMCTS statistics release I read myself.

1. **First, get basic facts, not more legal escalation.** Have the affected beneficiary (or you, on their behalf if authorized) run a search of probate records on GOV.UK for Sylvia Ryder to see whether a Grant of Probate has issued yet, and to whom. This costs at most a couple of pounds and needs no one's cooperation.
2. **Send one clear, dated, written request** (email is fine) to whoever is handling the matter, asking specifically: (a) has the Grant issued, and if not, what stage is the application at; (b) a current estate account or summary of assets/liabilities; (c) an expected timeframe for the next step. Keep a copy — this becomes Exhibit A for every later step.

4. **If there is no substantive response within a reasonable period (a few weeks) of the written request**, escalate to the firm's formal complaints procedure (every regulated firm must have one and must tell you how to use it).
5. **If the firm doesn't resolve it within 8 weeks (or doesn't respond at all)**, that is the trigger to go to the Legal Ombudsman — note their limitation periods above so this isn't left too long.
6. **Reserve the s.50 AJA 1985 removal application, and any Larke v Nugus request, for genuinely serious situations** (real evidence of mismanagement, or an actual will-validity concern respectively) — both are heavier tools than what the facts here currently call for, and a beneficiary would want their own solicitor for the court-application route.
7. **Fill the factual gaps** flagged in Section 3 before deciding this is more than a communication failure — in particular, confirm whether there's any genuine cross-border element (Section 4.2), since that alone can lawfully explain months of extra time that would otherwise look like unresponsiveness.
