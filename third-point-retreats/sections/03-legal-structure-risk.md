## 3.1 The structure at a glance

The family has settled on a four-layer stack. Described from the top down, as the planned approach pending final attorney documentation:

| Layer | Entity | What it holds | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Family Trust** | The beneficial interest in the Land Trust | Ultimate ownership; estate-planning and succession vehicle for the family. Holds no operating role. |
| 2 | **Land Trust** (in Massachusetts practice, a **nominee realty trust**) | Fee title to 173 Polpis Road | Record owner of the real estate. Provides title privacy and clean transferability. Landlord under the lease. |
| 3 | **Third Point Retreats, LLC** (Wyoming) | A leasehold interest in the property; the operating business, brand, bookings, bank accounts | Leases the house from the Land Trust and runs the short-term rental business: markets, contracts with guests, collects rent, pays operating expenses, employs/engages the house manager and vendors. **This is the entity that faces the public and absorbs the litigation risk.** |
| 3a | **Sister LLC — Jeep** (name TBD) | The beach-permitted Jeep | Holds the highest-severity single asset separately, with its own commercial auto policy. Third Point Retreats, LLC sub-rents or brokers it to guests under a separate signed agreement. |

```
              FAMILY TRUST  (beneficial owner — never contracts with a guest)
                    │
                    ▼
              LAND TRUST  (record title to 173 Polpis Rd — landlord)
                    │  arm's-length written lease, market rent
                    ▼
   THIRD POINT RETREATS, LLC (WY)  ────  JEEP LLC (WY or MA — confirm)
   marketing · guest contracts ·           vehicle title · commercial auto
   rents · payroll · vendors                policy · separate guest agreement
                    │
                    ▼
                 GUESTS
```

### Why each layer exists, in plain business language

**The Family Trust** is the estate-planning layer: continuity of ownership across generations and control of the asset — not liability protection in itself. Its job in this structure is to be **as far from the guest as possible**. Every layer below it exists so that a plaintiff who slips on the pool deck sues an entity, not the family.

**The Land Trust** is the title layer. In Massachusetts the customary vehicle is a **nominee realty trust**: the trustee appears as owner in the Registry of Deeds, the schedule of beneficiaries is *not* recorded, and a trustee's certificate under **M.G.L. c. 184, § 35** is what a title examiner relies on. That gives real, practical privacy of beneficial ownership in the public land records and makes future transfers of beneficial interest simpler than deeding real estate. Two cautions: a Massachusetts nominee trust is *not* a liability shield the way an LLC is — the trustee acts only at the direction of the beneficiaries — and Massachusetts has **no** Illinois-style land-trust statute, so "land trust" in the family's notes should be read as shorthand for whatever MA-law vehicle counsel selects. **Confirm with counsel** whether the existing trust is in fact a nominee realty trust, whether a c. 184 § 35 certificate is recorded, and whether the trustee should be an entity rather than an individual family member.

**Third Point Retreats, LLC** is the liability layer and the business. Everything that touches a guest happens here: booking terms, the rental agreement, the security deposit, vendor contracts, payroll, the bank account. If a guest is injured and sues, the intended defendant is this LLC and its insurance — not the Land Trust, not the Family Trust, not any individual family member. This is the wall the family is paying to build.

**The separate Jeep LLC** isolates the single asset with the worst risk-to-revenue ratio in the program. A Jeep driven on Great Point sand by a renter who has never driven on sand is a plausible six- or seven-figure auto-liability event; the vehicle generates perhaps $12–15K a season. In its own entity with its own commercial auto policy, a catastrophic auto claim attaches to a company whose only asset is a Jeep — it does not reach the house business, the rental income, or the real estate. Brad's instinct is correct and should be preserved even at the cost of a second filing fee and registered agent.

## 3.2 Why Wyoming — and the honest limits of what Wyoming buys

Wyoming is the most-used domicile for asset-protection LLCs for four reasons that are real and one benefit that is frequently oversold.

**1. Charging-order protection as the exclusive remedy.** Under **Wyo. Stat. § 17-29-503(g)**, a charging order is the *exclusive* remedy by which a judgment creditor of a member may reach that member's transferable interest — foreclosure on the interest is unavailable and the creditor may not reach the LLC's assets. Wyoming applies this **without a single-member exception**, which many states do not. A creditor is left holding a lien on distributions the LLC may simply choose not to make.

**2. No state income tax, trivial compliance burden.** Wyoming imposes no individual or corporate income tax. Formation runs roughly **$100**; the annual report license tax is **$60 minimum** (or $0.0002 per dollar of assets *located and employed in Wyoming*, whichever is greater), due in the anniversary month of formation. With no Wyoming-situs assets, expect the $60 floor. Confirm current fees at filing.

**3. Privacy at the state level.** Wyoming's public filings require no disclosure of members or managers — neither the articles of organization nor the annual report puts the family's names in a searchable state database. Combined with a nominee realty trust on title in Massachusetts, this meaningfully raises the cost of a casual search connecting "Third Point" to the family.

**4. A mature, business-friendly LLC statute** with an established registered-agent industry. A **Wyoming registered agent with a physical Wyoming street address is mandatory** — an ongoing cost of roughly $50–200/year.

### What Wyoming does *not* buy — read this carefully

- **Charging-order protection is the wrong shield for our actual risk.** It is *inside-out* protection: it stops a creditor of the *member* (the trust) from reaching the LLC's assets. Our realistic exposure is *outside-in*: a guest injured at the house sues the operating LLC. Against that, the protection is **ordinary limited liability plus insurance** — the same protection a Massachusetts LLC would provide. Wyoming's famous statute is a genuine benefit against a *different* risk than the one that keeps us up at night. Any advisor who pitches Wyoming primarily as a defense against slip-and-fall claims is confusing the two.
- **A Massachusetts court applies Massachusetts law to Massachusetts events.** Litigation over an injury at 173 Polpis Road will be brought in Massachusetts. MA courts generally look to Wyoming law for internal-affairs questions (who owns the LLC, what the operating agreement means) but apply their own law to tort and premises-liability claims, and **veil-piercing analysis is fact- and forum-driven.** Forming in Wyoming does not import Wyoming's protective posture into a Massachusetts courtroom. **Confirm with counsel.**
- **Single-member LLCs draw more veil-piercing scrutiny.** Wyoming's own Supreme Court pierced a single-member Wyoming LLC in *GreenHunter Energy v. Western Ecosystems Technology* (Wyo. 2014) on facts including deliberate undercapitalization, disregarded-entity tax treatment, and the member's manipulation of the entity to take reward without risk. Wyoming later amended its LLC Act to tighten the standard, but the lesson stands: **the structure protects you only if you run it like a real company.** See § 3.4.
- **Privacy has hard federal edges.** The IRS knows who the owners are; banks collect beneficial-ownership data under their own KYC rules regardless of state law. And the **Corporate Transparency Act** remains a moving target: FinCEN's **interim final rule of March 26, 2025** narrowed "reporting company" to entities *formed outside the U.S.* that register to do business here, exempting domestically formed entities and U.S. persons from beneficial-ownership reporting. A final rule was still pending as of mid-2026 (submitted to OMB in June 2026) amid continuing litigation and GAO criticism. **Assume federal beneficial-ownership reporting could be reinstated for domestic LLCs and keep counsel on a compliance calendar for it.** Do not sell anyone in the family on "anonymous."
- **Nothing here is offshore-grade.** This is a well-built domestic structure that raises the cost and friction of litigation and defeats casual, opportunistic claims. It does not make the family judgment-proof and should never be described that way, internally or externally.

**Net assessment:** Wyoming is a defensible, low-cost, low-friction choice with genuine privacy and creditor-remedy advantages; no reason to revisit the decision. But roughly **80% of the real-world protection in this plan comes from (a) respecting entity separateness and (b) the insurance stack** — not from the choice of formation state.

## 3.3 Foreign qualification — operating a Wyoming LLC in Massachusetts

**The compliance follows the property, not the formation state.** A Wyoming LLC that leases and commercially operates a house in Nantucket is doing business in Massachusetts. This does not weaken the Wyoming strategy — Third Point Retreats, LLC remains a Wyoming entity governed by Wyoming's LLC Act, and the charging-order and privacy advantages survive. It simply adds a Massachusetts registration overlay.

**What that means practically (planned approach, to be executed by MA counsel):**

1. **Foreign LLC registration with the Secretary of the Commonwealth** under **M.G.L. c. 156C, § 48**, filed **within ten days after commencing business** in Massachusetts. Filing fee **$500**. The application must name a **Massachusetts resident agent** with written consent, and be accompanied by a **Wyoming certificate of good standing**.
2. **Annual report** to the Secretary of the Commonwealth thereafter (budget ~$500/year; confirm current fee).
3. **A Massachusetts resident agent**, distinct from the Wyoming one. Two agents, two annual fees — budget for both.
4. **The penalty for skipping this is the sharp edge of the whole plan.** Under **M.G.L. c. 156C, § 54**, an unregistered foreign LLC doing business here may be fined up to **$500 per year** and — the part that matters — **cannot maintain an action or obtain recovery in any Massachusetts court while the failure continues.** It may still *defend* suits; its contracts stay valid; members do not become personally liable merely from the failure. But an unregistered Third Point Retreats, LLC could not sue a guest who trashed the house, enforce a damage claim, or pursue a defaulting vendor. Registration is a precondition to enforcing our own rental agreement.
5. **State and local short-term-rental registrations must be in the operating entity's name** — see § 3.6.
6. **Massachusetts tax filings.** Massachusetts taxes MA-source income regardless of formation state. Depending on classification: a multi-member LLC generally files **Form 3** and nonresident members file **Form 1-NR/PY** (a composite return is available for qualifying electing nonresidents); a single-member LLC may be disregarded, pushing reporting up to the trust. The MA **4% surtax on income above the indexed $1M threshold** may matter in a sale year or an unusually strong season. **For the CPA to determine — confirm classification, filings and withholding before the first booking.**

## 3.4 Lease mechanics between the Land Trust and Third Point Retreats, LLC

This is the joint that fails. The entire structure depends on a court accepting that the Land Trust and the LLC are genuinely separate parties transacting at arm's length. If a plaintiff's lawyer can characterize the LLC as a shell that exists only on paper — no real lease, no real rent, one commingled bank account, no capital — the alter-ego argument becomes available, the wall comes down, and the family has paid formation fees for nothing.

**What counsel needs to paper (planned approach):**

- **A real written commercial lease**, signed by the trustee for the Land Trust and by the manager for the LLC, with a defined term, renewal mechanics, rent, allocation of maintenance and capital obligations, insurance and indemnity provisions, and default remedies. Not a one-page memo.
- **Market rent, documented.** Set rent at a commercially reasonable figure and keep the reasoning in the file — ideally a broker's opinion or written analysis benchmarked against the property's economics (peak rate $32,500/wk; annual carry ≈ $200,000). Below-market rent risks recharacterization as a disguised gift or distribution; above-market rent strips the operating entity of the capitalization it needs. Non-arm's-length related-party leasing can also raise **gift-tax and imputed-income** questions, and IRC **§ 469 self-rental** rules can recharacterize the income. **The CPA and counsel should set the number together.**
- **Rent actually paid, on schedule, from the LLC's own account.** A lease that exists on paper but whose rent is never transferred is worse than no lease — it is documentary evidence of a sham.
- **Separate bank accounts and books.** LLC revenue goes into the LLC's account. Owner draws are documented distributions, not ad-hoc transfers. No family personal expenses through the LLC; no LLC expenses on a family card without reimbursement.
- **Adequate capitalization** — enough working capital to plausibly run a business carrying $200K/year, plus the insurance stack in § 3.5. Deliberate undercapitalization was an explicit factor in *GreenHunter*.
- **An operating agreement**, even for a single-member LLC, with named management, signature authority, and distribution mechanics.
- **Correct signature blocks everywhere.** Guest agreements, vendor contracts, website terms, STR registrations and insurance policies should all name **Third Point Retreats, LLC** — not the family, not the trustee, not "Brad."
- **Timing matters for fraudulent-transfer purposes.** Massachusetts' voidable-transactions regime (M.G.L. c. 109A) scrutinizes transfers made *after* a claim arises. The family's position is unusually clean: the property has **never been rented**, so there are no existing rental-related claimants. **Do the restructuring now, before the first 2027 booking is accepted** — that timing is itself a defense, and it will never be this easy again.

## 3.5 The insurance stack

**Entity structure is not a substitute for insurance. Insurance is the first line of defense; the entities are the backstop.** In a premises-liability claim the LLC is the defendant, and what pays the claim — and, just as importantly, the *defense costs* — is the policy. The planned stack, all of it **to be confirmed for adequacy, limits, and correct named insureds with the family's broker and counsel:**

| Coverage | Sits with | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Property / hazard (dwelling, contents, loss of rents)** | Land Trust (owner of the asset) | Must be written knowing the property is **commercially short-term rented** — a standard homeowner's or seasonal-dwelling policy can be voided by undisclosed commercial use. Coastal wind/flood deductibles and any FEMA flood-zone requirement need separate attention on a Polpis Road waterfront-view property. |
| **Commercial general liability / STR-specific liability** | Third Point Retreats, LLC | **Massachusetts requires operators to carry at least $1,000,000 per occurrence**, and Nantucket requires evidence of $1M liability coverage as part of STR registration. $1M is the legal floor, not the right number for a $32,500/week house. |
| **Umbrella / excess liability** | Over both the LLC and the Land Trust | At this asset and rate tier, $5M is a conversation starter, not a ceiling. Ask the broker to price $5M / $10M and let the family choose knowingly. |
| **Land Trust named as additional insured on the LLC's liability policy; LLC indemnifies the Land Trust under the lease** | Both | **Do not skip this.** A plaintiff will name the record owner as well as the operator. The Land Trust is not automatically insulated because it is "just the landlord." |
| **Commercial auto — the Jeep** | Jeep LLC | Full commercial coverage as already decided, written for rental/loan-to-guest use, with the oversand-permit and beach-driving exposure disclosed. Personal auto policies exclude rental-for-fee use. |
| **Workers' compensation** | Third Point Retreats, LLC | Massachusetts requires workers' comp for **domestic employees working 16+ hours/week**, and the Domestic Workers' Bill of Rights adds a written agreement, a notice of rights, and timesheet obligations. A full-season house manager will likely cross the threshold. |
| **Cyber / privacy liability** | Third Point Retreats, LLC | The direct-booking site collects guest names, dates, phone, email and (via the payment path) financial data. Small but cheap to cover. |
| **Vendor certificates of insurance** | Collected by the LLC | Pool/spa tech, housekeeping, landscaper, handyman, chefs, charter operators, bike vendor — every vendor should be under contract, insured, and naming the LLC as additional insured where practical. This is the cheapest risk transfer available. |

Two structural notes for the broker conversation: **(a)** make sure there is no coverage gap between the Land Trust's property policy and the LLC's liability policy — the classic failure is a claim that each carrier says belongs to the other; **(b)** the umbrella must sit above *both* entities and the auto policy, or the Jeep's separateness creates an uncovered seam.

## 3.6 Short-term-rental regulatory compliance

### State level (Massachusetts)

- **Registration with DOR before accepting reservations.** Operators register through **MassTaxConnect** and receive a **certificate of registration per property**; they appear in the state's Public Registry of Lodging Operators. A short-term rental is a rental of 31 consecutive days or fewer.
- **Room occupancy excise: 5.7% state** (5% statutory plus a 0.7% uncodified surtax), collected from the guest and remitted by the operator.
- **Local option excise: up to 6%** — **Nantucket is at 6%**, consistent with the 11.7% combined figure already used in the marketing materials.
- **⚠ Cape Cod & Islands Water Protection Fund — 2.75%. Open item, and a real number.** Several sources place Nantucket within the CCIWPF and quote an all-in **14.45% (5.7 + 6 + 2.75)**; others quote Nantucket at 11.7%. At $32,500/week, 2.75% is **≈ $894/week — roughly $9–11K across a peak season.** Our rate table currently discloses 11.7%. **Confirm with DOR and MA counsel before publishing rate cards and booking terms**; a disclosed tax number that turns out to be short is both a margin problem and a trust problem with a first-season guest.
- **Community impact fee — up to 3%**, applicable to "professionally managed" operators (generally, **two or more units in the same municipality**). Reporting indicates Nantucket adopted a 3% CIF at a 2024 Town Meeting. With a single rental property we should fall outside it — **but confirm**, and re-confirm if the family ever adds a second island rental under the same operator.
- **$1M minimum liability insurance**, as above.

### Town of Nantucket

- **Chapter 123** of the Town Code requires a **certificate of registration from the Board of Health** to operate a short-term rental; no operation without one since January 1, 2023. Certificates run one year and are renewed annually.
- **Calendar:** renewals run **September 1 – October 31**; new registrations must be completed by **October 31**. This lands squarely inside the Aug–Jan Nantucket booking window and is a hard date on the 2027-season timeline.
- Registration requires confirming compliance with Town regulations, **evidence of $1,000,000 liability coverage**, and payment of the fee. The Town has a named STR Community Liaison in the Health Department — a useful, low-friction first call on the certificate-transfer question below.
- **STR certificate C0570541970** is on file and is currently used in the website footer. **Open item flagged in the source documents and confirmed here: the certificate must be verified, and very likely re-issued or amended, so that the registered operator is Third Point Retreats, LLC.** Both state and town registration attach to the *operator*, and the operator is about to change from the trust/prior management to the new LLC. Do not take a 2027 booking on a certificate registered to the wrong party.

### Zoning — the genuinely live risk, and the good news

Nantucket's short-term-rental legality has been contested and the picture changed materially in the last year:

- **June 6, 2025** — Land Court (Judge Michael Vhay), in the *Ward v. Grape* / ZBA litigation, held that the then-current Nantucket zoning bylaw **did not permit rentals shorter than 31 days of a primary dwelling in the Residential Old Historic district**, except for rooms within an owner-occupied dwelling. The Town announced enforcement forbearance and pursued an appeal.
- **November 4, 2025** — a Special Town Meeting passed **Article 1 by 1,045–421 (~71%)**, classifying short-term rentals as an **allowed principal use islandwide**.
- The **Attorney General approved Article 1**, expressly rejecting a challenge arguing it violated the statutory bar on re-presenting a recently defeated zoning article.

**Practical read for Third Point:** 173 Polpis Road is **not** in the Residential Old Historic district (that is the downtown historic core; Polpis Road sits in the island's outer limited-use districts), so the *Ward* holding was never directly on point here, and Article 1 plus the AG's approval put islandwide STR use on much firmer footing for 2027. **Treat this as favorable, not settled** — appellate proceedings, further challenges and future Town Meeting articles are all live on an island with an organized anti-STR constituency. **Confirm with MA counsel: (1) the exact zoning district of 173 Polpis Road and STR's permitted-use status in it; (2) the current posture of the *Ward* appeal and any successor litigation; (3) whether any STR-restricting article is on the 2027 Annual Town Meeting warrant.** Re-check before committing a full 2027 calendar.

## 3.8 Governance & decision rights

The structure only works if people behave consistently with it. A short, written governance memo — one page, adopted by the family — should establish the following. **Owner** below means the Family Trust acting through its trustee(s)/designated family principal; **Operator** means Third Point Retreats, LLC acting through its manager.

| Decision | Who decides | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Season rate card, minimum-stay rules, published policies | **Owner approves; Operator proposes** | Rates are strategy, not operations. Annual approval, with a defined band inside which the Operator may discount without returning for sign-off (recommend ±5%). |
| Accepting/declining an individual booking within the approved rate card | Operator | Including guest-fit judgment calls. |
| Discount beyond the approved band, comp nights, first-season incentives | Owner | |
| Capital spend | Operator up to a set threshold (recommend **$5,000** per item / **$15,000** annually); Owner above | Emergency repairs affecting guest safety or habitability are pre-authorized to the Operator with same-day notice to Owner. |
| The basement renovation, and any permitting decision | **Owner** | Non-delegable. Title 5 / egress / occupancy amendments all run through Owner and MA counsel. |
| Concierge partner and vendor contracts | Operator up to threshold; **Owner** for anything exclusive, multi-year, revenue-sharing, or brand-bearing ("Third Point Concierge") | |
| Jeep release to a specific guest | **Owner (Brad) retains discretion**, per the existing decision | Operator executes the paperwork, screening, and permit; Owner says yes or no. |
| Insurance placement and limits | Owner, on broker recommendation | Annual review. |
| Entity, lease, trust, and tax matters | **Owner, with counsel and CPA** | The Operator never amends the lease or the operating agreement. |
| Day-to-day: housekeeping, turnovers, maintenance, guest communication, upsell sales | Operator / house manager | Within a written scope of authority and spending limit. |
| Anything touching the family's identity, prior ownership, or press | **Owner only** | Silence is the standing policy; the house manager has no authority to speak to media. |

Two practical guardrails worth writing down: **(1)** the house manager is engaged by, paid by, and reports to the **LLC** — not to a family member personally — and holds no signing authority on the trust or on the lease; **(2)** all guest-facing contracts, deposits, and refunds move through the LLC's account only. These are governance rules that double as veil-preservation rules.
