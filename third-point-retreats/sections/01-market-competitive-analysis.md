## 1.0 The short version

Third Point enters a market that in 2026 is **flatter and more crowded** than it was in 2022–24 — not weaker in absolute terms, but no longer one where a good house sells itself at asking. Island rental inventory expanded materially; ultra-luxury listing rates softened while mid-tier neighborhoods converged upward. **[S]**

Our commercial problem is not the rate. $32,500 is defensible. The problem is **proof and distribution**: an unrented house with no reviews, no booked-rate history, and no live booking channel, entering a 2027 buying window that is opening now. My highest-conviction recommendation is in §1.4 and is not about price.

## 1.1 Industry & market overview

### The macro backdrop is mildly favorable

AirDNA's 2026 US outlook forecasts **57.4% average occupancy** (above the 57.0% pre-pandemic norm), demand and supply each growing **~2.7%**, and **RevPAR up ~2.9%** — driven by slower new-listing growth restoring pricing power to established operators. **[S — AirDNA 2026 US Outlook]**

The luxury sub-segment that matters most to us is growing fastest. 2026 villa-market reporting describes the defining booking as **"three generations under one roof, ten to sixteen guests, ten to fourteen nights, often locked in six to eight months ahead."** Cited drivers: multigenerational luxury family travel valued ~**$96B globally in 2025**, projected to more than double by 2034; the ~$68T intergenerational wealth transfer; grandparent-funded trips now **>⅓ of top-tier multigen bookings**; Virtuoso reporting **85% of luxury advisors** seeing increased multigen demand. **[S]**

**[J]** This is close to a bespoke tailwind. That profile — ten-to-sixteen guests, two-plus weeks, separated suites, three generations — is exactly the program Third Point has today and more exactly the one it will have post-basement. This should drive the rate calendar, the photography brief and the renovation spec, not just the copy. **[Caveat]** These figures come from Mediterranean/Caribbean villa operators via press release. Treat direction as reliable, magnitudes as marketing. No Nantucket-specific multigen dataset exists that I could find.

### Nantucket 2026: redistribution, not growth

This is the most important external finding here, and it partially contradicts the July memos' optimism. Raveis Nantucket's Summer 2026 Rental Market Report calls 2026 **"a year of market redistribution"** driven by a large inventory increase: **[S]**

| Direction | Neighborhoods (2026 vs prior yr, listing rates) |
|---|---|
| **Down sharply (ultra-luxury)** | **Pocomo −58% Jun / −54% Jul**; Madequecham −40%/−28%; Dionis −30% Jul / −23% Aug; Shimmo −21% Aug |
| **Flat (premium core)** | Cliff +0.2% Jun; Brant Point +2.2% Jul; Town +6.3% Jul / +4.2% Aug |
| **Up sharply (secondary)** | **Quidnet +103% Jun / +156% Jul**; West of Town +61–158%; Monomoy +184% Aug; Mid-Island +66–69%; Sconset +17–29%; Wauwinet +16–19% |

Inventory: Town June listings **299 → 756**; Surfside **46 → 189**; Madaket **90 → 206**. **[S]**

**[Caveat — read these carefully.]** These are **average listing prices, not booked rates**, and are heavily mix-shifted: when a neighborhood's listing count triples the average moves because composition changed, not because any house cut its rate. Pocomo at −58% most likely reflects a few very large houses leaving the set. Do not present these as evidence to a lender or buyer.

**[J]** Two usable implications. First, the Polpis/Pocomo/Quidnet corridor is not moving as one block — assume no neighborhood tailwind; Third Point must earn its rate alone. Second, in a year when the number of listings is exploding, the binding constraint on a booking stops being supply of houses and becomes **the guest's attention and their confidence the house is what it claims**. That is a proof-and-presentation problem — the thing a direct brand is good at and an unreviewed debut is bad at.

For scale: AirDNA reports island STRs averaging **51% occupancy, $1,392 ADR, ~$71,675 monthly revenue**. **[S — obtained via search summary; the page returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch, so vintage and sample are unverified.]** These blend in-town cottages and are not a comp for us. The only useful signal is the shape: **51% annual occupancy on an island with a ~14-week rentable season is a summer business with a long unpaid tail.** That is our problem too.

### Seasonality and the booking wave

Peak is late June–August; shoulders are May–mid-June and September–mid-October; November–April is effectively a non-market except for three or four event weekends. **[S]**

On the Aug–Jan booking premise: external sources partially soften it. Raveis's guest-facing guidance says **January–March** offers the best availability, that the most coveted homes fill by March, and that holiday weeks need **9–12 months** of lead time; villa data separately cites **6–8 months** for large multigen groups. **[S]**

**[J — reconciling]** Both are true for different guests. The **committed** end (families renewing the same week; large groups coordinating ten calendars) transacts **Sept–Jan**. The **shopping** end transacts **Jan–April**. With zero repeat base and nobody on a list, Third Point will win little of the Sept–Dec wave in cycle one. **Our realistic first harvest is January–April 2027, which moves the site deadline from "impossible" to "tight": live, indexed and collecting emails by late October 2026.** Earlier is upside; later than November is where I start to worry.

**[OPEN]** Nobody has told us what the Fisher listing produced in inquiry volume at $27.5K/$32.5K. That is free, real demand data walking out the door with the agency — **ask Fisher for the inquiry log before the relationship closes.**

## 1.2 Target guest personas

### A — "The Convener" (multigenerational reunion booker) · target 4–6 peak weeks
**Who:** 58–74, the grandparent or eldest sibling who pays and organizes. Boston, NYC metro, Fairfield County, Philadelphia; often has Nantucket history. Party of 8–12, three generations.
**Optimizing for:** *nobody being unhappy.* Two adult couples of equal status getting equal-quality rooms (the #1 unspoken friction in multigen booking); grandparents not on the teenagers' floor; a table big enough for one shared dinner; kids occupied without a car trip.
**Price behavior:** low sensitivity on rate, **high sensitivity to surprise**. Pays $32.5K without blinking; walks over a $600 pool-heat surcharge, because the surcharge signals the whole week will be nickel-and-dimed (24% of guests cite surprise fees **[S]**).
**Lead time:** 6–11 months; frequently books **two weeks**, not one.
**Decision unit:** a **committee**. They shortlist 3–5 houses and forward a link or PDF to relatives who have never been to Nantucket and are looking on phones. **[J] This is why the floor plan and one-page fact sheet are the actual sales collateral, not nice-to-haves.**
**Why us over 323 Polpis ($38K, also sleeps 10):** same guest count, $5,500 cheaper, **¼-mile deeded beach vs 1.7 miles**, real Sound-and-lighthouse horizon vs cranberry bog, two primary suites on separate floors. They win the amenity checklist; we win the week the family actually has. **Over 200 Polpis ($47.5K, sleeps 12):** $15,000/week cheaper for the same group post-renovation.
**What loses it:** no reviews, no named human. Nobody spends $65,000 on two weeks for their whole family to be a house's first test.

### B — "The Principal" (peak-week single-family buyer) · target 2–4 peak weeks
**Who:** 40–55, finance/law/PE/founder, NYC or Boston. Two-couple travel or one family plus a nanny. Party of 6–10.
**Optimizing for:** the pool (non-negotiable at this rate with kids), the beach, a kitchen that supports a private chef, WiFi good enough for two working mornings, and **speed of reply** — they are comparing five houses in one evening.
**Price behavior:** moderate and **comparative**. They know the ladder and will benchmark $32,500 against 323 Polpis at $38K and 5 Village Way at $25–35K, expecting the delta explained. Rate opacity loses them outright.
**Lead time:** 3–7 months, with a real tail booking inside 8 weeks when a first choice falls through. Decides alone, fast, often through an assistant.
**Why us:** renovation freshness and the never-rented story read to this buyer as a genuine condition and hygiene advantage ("this house hasn't had twelve groups through it this summer"). The car-free logistics story (§1.4) lands harder here than with Persona A.
**What loses it:** a slow reply. The published sub-one-hour response promise is a weapon aimed squarely at this persona. **[S/J]**

### C — "The Shoulder Pair" (June and September) · target 3–5 shoulder weeks + event weekends
**Who:** either 55–72 empty-nesters with no school constraint chasing the island's best weather, or 35–48 couples/friend-groups, food-and-wine motivated. Party of 2–6 — **they buy ten sleeps and use four**, which is why they will never pay peak.
**Optimizing for:** weather, quiet, restaurant access, and the feeling of having overpaid slightly less. **[J] The heated pool and spa are the most persuasive shoulder amenity we own** — they turn a 62°F late-September afternoon into a vacation rather than a mistake, and most shoulder inventory has no pool.
**Price behavior:** **high sensitivity.** A $2,500/week move changes their answer. **Lead time:** 4–12 weeks — short; they respond to inventory alerts, not January planning.
**[J] Strategic note:** never sell this persona at the same table as Persona A. Separate list, separate creative (sweater weather, fire pit, dinner at Òran Mór), visibly better price. Selling shoulder weeks with peak imagery is the most common way luxury rentals leave $60–80K on the table each year.

### Secondary — "The Milestone" (60th birthday, anniversary, family celebration)
Real upside: premium for 2–3 shoulder nights plus heavy experiences spend (chef, photographer, clambake). **[OPEN]** Entirely gated by an **events policy** that does not exist yet. A narrow written policy unlocks the segment; silence means we field the inquiry with no answer and lose it. (Insurance/neighbor implications → legal section.)

### Explicitly deprioritized — the winter monthly remote-work renter
**[J]** The Nantucket November–March monthly market is thin and dominated by seasonal-worker and contractor housing at rates nowhere near sensible for a $200K carry, with heavy wear and freeze risk attached. **The monthly play I would run instead is a shoulder month — Sept 15–Oct 15 or May, at roughly $35–45K** — sold to semi-retired couples or a principal with school-free kids. Different product entirely.

### Where we win and lose

**Wins, by defensibility:** (1) **the horizon** — open Sound with Great Point and Sankaty Lights at first-floor level; geographic, permanent, and outranked only by true oceanfront that starts above our band; (2) **beach *access*, not view** — ¼ mile vs 323 Polpis's 1.7 miles is felt every day by a family with small children; (3) **bike path frontage** — also geographic, also permanent, and the load-bearing element of §1.4; (4) **two primary suites, one per floor** — the structural answer to multigen friction, rare in 4–5 BR comps; (5) **condition and the debut** — genuine, but it depreciates the moment we take a booking, so monetize it in 2027 rather than saving it.

**Losses, honestly:** (1) **amenity stack** — 323 Polpis's theater/gym/sauna/steam is the only axis where a same-capacity comp is unambiguously better, and it is worth exactly the $5,500 they charge; (2) **bedroom-count optics** — whatever we say about suites, half of Persona A's committee counts doors; (3) **no booked-rate history** — every comp can say "we get this"; we can only say "we ask this"; (4) **not walkable to town** — we will lose the Cliff/Brant Point buyer.

## 1.4 Positioning & differentiation

"Never lead with bedroom count; sell suites, space and the view" is a correct rule of *copy*. It is not yet a *position*, because it describes what we say rather than why we are the only rational choice for a defined buyer. The position I would defend:

> **Third Point is the multigenerational Nantucket house you do not need a car to enjoy.**

**Layer 1 — car-free is the sharpest unowned position on the island. [J]** The greatest friction in a Nantucket vacation is the car: **$320–370 each way ($640–740 round trip)** on the only car ferry, summer vehicle reservations opened **Jan 27, 2026** and effectively sold out, and the owner independently confirms July–August passage is "almost impossible." **[S]** Every competitor from $32K to $47K quietly assumes you will solve this yourself. Third Point can actually resolve it, on geography nobody can replicate: **the Polpis bike path begins at the driveway, the deeded beach is a quarter mile on foot, and a beach-permitted Jeep is available on request** — plus arranged transfers from fast ferry or airport and a branded bike fleet for ten. That converts the island's #1 anxiety into a service story. Critically, **it is a reason to choose us that has nothing to do with bedroom count, square footage or amenity checklists — the three axes where we are structurally disadvantaged.**

**Layer 2 — sell zones, not bedrooms.** The real product is **separated generational zones**: today two primary suites on separate floors plus two suites; post-renovation a genuine three-zone house — kids' floor (bunk + media), main floor (primary one, kitchen, living, the Sound at eye level), upper floor (primary two + two suites). The villa market's own 2026 language is *"separated suites"* **[S]** — adopt it. "Sleeps ten across four suites, on three levels, with a primary suite on each floor" is stronger than any bedroom count and is literally true.

**Layer 3 — the horizon is the name.** Keep *named for the point it faces* as the emotional close, not the opening argument. **[J]** A view is what a guest remembers; it is rarely what makes them click "inquire" over a competing tab. The view sells the second visit and the referral. Lead with the problem we solve; close with the horizon.

**Refuse to compete on:** the amenity arms race (323 Polpis and the $45K tier can always add a room; every dollar chasing them is a dollar not spent on the media room, concierge bench and photography that convert our personas); square footage and bedroom count (we lose both — get them out of headline positions); and price (we are not the cheap option and must never become it — the 146 Polpis gap is our *evidence*, not our *strategy*).

## 1.5 Demand calendar & the shoulder problem

### The arithmetic that defines the business

Carry is **~$200,000/year** **[S]**. At $32,500 that is **6.2 peak weeks to break even on carry alone**, before turnover, housekeeping, linens, utilities, insurance, marketing and management — out of roughly **9 true peak weeks** available. **The business has almost no margin for error in July and August, and its entire profit lives in the shoulders and the upsells.**

**[OPEN — the most important unanswered financial question in the plan.]** Does the $200K include variable operating cost, or is it fixed carry only (taxes, insurance, utilities, landscaping, debt service)? At ten booked weeks a professional luxury turnover program plausibly adds **$25,000–45,000/season** *(my estimate, unsourced)*. If that sits on top, break-even moves from ~6.2 to ~7.5 peak weeks and the plan's risk profile changes materially.

**Event-weekend anchor. Three such weekends is **$25,000–31,500 against weeks otherwise worth zero — roughly 13–16% of annual carry, earned in nine nights.** Highest return-on-effort item on the calendar, and currently unbuilt.

### What actually closes the shoulder gap, ranked by revenue per unit of effort **[J]**

1. **Event-weekend packages with 3-night minimums and premium nightly rates** (Stroll, Daffodil, Thanksgiving) — ~$25–31K/yr. Needs a rate card, a page section and a minimum-stay policy. Nothing physical.
2. **A separate shoulder email list and a September campaign.** Persona C books on 4–12 weeks and responds to inventory alerts. The 300-address target by Jan 2027 **[S]** should be explicitly **split** into peak and shoulder lists with different creative.
3. **A shoulder-month rate (~$35–45K)** for Sept 15–Oct 15 or May — four near-worthless weeks become one meaningful booking with a single turnover.
4. **The Jeep (~$12–15K/season **[S]**) and the experiences bench.** Note these scale with *booked weeks* — they amplify a good season, they do not rescue a bad one.
5. **Two-week incentives in peak.** The multigen segment already books 10–14 nights **[S]**; an included benefit for a consecutive second week is cheaper than a rate cut, reduces turnover cost, and reduces turnover *risk* — where debut properties lose their reviews.
