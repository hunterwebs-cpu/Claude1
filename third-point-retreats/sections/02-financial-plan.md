**Which to plan on.** Base. **Occupancy taxes are not revenue.** 5.7% state + 6% local = 11.7% on stays under 32 days **[S]**. Guest-paid, disclosed, collected in trust and remitted by TPR. Never book it to the P&L. **[NC]** Confirm with the CPA whether the 3% MA "community impact fee" for *professionally managed* units applies — the statutory trigger is two or more units in the same municipality, so a single-property operator should be exempt, but the registration should say so explicitly.

**Recommended monetization structure [M]:** Jeep LLC contracts *directly with the guest* under its own rental agreement and its own commercial policy. TPR acts as booking agent and charges Jeep LLC a **20% facilitation fee** for originating the rental, handling the guest agreement, and managing the oversand permit — **$2,750 to TPR** in the base case. This keeps vehicle liability entirely inside the Jeep LLC while giving TPR an arm's-length, defensible fee.

Alternatives considered and rejected:

- **Lease-back / re-rental** (TPR leases the Jeep seasonally from Jeep LLC and re-rents at retail). Captures more spread inside TPR but pulls vehicle liability into the entity that also holds guest occupancy risk. The whole point of the separate LLC is defeated. **Do not do this.**
- **Bundle the Jeep free into peak weeks** as rate defense. Worth roughly $1,000/wk of held rate on 9 peak weeks ≈ $9,000 — economically a wash against $13,750 gross, and it eliminates the scarcity framing the docs specifically recommend **[S]**. Keep it paid and on request.

**[NC]** Two inputs would firm this up materially: (1) the actual quoted premium for a commercial rental-use policy on a beach-permitted 4×4 in MA — this is the single line most likely to surprise; (2) whether the Jeep's title has actually moved from the trust into the Jeep LLC, since the July notes describe the decision but not the execution.

### 2.3.2 Recommended who-pays-what structure

**This is a structuring assumption and requires sign-off from whoever is advising on the trust/LLC relationship. Section 3 covers the legal mechanics; I am modeling a sensible default so the numbers have somewhere to sit.**

| Cost category | Borne by | Rationale |
|---|---|---|

| House manager, guest communications, on-call maintenance retainer | **TPR LLC** | Service delivery |
| PMS, back-office, bookkeeping, entity compliance | **TPR LLC** | |
| **Rent** | **TPR → Land Trust** | See below |

**Recommended rent formula [M]:** `Annual rent = greater of $200,000 or 50% of TPR gross rental revenue.`

Why 50%: a Nantucket rental agency takes 15–20% for demand generation alone **[S]**, leaving the owner 80–85% but also leaving the owner every operating cost and all occupancy risk. A master tenant that absorbs marketing spend, operating costs, and full vacancy risk should retain materially more than an agent. 35–50% to the operator is the defensible band; 50% to the landlord is the conservative, IRS-friendly end of it.

**[NC] — three questions for the CPA:**
1. Does the **$200,000 floor** survive an arm's-length test? A floor that exceeds a percentage-of-revenue market rent in a weak year is not something an unrelated tenant would sign. The alternative is a pure percentage rent with no floor, which pushes the shortfall risk onto the Land Trust.
2. Is TPR's activity a **trade or business** (active, self-employment/NIIT implications) or passive rental? Substantial services — concierge, daily-equivalent guest service, transport — push toward the former.
3. A **Wyoming LLC operating real property in Massachusetts** must almost certainly register as a foreign entity in MA and file MA returns. Confirm this is done before the first booking, not after.

### 2.4.2 Basement build-out — the capital question

**Unbudgeted in every source document.** The owner's revenue expectation is **[S]**: bunk room sleeping 4 + full bath + media/teen room → 5 BR / 5.5 BA / sleeps 14 → peak rate $37,500–40,000 → **+$50–75K/season at ~10 booked peak weeks**.

**Sourcing on Nantucket construction cost:**

- Fisher Real Estate's own construction series put stick-built Nantucket homes at **$575–$700+/sq ft** in 2017, tiered by price bracket, **excluding** hardscape, landscape, furnishings, design and permitting.
- More recent island market commentary (2025) puts Nantucket new construction at roughly **$1,200/sq ft**, with modular at **$500–600/sq ft**.
- The premium over mainland is structural, not cyclical: a limited on-island trade base, subcontractors commuting or housed at the client's expense, barge freight on every material, and weather-driven schedule risk.
- Mainland Massachusetts basement finishing runs **$45–95/sq ft** typical and **$100–150+/sq ft** at high-end spec; a basement full bath adds **$8–35K** depending on plumbing distance and whether the slab must be broken for an ejector pump.

**My method [M]:** a basement finish-out is not new construction — the foundation, envelope, and roof already exist — so it prices at roughly 30–45% of a comparable new-build per-square-foot number. Applying a **1.8–2.2× island multiplier** to a mainland high-end basement finish ($150–200/sq ft at this spec level) gives **$270–440/sq ft**, which cross-checks against 30–40% of the $700–1,200/sq ft Nantucket new-build range. I model the general finish-out at **$225–375/sq ft**, base **$290/sq ft**, and line-item the specialty scopes separately.

**This is the most important finding in the section and it contradicts the tone of the existing owner's report.** The owner's report calls the basement build "the single highest-return capital move on the property" and frames it against "a one-time build-out cost a fraction of that size" **[S]**. On rental cash flow alone, at realistic Nantucket construction pricing, that is not true — payback runs a decade, not a season or two.

Three things reconcile it:

1. **The disciplined scope captures most of the rate lift for 56% of the cost.** The move from sleeps-10 to sleeps-14 is what unlocks the $38K–$47.5K comp tier **[S]** (`owner-content-notes.md`; 200 Polpis at $47,500 sleeps 12). The media room closes an *amenity* gap against 323 Polpis; the bunk room closes a *capacity* gap, and capacity is what the rate ladder actually prices on **[S]** (`comps-and-guest-intel.md` §1). **Recommendation: build the bunk room and bath first; phase the media room into winter 2027–28, funded from season-one cash flow.**
2. **The real return is asset value, not rent.** A permitted 5 BR / sleeps-14 Polpis waterfront-view house is a different product in a sale than a 4 BR / sleeps-10. That gain accrues to the **Land Trust**, not to TPR — which raises a structuring question below.
3. **Who funds it matters.** If TPR pays for a permanent improvement to property the Land Trust owns, that is a leasehold improvement with gift, basis, and deductibility consequences. **[NC] Recommendation: the Land Trust funds the capital project directly.** Confirm with the CPA.

**[NC] — the binary risk that must be resolved before any money is spent.** Nantucket ties legal bedroom count to septic capacity **[S]**, and current Board of Health rules disallow "bonus bedrooms" in the Watershed Protection Districts **even with an Innovative/Alternative system**. Polpis sits in or adjacent to the Nantucket Harbor watershed. **If 173 Polpis is inside a WPD, the bunk room may not be permittable as a bedroom at any price** — which kills the sleeps-14 story and most of the rate lift, leaving only a media room. A septic engineer's feasibility letter costs a few thousand dollars and is the highest-return dollar in this entire plan. **Spend it before framing, before design fees, before the photographer is booked.**

## 2.5 Break-even analysis

**The owner's framing [S]:** at $32,500 peak, roughly 6–7 booked peak weeks covers the $200,000 carry.

**Check [M]:** `200,000 ÷ 32,500 = 6.15 weeks`. Arithmetically correct — but it is *gross* revenue against carry, and it ignores both the cost of servicing those weeks and the entire operating cost base of the rental business.

Three break-even definitions, in ascending honesty:

| Definition | Fixed costs to cover | Contribution per peak week | Break-even |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| **A. Gross rent vs. carry** (owner's framing) | $200,000 | $32,500 | **6.2 peak weeks** |
| **B. Contribution vs. carry** | $200,000 | $30,140 ¹ | **6.6 peak weeks** |
| **C. Full business break-even** | $335,700 ² | $30,140 | **11.1 peak weeks — exceeds the 9 available** |
| **C′. Full break-even, blended mix** | $335,700 | $27,640 ³ | **12.1 booked weeks (71% of core inventory)** |

¹ `32,500 − 2,960 variable + 600 concierge = $30,140`
² `200,000 carry + 135,700 TPR fixed ops = $335,700`
³ Base-case blended rate `$30,000 − 2,960 + 600 = $27,640`

**The reframe the family needs to hear:** *the owner's instinct is right about the house and wrong about the business.* Six to seven peak weeks covers the cost of **owning** Third Point. Covering the cost of **operating it as a rental business** takes roughly **twelve booked weeks out of seventeen** — 71% occupancy of the marketed calendar, in a debut season, with no reviews. The base case (14 weeks) clears it by about two weeks of cushion. The conservative case (9 weeks) misses it by three.

**Post-renovation break-even [M]**, at $38,750 peak (midpoint of the owner's $37,500–40,000 band), variable ops $3,260/wk (larger house, more guests), fixed ops $145,000 (higher insurance and staffing for 14 guests):

| Definition | Break-even |
|---|---|
| Contribution vs. carry | `200,000 ÷ 36,090 = ` **5.5 peak weeks** |
| Full business, blended ~$34,000 | `345,000 ÷ 31,340 = ` **11.0 booked weeks** |

The renovation buys about **one full week of break-even cushion** — meaningful, but not the transformation the rate headline suggests.

## 2.7 The value of going direct

**Sourced framing [S]:** Nantucket agency commission runs 15–20%; at $32,500/wk that is $4,875–6,500 retained per peak week (`marketability-analysis.md` §1).

**Full-season quantification [M]**, base case:

| | 15% | **17.5% (mid)** | 20% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Commission avoided on $445,000 rental revenue | $66,750 | **$77,875** | $89,000 |
| Less: marketing spend TPR now carries | (25,000) | **(25,000)** | (25,000) |
| Less: PMS, hosting, domain, ESP | (1,200) | **(1,200)** | (1,200) |
| **Recurring annual net advantage** | **$40,550** | **$51,675** | **$62,800** |
| Less: one-time launch costs (2027 only) | (21,100) | **(21,100)** | (21,100) |
| **Year 1 net advantage** | **$19,450** | **$30,575** | **$41,700** |

**Year 2 onward the one-time falls away**, and the advantage scales with revenue: roughly **$52,000 in 2028** and **$61,000 in 2029** at mid-commission on the Track 1 revenue path. **Three-year cumulative direct-booking advantage: ~$144,000 [M].**

**The candid counter-argument.** Agency commission is not pure rent extraction — it buys demand generation, an existing client book, brand trust, and inquiry handling, all of which a never-rented house with zero reviews conspicuously lacks **[S]**. The direct decision is therefore not "save $78K," it is "**bet that the site, the film, and $25K of marketing produce at least as many booked weeks as Fisher would have.**"

**Break-even on that bet [M]:** each lost week costs `$30,000 × (1 − 0.175) = $24,750` of net-of-commission revenue. The $51,675 annual advantage is therefore consumed by **2.1 lost weeks**. Going direct is correct if — and only if — the direct channel comes within about two weeks of what the agency would have delivered.

**Mitigation, straight from the source docs [S]:** keep one or two curated luxury marketplace listings purely as discovery feeds into the direct site, launch before the Fisher page comes down so the SEO handoff is clean, and own "Third Point Nantucket" and the address in search. The hybrid costs a few points on a minority of weeks and materially de-risks the bet.
