## 1. Introduction

This memo addresses only the four Tier 1 terms the client identified as worth analyzing: **RUDOLPH**, **ERECTOR SET**, **LIONEL TRAINS**, and **VIEW-MASTER**. Tier 2 terms (GRINCH, HOME ALONE, FROSTY SNOWMAN, WHITE CHRISTMAS) are being cut outright and are not analyzed here.

**Scope assumption, taken as given per the client's framing:** each term appears *only* as a plain-text entry in a word-search grid and its accompanying printed word list, inside a puzzle titled "Toys of Yesteryear." No logos, no stylized brand typography, no character artwork, and no use of the term in the book's title, subtitle, cover, or Amazon metadata/keywords. This is a materially different fact pattern from using a brand name in a book title or as a keyword to trigger search visibility — the latter is where most of the reported KDP trademark friction actually occurs (see §6).

**Limitation:** This is general legal information based on published trademark doctrine and publicly available registration data, not a formal trademark clearance opinion. No USPTO TESS search was run against the live register in real time — ownership facts below come from secondary sources (trademark databases, corporate legal pages, Wikipedia) and should be treated as *current as of search date*, not as a substitute for a TESS pull if the client wants certainty on ownership status immediately before publication.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Governing doctrine

**Trademark infringement (15 U.S.C. § 1114, § 1125(a)):** requires use of a mark "in commerce" **in connection with the sale of goods or services in a manner likely to cause confusion** as to source, sponsorship, or affiliation. A word appearing inside a puzzle grid, referring to the real-world toy/brand the puzzle is *about*, does not represent that the puzzle book is produced, sponsored, or endorsed by the mark owner.

**Nominative fair use — *New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, Inc.*, 971 F.2d 302 (9th Cir. 1992)** (case read via secondary sources reporting the holding — not independently pulled from a primary reporter in this pass; treat the three-part test as verified against multiple consistent secondary sources but not against the slip opinion itself). The court articulated a three-part test permitting use of another's mark to refer to the actual trademarked product/entity when:
1. The product or subject is not readily identifiable without using the mark;
2. Only so much of the mark is used as is reasonably necessary to identify it; and
3. The user does nothing to suggest sponsorship or endorsement by the mark holder.

This doctrine exists precisely for referential uses like an encyclopedia entry, a trivia question, or — squarely on point here — a word-search puzzle whose entire premise is testing recognition of real historical brand names.

**Dilution (15 U.S.C. § 1125(c)):** protects only *famous* marks against blurring/tarnishment, "regardless of confusion." But § 1125(c)(3)(A) expressly **excludes** from dilution liability "any fair use, including a nominative or descriptive fair use, ... other than as a designation of source for the person's own goods or services," including use "identifying and parodying, criticizing, or commenting upon" the mark owner's goods. A puzzle entry referencing a real toy by name, not selling itself under that name, falls within this carve-out. *Jack Daniel's Properties, Inc. v. VIP Products LLC*, 143 S. Ct. 1578 (2023) (confirmed via secondary reporting, not the slip opinion) narrowed this fair-use exclusion only where the accused use **is itself functioning as a trademark/source-identifier for the defendant's own product** (there, a dog toy shaped and named to directly parody Jack Daniel's bottle as its own product branding). That is not this fact pattern: the puzzle book's own source-identifying brand is its title/series, not "RUDOLPH" or "VIEW-MASTER."

**Copyright:** trademark analysis, not copyright, is the operative framework for all four terms as used. A bare word or short phrase (a brand name, a character's name) is not copyrightable subject matter under 17 U.S.C. § 102(b) and 37 C.F.R. § 202.1(a) (names, titles, and short phrases are excluded from copyright protection). Copyright is only in play for RUDOLPH to the extent the book might reproduce the *story*, *character depiction/artwork*, or *song lyrics* — none of which is present here; only the word "RUDOLPH" is used.

### 4.2 RUDOLPH

**Rights holder:** The Rudolph Company, L.P. holds the registered trademark RUDOLPH THE RED-NOSED REINDEER (USPTO Reg. No. 1,309,723), with licensing managed via Character Arts, LLC in connection with DreamWorks Classics. The underlying 1939 Robert L. May story and the 1964 Rankin/Bass special have a documented (and disputed) copyright history, including a well-known defect (a mis-transcribed copyright-notice date, "MCLXIV" instead of "MCMLXIV") that some commentators argue pushed portions of the original work into the public domain — this is unsettled and not something this memo resolves, and it is irrelevant here since no story content or character artwork is used.

**Analysis:** The registered mark is "RUDOLPH THE RED-NOSED REINDEER," a multi-word composite. The single word "RUDOLPH" alone, used generically as a common personal/reindeer name inside a Christmas-themed puzzle, sits closer to a common noun than to reproduction of the composite mark. Even treating it as invoking the character, nominative fair use squarely applies: readers can't test recognition of "the famous red-nosed reindeer" concept without the name, only the bare name is used (no "the Red-Nosed Reindeer," no image, no song lyric), and nothing suggests Rudolph Co./DreamWorks sponsorship of a generic puzzle book.

**Risk differentiator:** Coined/character name, but also a common given name independent of the character — this cuts risk further than a pure invented brand word would.

### 4.3 ERECTOR SET

**Rights holder:** Historically A.C. Gilbert Co.; the Erector brand passed to Meccano in 1990, and Meccano (and the Erector name) was acquired by Spin Master in 2013. Current marketing uses "Erector by Meccano."

**Analysis:** "Erector set" functions in ordinary American usage as much as a **generic product category** (a construction toy with metal beams and bolts) as a brand name — it appears in dictionaries as a genericized term, similar to how "thermos" or "escalator" drifted toward generic use, though it has not been formally cancelled as a mark. This is the single strongest fact-pattern of the four for the defendant/publisher: even outside nominative fair use, a term that functions descriptively/generically in common speech has a materially weaker claim to exclusivity, and the trademark owner would need to show the term still functions as a source-identifier for their specific product line rather than as the public's name for the product category.

**Risk differentiator:** Lowest risk of the four, precisely because of genericness, independent of nominative fair use analysis.

### 4.4 LIONEL TRAINS

**Rights holder:** Lionel, LLC holds registered marks in "LIONEL" and related indicia (Reg. covers toy trains/model railroads); Lionel, LLC merged with Round 2, LLC in 2026 to form Lionel Brands Group — current registrant of record may reflect this restructuring and should be re-confirmed on TESS if certainty is needed. Note: "LIONEL TRAINS" is the popular/colloquial phrase; the core registered mark is "LIONEL" standing alone, with "trains" functioning as a generic/descriptive qualifier of the product category.

**Analysis:** Because "LIONEL" is an arbitrary/fanciful mark as applied to toy trains (a personal name with no inherent connection to model railroading), it is a strong mark — but that cuts toward *strength of the mark* (relevant to confusion/dilution factors), not toward liability, since nominative fair use doesn't turn on mark strength. Using "LIONEL TRAINS" to reference the actual, real-world toy train brand in a nostalgia puzzle is the core New Kids on the Block scenario: no substitute term would communicate "the historic model-train toy brand," only the mark itself is used, and nothing implies Lionel's sponsorship of the puzzle book.

**Risk differentiator:** A strong/arbitrary brand mark, meaning if this *were* found infringing the mark's fame could support a dilution theory — but the nominative and dilution fair-use exclusions above apply squarely and should foreclose that outcome on these facts.

### 4.5 VIEW-MASTER

**Rights holder:** Mattel, Inc. (via Fisher-Price brand family); registered marks include "VIEW-MASTER" and "VM VIEW-MASTER" (USPTO records, e.g., Ser. No. 86700263).

**Analysis:** Same nominative fair use analysis applies. VIEW-MASTER is a coined/fanciful brand name (a hyphenated portmanteau) with no generic meaning outside the product — this makes it, like LIONEL, a strong mark, but again strength bears on confusion/dilution factors that are independently defeated by the fair-use exclusion for referential, non-source-identifying use.

**Risk differentiator:** No generic alternative exists to describe "the toy stereoscope slide viewer" as recognizably in a nostalgia trivia context — which if anything *reinforces* prong one of the nominative fair use test (the product is not readily identifiable without using the actual mark), strengthening the defense rather than weakening it.

### 4.6 Authority cutting the other way

The most honest caveat: trademark owners with aggressive brand-protection practices (Mattel and, historically, toy/entertainment IP holders generally) sometimes send cease-and-desist letters or file Amazon rights-holder complaints **regardless of the legal merits**, because the cost of a C&D letter is trivial and small self-published authors rarely litigate the point. This is a *practical/economic* risk, not a *legal* one — the legal analysis above holds, but it does not immunize the client from receiving a complaint that must be responded to or that could still trigger a KDP listing suspension pending review, even if ultimately meritless. No specific reported instance of any of these four rights holders (Rudolph Co., Spin Master, Lionel Brands Group, Mattel) enforcing against a word-search/trivia puzzle book was found in this research; enforcement patterns for this fact pattern are, as best as can be determined, not publicly documented at all — most likely because they rarely occur.

## 6. Conclusion

On the facts as scoped by the client, all four Tier 1 terms clear the nominative fair use bar, and none should be treated as requiring substitution on legal grounds. If the client's risk tolerance is "avoid legal exposure," all four are fine to keep. If the client's risk tolerance is "avoid ever receiving a KDP rights-holder complaint email regardless of its merit," that is a business/operational risk-tolerance question this memo cannot resolve for them — it is not reducible further by legal analysis, since the analysis here already shows minimal legal exposure.
