# The Justice Department Called It Consensual and Charged Her Anyway. I Live in That Unit.

*Fourteen years inside. What the law calls impossible happens daily.*

By Bilal Khan
*Surviving the Feds: Dispatches from the Inside*

---

She walked into my pickleball game.

Not to break it up. To play. A housing unit officer stepped onto the court in the gym at FCI Fort Dix, took a paddle, and hit the ball back and forth with us while she pulled on her vape.

---

That was not the strangest thing I saw her do. I watched her play-wrestling with inmates. I watched her braid a man's hair in the officer's station.

Some officers are cool. That was outrageous.

For months the compound talked about her and one particular man — that they were having sex, that she was bringing him contraband. The rumor was that she was related to somebody on executive staff and could do whatever she wanted. I want to be precise about that: the relationship talk was compound rumor. The pickleball, the hair, the wrestling — that I saw myself.

And what did any of us do about it? Absolutely nothing. It is none of my business what she and a grown man decide to do with each other. That is the code we live by in here.

Then he came off the compound. Months later she was moved outside the perimeter, then brought back inside, and we figured nobody had snitched and the investigation had cleared her. Then she went out again. A few weeks after that, Jordan Pensak was charged.

---

The Justice Department's own press release says she "had a **consensual** sexual relationship with an inmate at Fort Dix."

Consensual. In the same document announcing a felony carrying up to fifteen years.

When that got read on the compound, the reaction was *duh*. We all knew that. She did not run around forcing anybody. She was a young, good-looking woman doing twelve-hour shifts in a building with three hundred forty men and one officer. What did you think was going to happen?

She is charged, not convicted. She has been charged by complaint — no indictment has issued, she waived a preliminary hearing, and she is out on a fifty-thousand-dollar unsecured bond. Everything alleged against her is still an allegation.

But the charge does not care about the word in the press release. Under 18 U.S.C. § 2243, consent is not an element of the offense and it is not a defense. Force is not required. Injury is not required. The entire crime is that one person had custodial authority over the other.

That statute was a **one-year misdemeanor** until 2006. In January of that year Congress raised it to five years. Six months later it raised it again to fifteen, riding in on a child protection act. A fifteen-fold increase in nineteen months.

And here is a thing nobody has written: until March 2022, § 2243 gave a correctional officer an affirmative defense if they could prove they were **married** to the prisoner. Congress repealed that four years ago. For most of the life of this statute, you could beat it at the altar.

---

Now the part outside coverage never touches.

Sex happens in here. A lot. Whatever anyone wants to say about homosexuality, men in prison find privacy, and there is very little of it to find. When I first got to Fort Dix in 2016, two guys were using the elevator in religious services and got discovered when the chaplain opened the door. Watching them run out — one of them pulling his pants up like a caught philanderer — was the funniest thing I saw that year.

That is a **code 205** under 28 C.F.R. § 541.3, "engaging in sexual acts." Not a nothing charge. **High severity** — the same tier as fighting, assault, and extortion. Up to twenty-seven days of good conduct time, twenty-seven days of First Step Act credits, six months in the SHU, and your visits, phone, and commissary.

Force and non-consent already have their own codes — 114 and 229. Which means 205 is the code for sex nobody says was coerced. Two consenting adults can lose a month of their liberty for it. Kissing and embracing is a 409, the bottom tier. So between a kiss and sex, the Bureau of Prisons has placed the distance between the lowest rung and the second-highest.

Officers stumble onto it every so often and do not always write it up. I think that is shock. They spend their shifts listening to men in here trash homosexuality at full volume, and then they open a door and find two of those same men behind it. Some of them just close the door.

---

So here is the argument, and I am not going to make it comfortable.

The rule that voids a prisoner's consent does not stop the coercion. It punishes the consent, and it fails in both directions.

In 2018 I convinced a young man who had been sexually assaulted **at another prison** to report it. He asked the psychologist first whether reporting would get him moved. She told him he would stay put. Twenty minutes after he made the report he was told to pack his property and haul it a quarter mile to another building — because somebody decided people at risk of victimization should be housed away from people at risk of victimizing.

That is 28 C.F.R. § 115.42 working exactly as written — housing assigned "with the goal of keeping separate those inmates at high risk of being sexually victimized from those at high risk of being sexually abusive."

Here is what that means in practice. He went to a unit designated for men at high risk of victimization. That is not a sex offender unit, but it fills up with sex offenders, because they are the ones who get flagged as vulnerable — most of them will not fight to defend themselves. The only men the designation keeps out are the ones flagged as risks to perpetrate.

So a twenty-five-year-old who had just reported a sexual assault was housed among a great many men with a direct interest in someone who looked like him, and the Bureau called that protection. Nobody in there would have dared touch him. He still felt like he got thrown to the wolves. That is why men do not report.

And it runs the other way too. I have watched a relationship between two men end badly, and watched the wronged party have sex with the other one deliberately and then run out of the unit screaming rape. We never saw that man again. When consent is legally impossible, an accusation is a weapon anybody can pick up.

Neither of those is a story about bad people. Both are stories about a rule that treats every prisoner as incapable of saying yes, and then acts surprised when the paperwork gets used as a knife.

---

Watch the asymmetry in this case.

He came off the compound months before anyone knew why. She went on paid administrative leave and came back inside the perimeter for a while. Federal data on substantiated staff sexual misconduct in prisons bears that pattern out: sixteen percent of the men the law calls victims end up in administrative segregation, and eleven percent get transferred.

If he wanted to sue over any of it, he could not. In 2024 the Third Circuit held in *Fisher v. Hollingsworth* — a case about a man raped three times **at Fort Dix** — that there is no damages remedy for a federal failure to protect. That same opinion recites his sworn statement that a Fort Dix captain warned him against causing problems and explained "diesel therapy": shipping an inmate prison to prison until he stops complaining.

---

And she will not do fifteen years. Almost nobody does. Under the sentencing guideline for this offense, the average sentence imposed between 2018 and 2022 was thirty-five months and the median was **fifteen months**.

The time is the small part. Because § 2243 sits in Chapter 109A, a conviction makes her a registered sex offender — and the one exception for consensual adult conduct expressly does not apply when the adult was in the offender's custody. That clause was written for exactly this. She lives in New Jersey, where registration runs for life with permission to petition a judge after fifteen clean years.

Meanwhile his file carries a record of sexual victimization that gets pulled and re-read every time he lands at a new institution.

She may serve a year and a half. She asks a judge for her name back in 2043. He carries his mark to every prison he ever sees. And not one man on this compound believes either of them committed a crime against the other.

---

**What you can actually do about this**

**The PREA auditor is the only outside person who will ever stand in that building and ask.** Audits run on a three-year cycle under 28 C.F.R. § 115.401, and the facility must post notice before the auditor arrives. I talked to one about what happened to that young man, and the institution changed how it moved people afterward. It is the only thing on this list I have personally watched work.

**Know 28 C.F.R. § 115.78(f) before reporting anything.** A good-faith report cannot be charged against him as lying — even if the investigation clears the officer. Most men in here do not know that protection exists.

**Transfer does not waive a PREA allegation.** Under § 115.63, a facility receiving an allegation about abuse somewhere else must notify that facility within seventy-two hours and that facility must investigate.

**If they put him in the SHU "for his own protection," quote § 115.43.** Involuntary segregation is a last resort, only after staff document that no other separation is possible. It "shall not ordinarily exceed a period of 30 days," and it must be reviewed every thirty days. The Bureau documents it on a **BP-A1002**, signed by the Warden. Ask for it in writing.

WARNING: THERE IS NO DEADLINE ON A PREA ALLEGATION AND NO REQUIREMENT TO TRY INFORMAL RESOLUTION FIRST. ANYONE WHO TELLS HIM HE MISSED HIS WINDOW OR HAS TO WORK IT OUT WITH HIS UNIT TEAM IS WRONG, AND 28 C.F.R. § 115.52 IS THE CITE.

And do not call the Ombudsman, because there isn't one. Congress passed the Federal Prison Oversight Act in 2024 — 392 to 2 in the House, unanimously in the Senate — and wrote into it that the law takes effect ninety days after somebody appropriates money to carry it out. Nobody has. The one remedy Congress built for families is a phone number that does not ring.

---

There is a camera in my housing unit now. It points directly at the door of the officer's station.

There were none at all when any of this was happening. Three hundred forty men, one officer, ten-hour shifts and mandatory overtime on top of them. You are going to meet people. Most officers who walk through that gate just want to make an honest living, and some of them cannot hold that line, and a camera bolted to a wall does not change the arithmetic of one person watching three hundred forty.

She should have been fired. She should never work in a prison again. I have no fondness for her and I do not miss her.

But we all watched her play pickleball for months and said nothing, because in here a grown man's business is his own. The government looked at the same thing, wrote the word *consensual* in its own press release, and made one of them a sex offender and the other one a victim.

Neither of them gets to decide which. That was settled before they met.

---
