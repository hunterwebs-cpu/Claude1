# The 2255 Deadline: How Long Do You Have to File?

Most people who could file a 2255 motion never do. Some never hear of it. Many hear of it in year two, or year three, and then find out the clock ran out while they were being transferred between facilities and waiting on a letter from their lawyer. I have watched good claims die that way.<!-- VERIFY (Bilal): this is a first-person claim I wrote from the tone of your voice guide and your account of helping hundreds of inmates. The rule sounds simple: you have one year. What nobody explains is one year from *what*. Get the start date wrong by a single day and everything after it is built on sand. So when does your year actually begin?

## What the law says

The deadline is written into the statute itself. A motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 has a one-year limitation period, and the clock runs from the **latest** of four dates:[^1]

1. **The date your judgment of conviction becomes final.**
2. **The date a government-created obstacle was removed,** if the government, by violating the Constitution or federal law, kept you from filing.
3. **The date the Supreme Court first recognized a new right,** if that right was newly recognized and made retroactively applicable to cases on collateral review.
4. **The date you could have discovered the facts supporting your claim** if you had used due diligence.

Read that first line again: "latest of." If more than one of these applies to your claim, the latest one controls. But for the great majority of people, number one is the whole story. And number one is the one people get wrong.

## When a conviction becomes "final"

"Final" does not mean the day you were sentenced. It means the day your direct appeal options are used up. There are three ordinary cases.

**You did not appeal.** Your conviction becomes final when the time to file a notice of appeal runs out. In a federal criminal case that is 14 days after the judgment is entered on the docket.[^2] Not 14 days after the sentencing hearing. After *entry of judgment*. Look at your docket.

**You appealed and lost, and did not ask the Supreme Court to hear it.** Your conviction becomes final when the time to ask the Supreme Court for review runs out. That period is 90 days from the appeals court's judgment, or from the denial of a rehearing if you asked for one.[^3] You do not have to file the petition for the clock to count those 90 days. This surprises almost everyone.

**You appealed, lost, and asked the Supreme Court to hear it.** Your conviction becomes final on the day the Supreme Court denies your petition.

Here is how it works with real dates. These are examples, not your dates.

- *No appeal.* The judgment is entered on Monday, March 3, 2025. The 14 days to appeal run out on Monday, March 17, 2025. The conviction is final that day. The one-year period generally ends on the anniversary date, March 17, 2026.[^4]
- *Appeal, no Supreme Court petition.* The court of appeals affirms on July 22, 2025. Ninety days later is Monday, October 20, 2025. The conviction is final that day. The one year generally ends October 20, 2026.[^3][^4]

THE CLOCK DOES NOT STOP ON ITS OWN. A transfer does not stop it. A lockdown does not stop it. Waiting on your lawyer does not stop it. The court cannot extend it because you asked politely.

## The other three start dates

Numbers two, three, and four are exceptions. They are narrower than they sound, and courts read them tightly.

**Government obstacle (number two).** This covers a situation where the government itself, by breaking the law, kept you from filing. A prison policy that actually blocked all access to the courts might qualify. A crowded law library usually does not.

**A new Supreme Court right (number three).** The one-year period runs from the date the Supreme Court first recognized the right, and only if the right applies retroactively.[^5] Notice the wording: the date the right was *recognized*. Not the date a lower court told you about it, and not the date you read about it. If a new Supreme Court decision helps you, note its date on the day you learn of it and start counting.

**Newly discovered facts (number four).** The clock starts when you could have discovered the facts with due diligence. "Due diligence" is doing the work. If the facts were in a file your lawyer had for two years and you never asked for it, a court may say you could have found them sooner.

## If you missed it

Missing the year does not always mean the case is dead, but the door is narrow.

- **Equitable tolling.** Courts can, in rare cases, treat the deadline as paused. You generally have to show that you were pursuing your rights diligently and that something extraordinary and outside your control stood in the way.[^6] A lawyer's ordinary negligence is usually not enough. This is a hard argument to win.
- **A later start date.** Look again at numbers three and four. A different start date can make a motion timely even if the conviction became final long ago.
- **Other routes.** Compassionate release and retroactive guideline amendments are different tools with different rules. Section 2241 is available only in narrow circumstances when a 2255 motion is truly inadequate to test the legality of your detention.[^7]

I filed my own 2255 motion. It did not succeed. What it did was force the government to respond and defend an appeal, and it taught me how much of this comes down to procedure. Many claims are thrown out because the person who filed them misunderstood the process and made a critical error. The deadline is the cleanest example.

## You get one shot

There is one more rule that ties into timing. A second 2255 motion cannot be filed without a court of appeals first certifying that it meets a strict test: either newly discovered evidence that would establish, by clear and convincing evidence, that no reasonable factfinder would have found you guilty, or a new rule of constitutional law that the Supreme Court made retroactive.[^8] That is a very high bar. So the first motion needs to contain every claim you have, filed on time.

## What to do right now

1. Pull your docket, or have a family member pull it on PACER. Find the date the judgment was entered. Find the date of any appeal decision and any Supreme Court action.
2. Write the dates in a notebook. Count forward yourself. Then count again.
3. Do not file on the last day. Mail the motion weeks early. Federal rules treat an inmate's filing as made on the day it is placed in the prison's internal mail system, if you can prove it, but you do not want to rely on that.[^9]
4. If a new Supreme Court decision might help you, write down its date today.
5. If you are close to the deadline, or past it, get a licensed attorney to look at your dates. This is one place where an hour of a lawyer's time is worth it.
6. If you are preparing to file without a lawyer, the form itself is not hard. I explain it line by line in "How to Fill Out the 2255 Form," and the whole process, including the brief, is in *The 2255 Motion Handbook*.
