# How to Look Up a Federal Case or Arrest

When federal agents arrest someone, the United States Marshals Service takes custody of that person. Outside the biggest cities, the Marshals almost never keep anyone in a federal building. They place the person in a county jail that has a contract with them. The agents who made the arrest are not obligated to call your family. Many won't. So the first thing that happens to most families is silence. No name of a facility, no case number, no lawyer, and a phone that does not ring.

That silence is a system, not an accident. The good news is that federal custody and federal court both leave a paper trail, and most of it is open to you. So where do you start?

## Step one: find the person

Work these in order. Do all of them. Each one closes a gap the others leave open.

**Call the U.S. Marshals Service in the district where the arrest happened.** Every federal judicial district has a Marshals office. Ask one question: is this person in your custody, and where are they being held? They may refuse to tell a family member anything. Some will confirm a location, some will send you to the court. Call anyway, and write down who you spoke to, the date, and what they said.

**Search the county jails around the arrest.** Because the Marshals house most pretrial defendants in contract county jails, the person is very likely sitting in one. Look up the jail rosters for the county where the arrest happened and the neighboring counties. Most publish an online inmate search. Search under every version of the name, including a middle name and any nickname.

**Use the Federal Bureau of Prisons inmate locator.** The BOP runs a free search at bop.gov/inmateloc. It covers people in BOP custody from 1982 to the present and you can search by name or by register number.[^1] Understand what it will and will not show you. People held in federal detention centers, which the BOP runs, can show up. People held in a county jail under a Marshals contract will not. And if the result says "Released" or "Not in BOP Custody," read the fine print: the BOP itself says the person "may still be in the custody of some other correctional/criminal justice system/law enforcement entity, or on parole or supervised release."[^1] That is not the same thing as free.

**Find out who the lawyer of record is.** At the first court appearance, the judge asks whether the person can afford a lawyer. If not, the court appoints one. Whoever ends up representing the person, the docket lists them (more on that below). Learn the name. Do not treat the name as a reason to relax. A lawyer's name on a case tells you who is being paid or appointed. It does not tell you whether they are doing the work, and the docket is one of the few ways a family can check.

EVERY FEDERAL DEFENDANT MUST BE TAKEN BEFORE A JUDGE WITHOUT UNNECESSARY DELAY AFTER ARREST. If days go by and you still cannot find out where your person is or whether a judge has seen them, write down every date and every name you were given. That timeline matters later.[^4]

## Step two: find the case

The person is in custody, so there is a case. It is a public record in most instances, and the place to see it is called PACER.

PACER stands for Public Access to Court Electronic Records. It is the federal courts' own system. The web address is pacer.uscourts.gov. Registering is free.[^2] You can also skip the internet entirely and walk into the clerk's office of any federal courthouse, where a public terminal lets you search for free. You pay only for printing.

Do not use any website that is not a .gov site to "find court records." Companies sell PACER material for more than PACER charges. I covered registration and the cost trap in the full walkthrough, "How to Use PACER to Follow Your Federal Case," and I'm not going to repeat all of it here. The short version:

- PACER charges $0.10 per page. A single document is capped at $3.00. The cap does not apply to name-search results or to transcripts.[^2]
- If you run up $30 or less in charges in a quarter, they are waived.[^2]
- **Search inside the district where the arrest happened.** A nationwide search for a common name costs you money and buries you in strangers.

When you search, use the PACER Case Locator and choose the party search with the advanced options. Enter the last name and first name, limit it to criminal cases, and pick the district. The result gives you a case number. It looks like this: 2:26-cr-00123.

Read it left to right. The first number is the courthouse or division. The next is the year the case was opened. The letters "cr" mean criminal. The last number is the order in which the case was filed. That is all it is.

## Step three: read the docket

Open the case and you will see a list of numbered entries with dates. That list is the docket. It is the record of everything that has been filed and every hearing that has been set. You do not need to understand every entry. You need to find these:

- **Complaint, indictment, or information.** These are the charging documents. The person may have been arrested on a criminal complaint, which is a short sworn statement from an agent. A complaint is not the end of the story. The government generally has 30 days from the arrest to file an indictment or an information.[^3] That is how my own case began: I was charged by criminal complaint before any grand jury had voted on anything.
- **Notice of appearance.** This is a lawyer telling the court they represent the defendant. It answers who the lawyer is.
- **Detention order or bail order.** This tells you whether the person was released or held, and on what conditions.
- **Scheduling orders and notices of hearing.** These give you dates. Put them in a calendar.
- **Plea agreement.** If this appears, a plea is coming or has happened.

Some entries are text only, which means a document is not attached. Some documents cost money to open. Look at the price before you click.

## What you will not see

An empty result does not always mean "no case." Federal courts seal some cases and some indictments. A sealed indictment may not show up at all until the person is arrested or the seal is lifted. Grand jury proceedings are secret by rule. And a case that has just been filed can take a day or two to appear.

When you find something, do not assume the docket is the whole picture either. Sealed filings can be missing even when they were properly filed. But when a lawyer tells you "I filed that motion" and it is not on the docket, you should ask about it.

Inside, I could not use PACER at all. I wrote letters to the court asking for printed docket sheets, and that was my window into my own case. A family member on the outside has far more power than the person in the cell. Use it.

## What to do right now

1. Write down the person's full legal name, any other names they use, date of birth, the date and place of the arrest, and the arresting agency.
2. Call the U.S. Marshals Service office for that district and ask where the person is.
3. Search the county jail rosters in and around the arrest location, and search bop.gov/inmateloc.
4. Register a free PACER account at pacer.uscourts.gov and search for the case in the correct district.
5. Once you have the case number, write it down everywhere. You will need it for every call and every letter.
6. Print the docket and the charging document. If the person is in a jail, mail them copies. Many jails allow letters and printed pages. Ask the jail what it accepts.
7. Read the docket for the lawyer of record, and keep checking it against what that lawyer tells you. If a lawyer says something was filed and it is not on the docket, ask why. Do not hire or fire anyone in a panic. How to vet a federal defense lawyer, with the questions to ask, is Part Four of *Surviving Pretrial*.

Finding the case is the first small win. It will not fix anything on its own. But it turns "I don't know anything" into "here is what they say," and that is the only place you can start from. The map for everything that comes next, the detention hearing, the plea decision, the presentence report, and sentencing, is what I wrote *Surviving Pretrial* to be.
