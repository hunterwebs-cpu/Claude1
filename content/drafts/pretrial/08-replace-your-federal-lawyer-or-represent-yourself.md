# Replacing Your Federal Lawyer, or Representing Yourself

> It is … recognized that the accused has the ultimate authority to make certain fundamental decisions regarding the case, as to whether to plead guilty, waive a jury, testify in his or her own behalf, or take an appeal.
>
> Chief Justice Warren E. Burger
> *Jones v. Barnes*, 463 U.S. 745
> July 5, 1983
>

**By Bilal Khan**

<!-- SRC: book p.200, p.16 -->
Finding a lawyer will feel like the most urgent thing you have to do. Slow down. The gears of justice grind very slowly, and there is time. What are your options, and when do you change course?

## You will have a lawyer either way

<!-- SRC: book p.200, p.131-132 -->
If you are brought to court, you will be appointed a lawyer no matter what. Even if you have millions of dollars, the judge will still appoint you one. All you have to do is tell the judge you don't have a lawyer and want one. In jail it is close to impossible to find a private lawyer on your own, so the physical ability to hire one is greatly restricted. Asking for appointed counsel does not waive your right to hire a private lawyer later. You can move from public to private defense at almost any point in the case.

## Three kinds of lawyers

<!-- SRC: book p.201-203 -->
**A public defender.** You will hear they are "public pretenders." The book's answer is that the problem is caseload and funding, not loyalty. It would be career suicide for a lawyer to secretly work for the other side, and the case could be dismissed if one did. In the federal system public defenders are paid the same as prosecutors. They usually have larger teams than a solo private lawyer, including paralegals and other help. Their offices lack the investigative resources prosecutors have.

<!-- SRC: book p.203-204 -->
**A CJA lawyer.** If the public defender cannot take the case, the court appoints a private lawyer under the Criminal Justice Act. Some are very good. Some are bad. The biggest complaint the book has heard over the years is how hard they are to reach.

<!-- SRC: book p.208-211 -->
**A private lawyer.** Be careful with referrals. Family and friends rarely have any experience with a federal case. Inmates may be steering you toward a lawyer to win favor with that lawyer. The one place you should never take a recommendation from is anyone who works for the prosecutorial side: prosecutors, cops, U.S. Marshals, FBI agents, jailers, judges. "If the IRS recommends your accountant, you will pay a lot in taxes." If the prosecutor's world likes your lawyer, ask yourself why. Billboard lawyers want volume and usually specialize in simple cases. Don't send money on a name alone.

## A way to test-drive

<!-- SRC: book p.205, p.207 -->
One advantage of appointed counsel is that you get a test drive without paying anything. The book's advice if you have money: elect appointed counsel, save the money, and see how it goes for a couple of months. If you feel your lawyer is not pulling a proper load or is screwing you, you can hire one, or ask the court for a new one for irreconcilable differences. Appointed counsel can be a great solution if you expect to plead guilty, because nothing is more painful than spending tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars on a defense that ends in a prison sentence.

## When to replace the lawyer you have

<!-- SRC: book p.123 -->
Give your lawyer a fair chance first. Don't micromanage. But if the boat is going to sink, get a new lawyer.

<!-- SRC: book p.241 -->
A lawyer threatening to leave unless you do what he says is a reason to tell him to get off the case. You will not be left alone. If you can't afford a new lawyer, he must file a motion to have free counsel appointed.

<!-- SRC: book p.266, p.341 -->
Hiding things from you is a reason to ask for or hire a new lawyer. So is a lawyer who makes a false or misleading statement to the judge and then refuses to correct the record.

<!-- SRC: book p.270 -->
Do not wait a long time. In my own case, I fired my lawyers about eighteen months after they told a court I would plead guilty, which was the opposite of my instructions. I might have found out much sooner had I demanded a transcript of the hearing.

<!-- SRC: book p.319 -->
To remove a lawyer you will have to show irreconcilable differences, a breakdown of communication, or a conflict of interest.

## Representing yourself

<!-- SRC: book p.119, p.186-187 -->
You have a right to represent yourself. The Supreme Court said so in *Faretta v. California*.[^1] To use it, you must clearly tell the judge that you want to proceed pro se, and the judge will hold a hearing to be sure you understand what you are doing and that you are competent. The book's warning: the judge is going to try to scare the hell out of you, and you should be scared. It is like flying a jet with no flight training. An old line: "a lawyer who represents himself has a fool for a client."

<!-- SRC: book p.188-189 -->
Ask as early as you can. A request on the eve of trial will probably be denied, because judges assume it is a delay tactic. "My lawyer hasn't spent enough time with me" or "I think my lawyer sucks" will not be enough. A judge will allow it if you show good cause, for instance if your lawyer threatens your life. And sometimes it is forced on a defendant: if you make it impossible for any lawyer to work with you after at least one replacement, the judge can say your only option is to proceed without one.

<!-- SRC: book p.313-319 -->
The risks, from the book:
1. **No right to a law library in jail.** Jails may have none, or an incomplete one. Federal detention centers run by the Bureau of Prisons have an electronic law library that is updated only every couple of months.
2. **Lack of understanding of the law and the rules.** You are bound by the rules like a lawyer.
3. **Lack of ability to advocate.** It is a thousand times harder when you are arguing for yourself.
4. **You waive any later claim that your lawyer was ineffective.** You can't complain about your own counsel.
5. **Juries don't like pro se parties.** You will likely cause delays.
6. **Standby counsel.** A judge will often appoint it. Standby counsel is not your lawyer. They are there to help get your filings made and answer procedural questions, not to strategize, and you cannot get rid of them.

<!-- SRC: book p.190 -->
If you are positive, answer the judge's questions and ask as early as possible. It is a hearing you will be alone for, and once it is granted you will be alone for the rest of the case.

## What to do right now

<!-- SRC: derived -->
1. Don't rush to hire. Tell the judge you want a lawyer appointed if you don't have one.
2. If you have money, consider starting with appointed counsel and watching their work for a couple of months.
3. Check their work. The guide on talking to your federal defense lawyer shows you how.
4. If they threaten to quit, hide things, or won't correct the record, put it in writing and ask for a new lawyer.
5. If you want to represent yourself, decide early, and understand the six risks first.
6. Hiring and interviewing a lawyer is covered in Part Four of *Surviving Pretrial*.
