# The Pretrial Services Officer in a Federal Case

> [EPIGRAPH PLACEHOLDER. A verified quotation from the federal bench, to be supplied by Bilal. Do not publish without one.]

**By Bilal Khan**

---

<!-- SRC: book p.141 -->
Sometime around your first court appearance, a probation officer is going to sit down with you and ask about your life. That person does not work for your lawyer. They work for the court. What is the interview for, and what happens to what you say?

## Who they are and what they write

<!-- SRC: book p.141 -->
It is frequent for the United States Probation Office, Pretrial Services Division, to prepare a report for the judge that discusses whether you should be released and on what conditions. Part of that report comes from an interview with you, and the interview generally happens on the same day as your initial appearance.

## What they ask

<!-- SRC: book p.141-142 -->
The officer will ask you a number of questions about your home, job, income and other background information. It is probable that the officer will also want to interview someone you propose as the person who will be responsible for you while you are out of custody.

## Why the report matters at your detention hearing

<!-- SRC: book p.141 -->
Lawyers like the report because it is usually helpful to the defendant. The book's warning is that it should not be the end of your lawyer's preparation. A short cut for a lot of lawyers is to request the report and stop there. The detention hearing decides whether you sit in jail for the whole case, and the judge will weigh what the report says against what the prosecutor argues. Your lawyer still needs evidence, witnesses and a plan.

## What the law says about what you tell them

<!-- SRC: primary source: 18 U.S.C. 3153(c) -->
Federal law gives this interview some protection. Information gathered in the course of pretrial services work "shall be used only for the purposes of a bail determination and shall otherwise be confidential," and it is not admissible on the issue of guilt in a criminal proceeding, unless the proceeding is a prosecution for a crime committed in the course of obtaining pretrial release or for failure to appear.[^1] That protection is narrower than people assume and it does not extend to everything that follows.

## If you are released, they supervise you

<!-- SRC: book p.334-335, p.142 -->
The officer also sets the practical terms of your release. On house arrest, you may go only to places a probation officer has approved in advance. Sometimes the two of you can work out a schedule for things like grocery shopping. Your lawyer can ask probation for the list of conditions usually imposed on defendants in your district.

## It follows you to sentencing

<!-- SRC: book p.413 -->
This is where defendants get hurt. If you were released and placed in a halfway house or on house arrest, the pretrial services information is placed in your presentence report. The probation officer can write about literally anything from your time under supervision. If you caused problems, refused to follow rules, or went to therapy, it is noted. The book says to expect the prosecutor to use it against you. If there is something negative in that section, discuss with your lawyer whether it is an accurate depiction of events so he can try to have it removed or add your version.

## What to do right now

<!-- SRC: derived -->
1. Ask your lawyer what the pretrial services officer is going to ask and what happens to the answers before you sit down with them.
2. Tell your lawyer the name, address and phone number of anyone you would propose as a custodian. The officer will want to talk to them.
3. Don't let your lawyer treat the pretrial services report as the whole defense at the detention hearing.
4. If you are released, keep a record of every approval and every instruction the officer gives you.
5. When your presentence report arrives, find the pretrial services section and read it for accuracy.
