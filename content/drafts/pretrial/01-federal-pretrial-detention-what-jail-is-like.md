# What Jail Is Like While You Wait on a Federal Case

> [EPIGRAPH PLACEHOLDER. A verified quotation from the federal bench, to be supplied by Bilal. Do not publish without one.]

**By Bilal Khan**

---

<!-- SRC: book p.26-27 -->
If you are ordered held while your federal case is pending, you will feel like a prisoner. That is because you are one. Depending on where you are held, people will call you a "detainee," an "inmate," or "unsentenced." In the Maricopa County jail system, for instance, the word is "UNSENTENCED." In most jails around New Mexico, it is "detainee." What is the difference between any of them? Nothing.

<!-- SRC: book p.27 -->
Under the law you are innocent until you plead guilty or a jury says otherwise. In practice, the jail treats you like everyone else inside. And you will be there a while. In the federal system the average defendant stays on pretrial for well over a year before sentencing and transfer to a prison.<!-- VERIFY: the "well over a year" figure is from the book (p.27). Re-check against current Bureau of Justice Statistics or US Courts data before publishing, or keep it attributed to Bilal's experience. -->

So what does that year actually look like?

## Who is in charge of the place

<!-- SRC: book p.26-28 (conditions), p.102 (government place) -->
Most people waiting on a federal case are not in a federal prison. They are in a county jail. A county jail is not a federal facility. The county runs it, sometimes through a private company, and the federal government pays for the beds.<!-- VERIFY: "pays for the beds" comes from how the US Marshals contract with county jails (seen in a Marshals Service summary, not read from the page). Confirm wording with Bilal. --> So the rules about phones, visits, mail and books are that jail's own rules.

Do not make the mistake of thinking it is neutral ground. The jail is a government place, and the people inside it work for the government, even if a private company runs it. The money comes from the government. A jail employee's paycheck depends on the people who pay it being happy, and that is not you.

<!-- SRC: book p.28-29 -->
Conditions are often worse than in prison. Many jails will not give you an ice cube. Many only allow video visits, or visits through glass with a phone, while most lower-security prisons allow contact visits. The jail can make you keep yourself and your living area clean, and that is about the only thing it cannot make you do. It can also use solitary confinement if you break its rules. Yelling about your rights does not change that.

## Do not talk about your case. Not to anyone.

<!-- SRC: book p.17 (Rule 2) -->
This is Rule Number 2 in *Surviving Pretrial*, and it is the one that costs people the most when they ignore it: shut the hell up. If someone asks about your case, say, "I'm fighting it and that's all I can say about my case." Only a snitch would be upset that you don't talk. That goes for other inmates, friends and family.

<!-- SRC: book p.99-100 (Donna hypothetical), p.143-144 (Bilal's own case) -->
Here is how it happens. A defendant tells his lawyer something in front of the other defendants, thinking it is private. The man sitting next to him wants a deal, hears it, and sells it to the prosecutor. Nothing the lawyer does afterward can un-ring that bell. It is not a story I made up to scare you. In my own case, an inmate I did not know got into my letters to my lawyer and then called the prosecution to say he had learned a lot about me. Ninety percent of what he said was false. The true part was harmless. The prosecutors treated him like platinum anyway.

## Your lawyer, your phone, and your mail

<!-- SRC: book p.143 -->
It is a royal pain to set up a private legal call from a jail. I was in county jail for four and a half years. After about three years, the prosecution turned over discs of recorded phone calls. More than a hundred of them were calls with my attorneys, and a federal agent had reviewed every one. We objected. The judge did not care.

<!-- SRC: book p.102-103 -->
Another common version: you are called to an office, handed a phone, and your lawyer is on the line, but the case manager is still in the room. A criminal defendant has a right to speak with his attorney privately, and that means nobody listening. The case manager standing there is wrong. Your job is still to protect yourself. Tell your lawyer right away that the call is not private, and do not discuss anything important on it.

<!-- SRC: book p.103-104 -->
The safest way to be private with your lawyer is usually the mail. Put "CONFIDENTIAL: ATTORNEY-CLIENT PRIVILEGE" on the outside of the envelope so nobody can say they opened it by accident. Letters between a lawyer and an incarcerated client should be opened in front of you and only briefly inspected for contraband. Your jail has its own rules for privileged mail. If you or your lawyer miss the exact rules, the jail can say it was justified in breaking the privilege. Learn the rules and follow them.

<!-- SRC: book p.143 -->
Reviewing your discovery is just as hard in a general population housing unit. Another inmate may be reading over your shoulder. Keep it locked up and keep it quiet.

## Jailhouse lawyers and commissary

<!-- SRC: book p.241-242 -->
Jails and prisons are places of opportunity, and everything is for sale, legal advice included. If you are taking advice from a jailhouse lawyer in exchange for commissary, you are probably not getting good advice. Some jailhouse lawyers know what they are doing. Most of them have a gift of gab and nothing more. Hint from the book: if the person cannot show you where in the law what he is saying is written, he probably does not know.

## Time in jail counts. Other kinds of pretrial release do not.

<!-- SRC: book p.142, p.340 -->
If you sit in jail, that time is credited against any sentence you later receive. If you are released on bail, you get no credit. That includes house arrest and a halfway house.[^1] That is one reason the decision between jail and release is yours to make, and a real one.

## How to send a book to someone in jail

<!-- BILAL: This section is your idea. The facts I can verify are the Bureau of Prisons rules. The county jail part needs you: see the open questions at the bottom of the file. Do not publish this section until you have filled in what you know about county jails. -->
If your person is in a federal Bureau of Prisons facility, one national rule covers books. At every BOP institution, a hardcover book may come only from the publisher, a book club, or a bookstore. At medium and high security institutions, the same goes for paperbacks. At minimum and low security institutions, a paperback may come from any source. The sender's address must be clearly marked on the package.[^2]

The warden can refuse a book only if it is a threat to the security or good order of the institution or could help someone commit a crime. The warden may not refuse a book just because it is religious, political, or unpopular. If a book is rejected, the inmate must be told in writing, and the person who sent it gets a copy of the rejection letter and 20 days to ask the Regional Director for an independent review.[^2]

If your person is in a county jail, none of that applies. The county decides. Call the jail, ask for the mail room or inmate services, and ask whether they accept books, who is allowed to send them, and how many at a time. Many jails put an inmate handbook on their website. <!-- BILAL: what did you learn about books at the jails you were held in? Which accepted Amazon, which did not? -->

## What to do right now

<!-- SRC: derived from the sections above -->
1. Find out whether your person is in a county jail or a federal facility. The rules are different.
2. Tell them Rule Number 2. Don't talk about the case with anyone inside.
3. Write to the lawyer with the envelope marked "CONFIDENTIAL: ATTORNEY-CLIENT PRIVILEGE."
4. If you are the family, be careful what you say on recorded calls. Assume all of them are listened to.
5. Decide about books only after you learn the facility's rules.

## About the Author

Bilal Khan was sentenced to 20 years in the federal system. He is a United States Marine Corps veteran and an entrepreneur in the health care sector. Inside, he taught federal law classes to fellow inmates and helped hundreds navigate the complexities of BOP administrative remedies, post-conviction issues, and other legal challenges that inmates face. He is the author of two books in the Surviving the Feds Series:

**Surviving Pretrial** covers everything from the moment of arrest through sentencing — detention hearings, bail, discovery, plea negotiations, the PSR, rights in custody, and the strategies that actually move outcomes. **The 2255 Motion Handbook** addresses post-conviction relief — what a § 2255 motion is, when it applies, how to file it, what claims it can raise, and how to avoid the procedural mistakes that end cases before they start. If the sentence has already been imposed and you are looking for a path back to court, this is the book.
