# Federal Detention Hearing and Bail

> [EPIGRAPH PLACEHOLDER. A verified quotation from the federal bench, to be supplied by Bilal. Do not publish without one.]

**By Bilal Khan**

---

<!-- SRC: book p.140 -->
I have yet to meet a defendant who isn't concerned about the detention hearing. You should be. It will decide whether you are held in jail for the whole pretrial process. So how does it work, and what can you do before it starts?

## What the hearing decides

<!-- SRC: book p.140 -->
It is a common misconception, but the federal Constitution does not guarantee you bail in a criminal case. It only guarantees that if bail is granted, it won't be excessive. When you face a federal case, the judge must follow the Bail Reform Act. The federal system relies less on money and more on conditions. That doesn't mean money is never ordered as security for your appearance, but it probably won't be an absurd amount you could never reach. The reason: a judge can simply order you detained without bail.

<!-- SRC: book p.141 -->
The hearing does not decide whether you are guilty. The judge is deciding whether you are a danger to the community or a flight risk while the case is pending.[^1]

## How it fits with the other hearings

<!-- SRC: book p.141 -->
More often than not, the detention hearing follows the preliminary hearing right away, often the same day. They are not the same thing. The preliminary hearing asks whether there is probable cause to believe you committed a crime.

<!-- SRC: primary source: 18 U.S.C. 3142(f) -->
By statute, the hearing is held immediately on your first appearance unless a continuance is requested, and you have the right to be represented by counsel, to testify, to present witnesses and to cross-examine the government's witnesses.[^1]

## Treat it like a trial

<!-- SRC: book p.331 -->
If you want bail, you need to prepare as if you are going to trial. A bail hearing is a mini-trial. Your lawyer should plan to call at least one witness and fight to prove you are not a flight risk or a danger to the community if you are monitored on bail. For some charges the law creates a presumption against release, and then you must show, by a preponderance of the evidence, that you are not a danger or a flight risk.

EVERYONE SHOULD APPROACH BAIL HEARINGS AS IF THE PRESUMPTION APPLIES AGAINST THEM.

## Your lawyer probably has very little time

<!-- SRC: book p.141, p.332 -->
Detention hearings are scheduled quickly. Often the defense lawyer is not prepared to argue one properly. A common shortcut is to ask for the Pretrial Services Report and stop there. It is on you to make sure your lawyer has what he needs. That means you need to bring your lawyer specific answers to the questions below.

## What to tell your lawyer

<!-- SRC: book p.332-337 -->
**Would you go to a halfway house?** Decide before the hearing. If you would rather not, tell your lawyer right away so the argument pushes for home. The judge will be upset if you sit through a hearing where a halfway house is granted and then say you don't want it.

**Would you wear a GPS monitor, and pay for it?** If you aren't going to a halfway house, you will probably be ordered to wear one anyway. Volunteering to wear it and pay for it goes a long way with a judge.

**Who would take responsibility for you?** A third-party custodian. The more closely related they are, the better. Give your lawyer their name, address and phone number. Our guide to third-party custodians covers who qualifies.

**What conditions would you accept?** There are required conditions and discretionary ones. If your offense involved the internet, expect limits on internet access and devices, including cell phones, and if you'll pay for probation's monitoring software the limit may be loosened. A curfew is common, especially in drug cases and other cases of offenses committed at night. You may be barred from bars and alcohol and from medication not prescribed by a doctor. Your lawyer can get the common conditions from probation.

**What are your ties to the community?** Jobs, property, friends, family in the area, anything that gives you a reason to stay. There is no such thing as too detailed.

**What is your financial condition?** Savings and income show you can support yourself without committing crimes. If you are near bankruptcy, it will make the judge nervous. Be honest with your lawyer and don't try to look perfect.

**What community and philanthropic work do you do?** It helps show you have no desire to leave and are not a danger. Talk to your lawyer first if the charge involved that kind of activity.

**Any other pending cases or warrants?** Tell your lawyer about outstanding tickets, failures to appear, civil suits and your criminal history up front. In my own case, the prosecutor argued I would never show up because I had a failure-to-appear warrant on a traffic ticket, telling the magistrate it showed I had "no respect" for a magistrate's duty. It was an outlandish argument, and the magistrate bought it and repeated it at my federal hearing. My lawyer had no idea how to handle it. If something like that is sitting out there, you may want to delay the hearing until it is cleaned up.

<!-- SRC: primary source: 18 U.S.C. 3142(g) -->
For reference, the judge is required by statute to consider the nature of the charge, the weight of the evidence, your history and characteristics (including family ties, employment and community ties), and the danger your release would pose.[^1]

## A motion and a response, in writing

<!-- SRC: book p.338-340 -->
In federal court, your lawyer should file a written Motion for Release Pending Trial with a brief. If all the judge has is the government's Motion for Detention, that is what stays on top of the judge's mind. If your lawyer doesn't want to file a formal motion and brief, be wary of the representation. The book's view is that a lawyer who needs an extra week to draft it is better than one who skips it.

If the government files a motion for detention, your lawyer should file a written response, and you are entitled to read it first. If you see anything in it that is false, mark it and explain why to your lawyer. Prosecutors routinely stretch the facts to help their detention case. Any time your lawyer can damage the prosecutor's credibility with the judge, he should.

## Jail or release: the choice is yours

<!-- SRC: book p.142, p.144 -->
If you are granted some level of bail, you get no credit toward any later sentence for that time. If you sit in jail, you do get credit. The choice will ultimately be yours. If you would rather stay in jail, tell your lawyer and he will arrange for you to waive the hearing.

## You can appeal a detention order right away

<!-- SRC: book p.144-145 -->
Many lawyers don't tell their clients this. If a magistrate judge held the hearing, you are entitled to a review by a district judge. If the district judge also denies release, you can appeal to the Court of Appeals. These appeals take work, which is why lawyers tend not to mention them. If you went through the hearing and lost, you can and should demand that your lawyer appeal immediately.[^2]

## What to do right now

<!-- SRC: derived -->
1. Write down your answers to the questions above and get them to your lawyer before the hearing.
2. Tell your lawyer your decision on a halfway house.
3. Line up a custodian, and a witness.
4. Insist on a written motion and a written response.
5. If you lose, ask for an immediate appeal.
