# House Arrest and GPS Monitors Before a Federal Trial

> [EPIGRAPH PLACEHOLDER. A verified quotation from the federal bench, to be supplied by Bilal. Do not publish without one.]

**By Bilal Khan**

---

<!-- SRC: book p.142 -->
If a federal judge lets you out before trial, the release usually comes with strings. The heaviest ones are a halfway house and house arrest. Which would you rather have?

## What house arrest means

<!-- SRC: book p.142 -->
If you are placed on house arrest, you will be given a GPS monitor that cannot be removed. You are expected to go only to places a probation officer approved before you go. Sometimes you and the officer can work out a schedule for ordinary things like grocery shopping. For the most part, you will be sitting at home.

<!-- SRC: book p.142-143 -->
Compared with jail, you have your own bed, your own kitchen, your own television and your own light switch. You can't leave often, and you will face other restrictions, but you aren't locked in with people who may be trying to curry favor with prosecutors by collecting information about you.

## The biggest benefit: talking to your lawyer

<!-- SRC: book p.143 -->
The biggest benefit is communicating with your attorney without prying eyes. At home you can help your lawyer prepare your case in comfort. In jail, private legal calls are hard to set up, and reviewing your discovery or writing a letter to your lawyer can be dangerous when another inmate is trying to read it. In my own case, more than a hundred of my calls with attorneys were recorded by the jail and reviewed by a federal agent, and another inmate got into my letters to my lawyer and sold what he learned to the prosecution.

## No credit for time served

<!-- SRC: book p.142, p.340, p.408 -->
Here is the catch. If you sit in jail, that time counts against any sentence you later receive. On house arrest it doesn't. You get no credit for time spent on house arrest, in a halfway house, or in any place other than a jail or prison while your case is pending.[^1] Plenty of defendants feel the restrictions are so heavy that they should count. They don't.

## Which conditions to expect

<!-- SRC: book p.334-335 -->
Releases come with a list of required conditions, and the judge can add discretionary ones. The book's examples: a curfew, which is especially common in drug cases and other offenses commonly committed at night; a bar on going to bars, drinking alcohol, or taking medication not prescribed by a physician; and, for internet-based offenses, limits on internet access and devices, including cell phones. If you will pay for the probation office's monitoring software, the internet restriction can sometimes be loosened. Your lawyer can get the list of common conditions by contacting probation.

<!-- SRC: primary source: 18 U.S.C. 3142(c)(1)(B) -->
The statute lets a judge order a range of conditions, including remaining in the custody of a designated person, specified restrictions on personal associations, place of abode or travel, regular reporting to pretrial services, a curfew, and any other condition reasonably necessary to assure that you appear.[^2]

## How to ask for it

<!-- SRC: book p.333 -->
If you want to go home and not to a halfway house, you need your lawyer to argue for it. The book's advice: if you are not going to a halfway house, you will probably be ordered to wear a GPS monitor anyway. If you come right out and say you will wear it and pay for it, you go a long way toward convincing a judge you are not a flight risk.

<!-- SRC: book p.334 -->
Whether you go home also turns on whether someone will take responsibility for you. The more closely related that person is to you, the better. Without one, the needle moves toward a halfway house, though it is still possible to argue for home.

## What gets written down about you

<!-- SRC: book p.413 -->
Your time on house arrest also ends up in your presentence report. The probation officer can write about anything from your time under supervision. If you caused problems, refused to follow rules, or went to therapy, it goes in the report, and the prosecutor will use it against you. If something is wrong, tell your lawyer so he can try to get it corrected.

## What to do right now

<!-- SRC: derived -->
1. Decide whether you would accept house arrest and a GPS monitor, and whether you would pay for it. Tell your lawyer.
2. Line up a person who could be your third-party custodian and give your lawyer their name, address and phone number.
3. Ask your lawyer, through probation, for the list of conditions judges usually impose in your district.
4. If you are released, follow every condition as if a judge will read the report. One will.
