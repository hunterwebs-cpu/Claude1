# How to Talk to Your Federal Defense Lawyer

> It is … recognized that the accused has the ultimate authority to make certain fundamental decisions regarding the case, as to whether to plead guilty, waive a jury, testify in his or her own behalf, or take an appeal.
>
> Chief Justice Warren E. Burger
> *Jones v. Barnes*, 463 U.S. 745
> July 5, 1983
>

**By Bilal Khan**

<!-- SRC: book p.121 -->
Lawyers are terrible communicators outside of the courtroom. Very few of them can talk to a criminal defendant, because they speak a different language. So who decides what, and how do you talk to someone who does not want to explain?

## What is yours to decide, and what is your lawyer's

<!-- SRC: book p.113-114 -->
Whether you hire a lawyer or one is appointed, you will ultimately be responsible for every error that happens in your case. You are the one who sits in jail if an error occurs. Your lawyer decides strategy: which motions to file and when, which objections to make, which witnesses to call, what questions to ask. There are five decisions your lawyer may not make for you:

1. **How you plead.** Nobody can decide your plea but you.
2. **Whether you testify** in your own defense.
3. **Whether you have a judge trial or a jury trial.**
4. **Whether you appeal.**
5. **Who your lawyer is**, but only if you can afford to choose.

<!-- SRC: book p.115 -->
Your lawyer cannot decide whether there will be a trial. If you want a trial, even if it will mean your demise, he has to help you through it. Your lawyer must communicate every plea offer the government makes, and the decision to accept or reject it is yours.

<!-- SRC: book p.120 -->
If you feel anyone is pressuring you to decide one way or the other, that is the time to tell the judge. The judge will remind everyone in the room that those decisions are yours to make without fear of any problems from your own lawyer.

<!-- SRC: book p.122-123 -->
The book's picture: you are the admiral, your lawyer is the captain. If you see the boat heading for an iceberg, tell your lawyer. If it is going to sink, get a new lawyer. But do not be a micromanager, and give your lawyer a fair chance to show whether he is a fit captain.

## Keep it quiet

<!-- SRC: book p.98-99 -->
What you tell your lawyer is protected by the attorney-client privilege. That protection has limits. If you tell others about your case, you have nullified the privilege as to what you disclosed. If the lawyer is part of the criminal enterprise, there is no privilege. If you say you intend to commit a crime in the future, the privilege does not apply. And it does not protect physical evidence of a crime.

<!-- SRC: book p.99-100 -->
The most common way defendants lose it: they tell their lawyer something in a room full of other defendants. The man next to you wants a deal, hears you, and sells it to the prosecutor.

<!-- SRC: book p.101 -->
Family is the next most common way. Most lawyers will not discuss your case with parents, spouses or friends, even if you ask them to. It is not rudeness. Anything a lawyer shares with them may no longer be privileged, and they can be called as witnesses.

<!-- SRC: book p.17 -->
This is the same lesson as Rule Number 2: do not talk to anyone about your case. Not friends, not family, and definitely not other inmates. Only speak with your lawyer in a private area where nobody else can hear.

## Calls and mail from jail

<!-- SRC: book p.102-104, p.143 -->
Legal calls from jail are a problem. I was in county jail for four and a half years, and more than a hundred of my calls with attorneys were recorded and reviewed by a federal agent. If the case manager stays in the room during a call that is supposed to be private, tell your lawyer at once that the call is not private and do not discuss anything that matters. Mail is usually the best way to be private. Write "CONFIDENTIAL: ATTORNEY-CLIENT PRIVILEGE" on the outside of the envelope, and follow the jail's rules for legal mail exactly. If you or your lawyer miss them, the jail may say it was justified in opening it.

## How to check your lawyer's work

<!-- SRC: book p.265 -->
Checking your lawyer's work is going to become the most important job you have. If he isn't doing his job, you pay the ultimate price, and the lawyer still goes home.

<!-- SRC: book p.265-267 -->
**Get copies of everything.** Emails to and from the prosecutor, filings, investigative reports. The only reason a lawyer could legitimately not share a document is a judge's order, and he should be able to show you the order. If you are billed, or an appointed lawyer logs an email, and you haven't seen that email, ask to see it. Then read every document. Check that the facts match your version of events, and look for concessions or waivers you did not approve. Facts must be proven by the government. When your lawyer concedes one, it makes the prosecutor's life easier, and it should never be a surprise to you. If you notice a problem, tell him at once and put your complaint in writing. Keep a copy, and mail it certified if you can.

<!-- SRC: book p.268-269 -->
**Get the docket sheet every month.** It is the index of everything filed in your case. Your lawyer can print it in a second, and your family can sign up for PACER and pull it. Cases start at document 1 and count up. If numbers are skipped, it is usually sealed filings, and if you haven't been told about them, question your lawyer. The book's advice: require your lawyer to give you a copy at least once a month. When he knows you are paying attention, he takes fewer liberties. If you are in jail and have no help, write to the Clerk of the Court, saying only that you are the defendant in the case and would like a copy of the docket sheet mailed to you, with your case number and the jail's mailing address.

<!-- SRC: book p.269-271 -->
**Be at every hearing.** Tell your lawyer you will never waive your right to be present. If the jail forgot to bring you, your lawyer should object and demand you be produced. If the hearing goes on anyway, ask for a transcript right away. If you hear your lawyer say something false, or waive a right, stand up and ask, "Your honor, can I please speak with my lawyer for a second?" and tell him what was wrong so he can correct the record. In my own case, lawyers told a state court I would plead guilty after I had told them there was no circumstance in which I would. I did not find out for about eighteen months. A transcript would have shown me immediately.

<!-- SRC: book p.271 -->
If you do not understand something said at a hearing, ask your lawyer to explain it, and wait until the end of the hearing so you do not split his attention. Keep the question plain: "What does ___ mean?" If the officers sweep you out before you can talk, your lawyer can meet you in the attorney booths in the courthouse.

<!-- SRC: book p.272-273 -->
**Run random checks.** Ask your lawyer for a copy of something you already have. Drop in a question about something off topic, like the status of the investigation. If he said an expert would be needed, he should be able to give you a name. At every visit, ask at least one question about the discovery: who the government interviewed, who they plan to use as witnesses, who the lead agent is. If he doesn't know, you want him to say so. Don't be swindled by "that's not important."

## When a lawyer threatens to quit

<!-- SRC: book p.241 -->
If a lawyer threatens to leave your case unless you take a specific course of action, tell him to get off the case and get a new lawyer. You will not be left alone. If you cannot afford one, your old lawyer must file a motion for free counsel for you. A threat is different from a disagreement about strategy, and different from a lawyer who believes you are asking him to do something unethical.

## What to do right now

<!-- SRC: derived -->
1. Write down the five decisions that are yours and keep them where you can see them.
2. Stop discussing your case with anyone but your lawyer.
3. Mark legal mail "CONFIDENTIAL: ATTORNEY-CLIENT PRIVILEGE."
4. Ask your lawyer for copies of everything, and a docket sheet every month.
5. Have family pull the docket on PACER and compare it with what you are told.
6. Ask one question about the discovery at every visit.

Be Well, Stay Safe, and Survive the Feds,

## About the Author

Bilal Khan was sentenced to 20 years in the federal system. He is a United States Marine Corps veteran and an entrepreneur in the health care sector. Inside, he taught federal law classes to fellow inmates and helped hundreds navigate the complexities of BOP administrative remedies, post-conviction issues, and other legal challenges that inmates face. He is the author of two books in the Surviving the Feds Series:

**Surviving Pretrial** covers everything from the moment of arrest through sentencing — detention hearings, bail, discovery, plea negotiations, the PSR, rights in custody, and the strategies that actually move outcomes. If someone you love is in the federal system and hasn't been sentenced yet, this is the book.

**The 2255 Motion Handbook** addresses post-conviction relief — what a § 2255 motion is, when it applies, how to file it, what claims it can raise, and how to avoid the procedural mistakes that end cases before they start. If the sentence has already been imposed and you are looking for a path back to court, this is the book.

Both are available on Amazon under the Surviving the Feds Series.
