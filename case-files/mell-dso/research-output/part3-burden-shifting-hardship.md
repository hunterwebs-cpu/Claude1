## 0. EXECUTIVE ANSWER (read this first)

**The threshold question — "is there a burden-shifting framework under which a Chapter 7 debtor can except a true DSO from § 523(a)(5) by proving hardship, inability to pay, or the creditor-spouse's independent wealth?" — is answered NO.** There is no such framework. There has been none since October 17, 2005. Congress affirmatively deleted the only one that ever existed.

Worse for the debtor than the question assumes: post-BAPCPA, **even winning the characterization fight may buy nothing in Chapter 7**, because § 523(a)(15) makes the *non*-support residue of a divorce obligation nondischargeable too. The Third Circuit has said so. *Orr v. Edwards (In re Ruitenberg)*, 745 F.3d 647, 649 (3d Cir. 2014) (BINDING) ("under the changes implemented by the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005 ('BAPCPA'), Pub.L. No. 109-8, § 215(3), 119 Stat. 23 (2005), equitable distribution claims are nondischargeable in Chapter 7").

**Where a real, doctrinally supported burden-shifting framework actually lives is in the claims-allowance process, not the discharge process.** *In re Allegheny International, Inc.*, 954 F.2d 167, 173-74 (3d Cir. 1992) (BINDING), applied to a former spouse's DSO claim in *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. 782, 785 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2011) (PERSUASIVE). That is the single most useful doctrine in this memo. It goes to **amount and classification**, never to dischargeability.

The debtor's remaining genuine leverage is: (1) *amount* — objecting to the claim under § 502(b) and Rule 3007; (2) *classification for priority and Chapter 13 purposes* under *Gianakas*; (3) *timing* (whether a claim exists at all, and whether it is pre- or post-petition); (4) *chapter selection* (Chapter 13 discharges § 523(a)(15) debt; Chapter 7 does not); and (5) *state family court modification* under N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23 and *Lepis*, which the automatic stay does not bar.

## 1. THE THRESHOLD ANSWER: § 523(a)(5) CONTAINS NO HARDSHIP, ABILITY-TO-PAY, OR BALANCING DEFENSE

### 1.1 The operative text, in full

11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(5), in its entirety, reads:

> "(5) for a domestic support obligation;"

*Source: uscode.house.gov, prelim edition, 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(5).* That is the whole paragraph. Nine words. There is no proviso, no "unless," no exception, no balancing clause, no reference to the debtor's income, assets, expenses, dependents, or circumstances, and no reference to the creditor's wealth.

The introductory language of § 523(a) supplies the operative command:

> "(a) A discharge under section 727, 1141, 1192, 1228(a), 1228(b), or 1328(b) of this title does not discharge an individual debtor from any debt—"

### 1.2 The contrast Congress drew: § 523(a)(8)

11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8) opens:

> "(8) unless excepting such debt from discharge under this paragraph would impose an undue hardship on the debtor and the debtor's dependents, for—
> (A)(i) an educational benefit overpayment or loan made, insured, or guaranteed by a governmental unit, or made under any program funded in whole or in part by a governmental unit or nonprofit institution; or
> (ii) an obligation to repay funds received as an educational benefit, scholarship, or stipend; or
> (B) any other educational loan that is a qualified education loan, as defined in section 221(d)(1) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986, incurred by a debtor who is an individual;"

The undue-hardship clause is placed at the very front of the paragraph, before the enumerated debts, in the same subsection, only three paragraphs away from (a)(5). Congress knew exactly how to write a hardship escape hatch into § 523(a). It wrote one into (a)(8). It wrote none into (a)(5).

### 1.3 The contrast Congress drew: § 1328(b) (Chapter 13 "hardship discharge")

11 U.S.C. § 1328(b):

> "(b) Subject to subsection (d), at any time after the confirmation of the plan and after notice and a hearing, the court may grant a discharge to a debtor that has not completed payments under the plan only if—
> (1) the debtor's failure to complete such payments is due to circumstances for which the debtor should not justly be held accountable;
> (2) the value, as of the effective date of the plan, of property actually distributed under the plan on account of each allowed unsecured claim is not less than the amount that would have been paid on such claim if the estate of the debtor had been liquidated under chapter 7 of this title on such date; and
> (3) modification of the plan under section 1329 of this title is not practicable."

**Note the trap.** § 1328(b) is a *hardship discharge*, but it does not reach a DSO. § 1328(c) provides:

> "(c) A discharge granted under subsection (b) of this section discharges the debtor from all unsecured debts provided for by the plan or disallowed under section 502 of this title, except any debt—
> (1) provided for under section 1322(b)(5) of this title; or
> (2) of a kind specified in section 523(a) of this title."

A § 1328(b) hardship discharge therefore excepts **all** of § 523(a) — both (a)(5) and (a)(15). The "hardship discharge" is *narrower* than the full-completion discharge of § 1328(a), not broader. Any argument that pins hope on § 1328(b) is backwards.

### 1.4 The negative-implication canon

The governing canon is stated in *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983) (BINDING — SCOTUS):

> "[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion."

*Russello* continues, quoting *United States v. Naftalin*, 441 U.S. 768, 773 (1979): **"The short answer is that Congress did not write the statute that way."** *Russello*, 464 U.S. at 23.

Applied here: Congress wrote "unless excepting such debt from discharge under this paragraph would impose an undue hardship on the debtor and the debtor's dependents" into § 523(a)(8) and did not write it into § 523(a)(5). Under *Russello*, that omission is presumed intentional. The presumption is at its strongest here because (a)(5) and (a)(8) sit in the same subsection of the same section of the same Act, and because — as Part 2 shows — Congress in 2005 *affirmatively removed* the closest analogue to a hardship defense from the neighboring paragraph while simultaneously rewriting (a)(5) and leaving (a)(8)'s hardship clause in place.

### 1.5 A case making the (a)(5)/(a)(8) comparison expressly

The Supreme Court came close in *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 287-88 (1991) (BINDING), reasoning from the *structure* of § 523(a):

> "Our conviction that Congress intended the preponderance standard to apply to the discharge exceptions is reinforced by the structure of § 523(a), which groups together in the same subsection a variety of exceptions without any indication that any particular exception is subject to a special standard of proof. The omission of any suggestion that different exemptions have different burdens of proof implies that the legislators intended the same standard to govern the nondischargeability under § 523(a)(2) of fraud claims and, for example, the nondischargeability under § 523(a)(5) of claims for child support and alimony."

*Grogan* is a burden-of-proof case, not a hardship case, but the interpretive move is the same one: silence in one paragraph, against express language in another, is meaningful.

**CANDID FLAG.** I did not locate a reported decision that states, in terms, "§ 523(a)(5) contains no undue hardship exception, unlike § 523(a)(8)." Multiple searches across CourtListener's opinion corpus for that comparison returned nothing directly on point. `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING]` — if such a case exists it is worth finding, but **do not represent to the court that one has been found unless it has been read.** The argument is strong on the statutory text plus *Russello* alone and does not need a case to carry it.

## 2. THE BAPCPA DELETION — CONGRESS REMOVED THE DEBTOR'S HARDSHIP DEFENSE ON PURPOSE

This is the most important historical point in the memo, and it cuts against the debtor. It should be stated to the client plainly.

### 2.1 Pre-BAPCPA § 523(a)(15) (1994–2005), VERBATIM

From the 1999 edition of the United States Code (uscode.house.gov, USC-1999-title11-section523), 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(15) then read:

> "(15) not of the kind described in paragraph (5) that is incurred by the debtor in the course of a divorce or separation or in connection with a separation agreement, divorce decree or other order of a court of record, a determination made in accordance with State or territorial law by a governmental unit unless—
> (A) the debtor does not have the ability to pay such debt from income or property of the debtor not reasonably necessary to be expended for the maintenance or support of the debtor or a dependent of the debtor and, if the debtor is engaged in a business, for the payment of expenditures necessary for the continuation, preservation, and operation of such business; or
> (B) discharging such debt would result in a benefit to the debtor that outweighs the detrimental consequences to a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor;"

### 2.2 Pre-BAPCPA § 523(a)(5) (through 2005), VERBATIM

Same source, 1999 edition:

> "(5) to a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor, for alimony to, maintenance for, or support of such spouse or child, in connection with a separation agreement, divorce decree or other order of a court of record, determination made in accordance with State or territorial law by a governmental unit, or property settlement agreement, but not to the extent that—
> (A) such debt is assigned to another entity, voluntarily, by operation of law, or otherwise (other than debts assigned pursuant to section 408(a)(3) of the Social Security Act, or any such debt which has been assigned to the Federal Government or to a State or any political subdivision of such State); or
> (B) such debt includes a liability designated as alimony, maintenance, or support, unless such liability is actually in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support;"

**Critically: even the pre-BAPCPA (a)(5) had no hardship or ability-to-pay defense.** The (A)/(B) provisos in old (a)(5) were an *assignment* carve-out and a *substance-over-label* carve-out. The affirmative defenses lived only in old (a)(15) — i.e., only for the *non*-support debts. There has never been an ability-to-pay defense to a true support obligation under the Bankruptcy Code.

### 2.3 The BAPCPA amendment, VERBATIM (Pub. L. No. 109-8, § 215)

From the enrolled act (govinfo.gov, PLAW-109publ8), Title II, § 215:

> "SEC. 215. NONDISCHARGEABILITY OF CERTAIN DEBTS FOR ALIMONY, MAINTENANCE, AND SUPPORT.
>
> Section 523 of title 11, United States Code, is amended—
> (1) in subsection (a)—
> (A) by striking paragraph (5) and inserting the following:
> `(5) for a domestic support obligation;'; and
> (B) by striking paragraph (18);
> (2) in subsection (c), by striking `(6), or (15)' each place it appears and inserting `or (6)'; and
> (3) in paragraph (15), as added by Public Law 103-394 (108 Stat. 4133)—
> (A) by inserting `to a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor and' before `not of the kind';
> (B) by inserting `or' after `court of record,'; and
> (C) by striking `unless—' and all that follows through the end of the paragraph and inserting a semicolon."

Subparagraph **(3)(C) is the deletion**: "by striking `unless—' and all that follows through the end of the paragraph and inserting a semicolon." That single clause erased both the ability-to-pay defense and the benefit/detriment balancing defense.

The codified amendment note in the current U.S. Code confirms the same, reciting the struck text verbatim:

> "Pub. L. 109–8, §215(3), in par. (15), inserted 'to a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor and' before 'not of the kind' and 'or' after 'court of record,' and substituted a semicolon for 'unless—
> "(A) the debtor does not have the ability to pay such debt from income or property of the debtor not reasonably necessary to be expended for the maintenance or support of the debtor or a dependent of the debtor and, if the debtor is engaged in a business, for the payment of expenditures necessary for the continuation, preservation, and operation of such business; or
> "(B) discharging such debt would result in a benefit to the debtor that outweighs the detrimental consequences to a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor;'."

*Source: 11 U.S.C. § 523, 2005 Amendment notes, uscode.house.gov prelim edition.*

### 2.4 Legislative history — Congress said what it was doing

H.R. Rep. No. 109-31, pt. 1 (2005) (govinfo.gov, CRPT-109hrpt31-pt1), section-by-section analysis:

Section 215 of the Act amends Bankruptcy Code section 523(a)(5) to provide that a ``domestic support obligation'' (as defined in section 211 of the Act) is nondischargeable and eliminates Bankruptcy Code section 523(a)(18). Section 215(2) amends Bankruptcy Code section 523(c) to delete the reference to section 523(a)(15) in that provision. Section 215(3) amends section 523(a)(15) to provide that obligations to a spouse, former spouse, or a child of the debtor (not otherwise described in section 523(a)(5)) incurred in connection with a divorce or separation or related action are **nondischargeable irrespective of the debtor's inability to pay such debts**."

(emphasis added). Pin cite reported as H.R. Rep. No. 109-31(I), at 61 (2005) in *Taylor v. ### 2.6 The dead framework — for the record, so it can be identified and killed on sight

Opposing counsel or a court reaching for habit may cite the following. **All of it is superseded for any case commenced on or after October 17, 2005.**

- ***In re Crosswhite***, 148 F.3d 879, 884-85 (7th Cir. 1998) (PERSUASIVE — 7th Cir.; **SUPERSEDED BY STATUTE** as to the burden framework):
  > "Upon consideration of the statutory language, the structure of the statute, the legislative history and the case law, we conclude that there is a clear shift in the burden of proof under § 523(a)(15). The burden of proving initially that she holds a subsection (15) claim against the debtor should be borne by the creditor (nondebtor/former spouse). To make that showing, the creditor must establish that the debt is within the purview of subsection (15) by demonstrating that it does not fall under § 523(a)(5) and that it nevertheless was incurred by the debtor in the course of the divorce or in connection with a divorce decree or similar agreement. Once that showing has been established, the burden of proving that he falls within either of the two exceptions to nondischargeability rests with the debtor. In short, once the creditor's initial proof is made, the debt is excepted from discharge and the debtor is responsible for the debt unless either of the two exceptions, subpart (A), the 'ability to pay' test, or (B), the 'detriment' test, can be proven by the debtor."

  And at 885: "the statute clearly places the ultimate burden on the debtor to prove to the court, in the manner of an affirmative defense, that the debt should be discharged under subpart (A) because he is unable to pay or under subpart (B) because the benefit to him outweighs the harm to the creditor."

### 2.7 STATE IT PLAINLY

**The pre-BAPCPA burden-shifting framework of § 523(a)(15)(A) and (B) is DEAD for every case filed on or after October 17, 2005.** It was not narrowed, distinguished, or judicially limited. Congress struck the text. Any brief, opinion, or argument invoking "ability to pay" or "benefit outweighs detriment" as a discharge defense in a post-2005 case is citing repealed law.

Post-BAPCPA courts have said so:

- *Berse v. Langman (In re Langman)*, 465 B.R. 395, 405 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2012) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.):
  > "First, BAPCPA eliminated two balancing tests that had formerly provided debtors with defenses to nondischargeability for non-support debts based on (1) whether the debtor would be able to pay the debt and (2) whether discharging the debt would result in a benefit to the debtor that would outweigh the detrimental consequences to the former spouse or child."

- *Clair, Griefer LLP v. Prensky (In re Prensky)*, 416 B.R. 406, 409 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2009) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.):
  > "Prior to the enactment of BAPCPA in 2005, § 523(a)(15) included a requirement that the debtor have an 'ability to pay' the debt. However, BAPCPA modified § 523(a)(15) to remove, among other things, the 'ability to pay' component, thereby no longer conditioning the discharge of debts of the nature described in § 523(a)(15) upon the Debtor's ability to pay."

- *Tarone v. Tarone (In re Tarone)*, 434 B.R. 41, 49 (Bankr. E.D.N.Y. 2010) (PERSUASIVE), quoted in *Langman*, 465 B.R. at 406: "[U]nder BAPCPA, all debts owed to a spouse, former spouse, or child of a debtor are nondischargeable if incurred in the course of a divorce proceeding, notwithstanding the debtor's ability to pay the debt or the relative benefits and detriments to the parties."

**The deletion is also the strongest possible support for the § 1.4 negative-implication argument.** Congress in 2005 (i) rewrote (a)(5) to nine words with no defense, (ii) *deleted* the only ability-to-pay/balancing defense in the neighborhood, and (iii) left (a)(8)'s undue-hardship clause standing and even re-enacted (a)(8) by § 220 of the same Act. The disparate treatment is not an accident of drafting; it is a documented, deliberate, contemporaneous choice.

## 3. WHERE THE DEBTOR'S REAL LEVERAGE IS

### 3(a) CHARACTERIZATION, NOT DISCHARGE — and its post-BAPCPA limits

#### The definition

11 U.S.C. § 101(14A) (VERBATIM, uscode.house.gov prelim edition):

> "(14A) The term 'domestic support obligation' means a debt that accrues before, on, or after the date of the order for relief in a case under this title, including interest that accrues on that debt as provided under applicable nonbankruptcy law notwithstanding any other provision of this title, that is—
> (A) owed to or recoverable by—
> (i) a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor or such child's parent, legal guardian, or responsible relative; or
> (ii) a governmental unit;
> (B) in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support (including assistance provided by a governmental unit) of such spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor or such child's parent, without regard to whether such debt is expressly so designated;
> (C) established or subject to establishment before, on, or after the date of the order for relief in a case under this title, by reason of applicable provisions of—
> (i) a separation agreement, divorce decree, or property settlement agreement;
> (ii) an order of a court of record; or
> (iii) a determination made in accordance with applicable nonbankruptcy law by a governmental unit; and
> (D) not assigned to a nongovernmental entity, unless that obligation is assigned voluntarily by the spouse, former spouse, child of the debtor, or such child's parent, legal guardian, or responsible relative for the purpose of collecting the debt."

The four elements are stated in *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. 782, 786 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2011) (Kaplan, J.) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.):

> "Thus, the elements that must be satisfied for a domestic support obligation to arise are as follows: (i) the payee of the obligation must be either a governmental unit or a person with a particular relationship to the debtor or a child of the debtor; (ii) the nature of the obligation must be support; (iii) the source of the obligation must be an agreement, court order, or other determination; and (iv) the assignment status of the obligation must be consistent with paragraph (D)."

BAPCPA did not change the substantive test. *Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 786: "Although the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005 ('BAPCPA') changed the language in 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(5) to apply to 'domestic support obligations' as defined in 11 U.S.C. § 101(14A), this change 'did not change the standard for whether an obligation is in the nature of support.'" (quoting *Stover v. Phegley (In re Phegley)*, 443 B.R. 154, 157 (B.A.P. 8th Cir. 2011)); accord *Langman*, 465 B.R. at 404.

#### The *Gianakas* factors, VERBATIM with pinpoints

***Gianakas v. Gianakas (In re Gianakas)***, 917 F.2d 759 (3d Cir. 1990) (**BINDING — 3d Cir.**).

Governing-law and framing, 917 F.2d at 762:

> "Moreover, although the decree or settlement establishing the obligation almost invariably arises in the context of a state court proceeding, whether the obligation is in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support for the purposes of the Bankruptcy Code is a question of federal, not state, law."

> "We believe that whether an obligation is in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support, as distinguished from a property settlement, depends on a finding as to the intent of the parties at the time of the settlement agreement. . . . That intent can best be found by examining three principal indicators. The many other factors referred to by various courts are merely elements of these indicators."

*Id.* at 762.

**Indicator One** — 917 F.2d at 762-63:

> "First, the court must examine the language and substance of the agreement in the context of surrounding circumstances, using extrinsic evidence if necessary. However, it is likely that 'neither the parties nor the divorce court contemplated the effect of a subsequent bankruptcy when the obligation arose.' Therefore, the parties and the state courts may not have focused on whether a particular obligation was to serve as support or as a property settlement unrelated to support. As the Pennsylvania Superior Court noted, even an obligation designated as property settlement may be related to support because state courts often will adjust alimony awards depending on the nature and amount of marital assets available for distribution. In fact, 'property division often achieves the same goal as alimony, i.e., support.'"

(internal citations omitted).

**Indicator Two — the parties' financial circumstances at the time of the settlement** — 917 F.2d at 763. **This is the pinpoint the wealth argument runs through:**

> "Because the language of the agreement alone may not provide a sufficiently conclusive answer as to the nature of an obligation, the second indicator to which we must look to assist in ascertaining the parties' intent is the parties' financial circumstances at the time of the settlement. The facts that one spouse had custody of minor children, was not employed, or was employed in a less remunerative position than the other spouse are aspects of the parties' financial circumstances at the time the obligation was fixed which shed light on the inquiry into the nature of the obligation as support."

**Indicator Three** — 917 F.2d at 763:

> "Third, the court should examine the function served by the obligation at the time of the divorce or settlement. An obligation that serves to maintain daily necessities such as food, housing and transportation is indicative of a debt intended to be in the nature of support."

Standard of review, 917 F.2d at 761-62:

> "The findings of fact by the bankruptcy court on the underlying or historical facts, such as the intent of the parties at the time of the agreement, are subject to review for clear error. Of course, questions of law, which would include identification of the factors that are relevant to the determination, are subject to plenary review."

#### The single most important limiting holding in *Gianakas* — read this before building any wealth argument

917 F.2d at 763 (BINDING):

> "We agree with the majority view, and hold that the inquiry of the bankruptcy court should be limited to the nature of the obligation at the time it was undertaken. Where appropriate and authorized by state law, debtors can resort to state court to seek modifications of divorce settlements on the grounds of changed circumstances."

And immediately preceding, rejecting the *Calhoun* "present need" approach, 917 F.2d at 763:

> "As explained in *Forsdick*, 812 F.2d at 803-04, an inquiry into present need 'would put federal courts in the position of modifying matrimonial decrees of state courts, thus interfering with the delicate state systems for dealing with the dissolution of marriages. . . .' It would also serve essentially as a penalty for a former spouse who may have struggled to gain self-sufficiency."

**Consequences, stated candidly:**

1. **The creditor-spouse's wealth is relevant ONLY as of the time of the settlement/decree.** "She is wealthy now" is legally irrelevant under binding Third Circuit law. "She was wealthy and financially independent *at the time the obligation was fixed*, and therefore the parties cannot have intended this obligation as support" is a proper *Gianakas* Indicator-Two argument. The temporal line is the whole ballgame.

2. **A former spouse's post-decree self-sufficiency actively cuts the other way.** *Gianakas* says so in terms: inquiry into present need "would . . . serve essentially as a penalty for a former spouse who may have struggled to gain self-sufficiency." Any brief that leads with the creditor's current net worth invites the court to quote this sentence back.

3. Bankr. D.N.J. has applied the limitation to *reject* an ex-spouse's equitable appeal based on present circumstances. *Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 787:
   > "Although the Court is sensitive to Creditor's current circumstances, the Third Circuit has expressly rejected the view that a debt may be designated a domestic support obligation based on the parties' present needs."

   Note that in *Anthony* the present-circumstances bar ran *against the creditor*. It is a neutral rule; it will run against the debtor with equal force.

#### Cases where the recipient's financial position drove characterization

The clearest illustration cited *by* the Third Circuit is *Shaver v. Shaver*, 736 F.2d 1314, 1317 (9th Cir. 1984) (PERSUASIVE — 9th Cir.), cited in *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763. *Shaver* found support where the recipient "was unemployed and possessed no special job-related skills," three minor children remained in her custody, and the payor "was the sole owner of a car dealership and had substantial income." 736 F.2d at 1317. The court added:

> "In making the so-called property settlement in the initial decree, the judge stated that he had considered '[t]he economic circumstances of the spouse at the time the disposition of property is to become effective [and] [t]he earnings or earning ability of the parties'. Such considerations are irrelevant to a true property settlement, but are of primary importance to a settlement intended for support."

*Shaver* is the mirror image of the debtor's argument: it shows that the *inverse* facts — recipient employed, independently wealthy, no custodial burden, comparable or superior earning capacity at the time of the decree — are precisely the evidence that defeats support characterization. That inference is sound but the memo must be honest that *Shaver* is a case for the *creditor* on its own facts; it is cited for its reasoning, not its holding.

#### THE HARD TRUTH ABOUT CHARACTERIZATION IN CHAPTER 7

**Winning the *Gianakas* fight may accomplish nothing on dischargeability in a Chapter 7 case.** Since BAPCPA, § 523(a)(15) catches whatever falls out of (a)(5).

11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(15) (current, VERBATIM):

> "(15) to a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor and not of the kind described in paragraph (5) that is incurred by the debtor in the course of a divorce or separation or in connection with a separation agreement, divorce decree or other order of a court of record, or a determination made in accordance with State or territorial law by a governmental unit;"

**BINDING Third Circuit:** *In re Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d 647, 649 (3d Cir. 2014):

> "And, as acknowledged by the parties, under the changes implemented by the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005 ('BAPCPA'), Pub.L. No. 109-8, § 215(3), 119 Stat. 23 (2005), equitable distribution claims are nondischargeable in Chapter 7."

**Bankr. D.N.J.:** *Langman*, 465 B.R. at 406:

> "As a practical consequence, it is now no longer necessary for bankruptcy courts to determine the exact extent to which a state court matrimonial judgment constitutes a 'domestic support obligation' if the plaintiff can demonstrate that the judgment would be nondischargeable in any event under § 523(a)(15) as a debt that is (1) owed to 'a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor' and that was (2) 'incurred by the debtor in the course of a divorce or separation or in connection with a separation agreement, divorce decree or other order of a court of record.'"

*Langman*, 465 B.R. at 406, quoting *Gilman v. Golio (In re Golio)*, 393 B.R. 56, 61-62 (Bankr. E.D.N.Y. 2008):

> "In individual Chapter 7 and 11 cases, the distinction between a domestic support obligation and other types of obligations arising out of a marital relationship is of no practical consequence in determining the dischargeability of the debt."

**So why fight characterization at all?** Because it still matters, materially, in five places:

| Consequence | If DSO under § 523(a)(5) | If property settlement under § 523(a)(15) |
|---|---|---|
| Ch. 7 dischargeability | Nondischargeable | **Also nondischargeable** — no difference |
| Ch. 13 dischargeability (§ 1328(a)(2)) | Nondischargeable | **DISCHARGED on plan completion** |
| Priority (§ 507(a)(1)(A)) | First priority | General unsecured |
| Exempt property liability (§ 522(c)(1)) | Exempt property **liable** | Exempt property not liable on this ground |
| Automatic stay (§ 362(b)(2)) | Broad enforcement exceptions apply | Stay applies |
| Trustee duties, § 707(b) means-test treatment, § 1325(b) | DSO-specific rules | General rules |

`[VERIFY BEFORE FILING] — the § 707(b)/§ 1325(b) row is stated at a general level and was not separately researched in this track.`

**Therefore: characterization is a priority/chapter-selection/exemption fight, not a Chapter 7 discharge fight.** Framing it to a Bankr. D.N.J. judge as a discharge argument invites *Langman* and *Ruitenberg* in reply.

### 3(b) BURDEN OF PROOF AND WHO CARRIES IT

#### The creditor carries it, by a preponderance

***Grogan v. Garner***, 498 U.S. 279, 291 (1991) (**BINDING — SCOTUS**):

> "For these reasons, we hold that the standard of proof for the dischargeability exceptions in 11 U.S.C. § 523(a) is the ordinary preponderance-of-the-evidence standard."

And *id.* at 287:

> "Requiring the creditor to establish by a preponderance of the evidence that his claim is not dischargeable reflects a fair balance between these conflicting interests."

Third Circuit application: *Berse v. Langman (In re Langman)*, 465 B.R. 395, 403 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2012) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.):

> "As a result, in a trial on the merits, the creditor bears the burden of proving that a debt is nondischargeable under § 523(a) under a preponderance of the evidence standard, and 'exceptions to discharge are strictly construed against creditors and liberally construed in favor of debtors.'"

Pre-BAPCPA but still-good Third Circuit statement on who bears the § 523(a)(5) burden — *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 761 (**BINDING**):

> "A party, such as appellee here, who objects to the discharge of a particular debt has the burden of proving nondischargeability."

#### Third Circuit authority on narrow construction

***Insurance Co. of North America v. Cohn (In re Cohn)***, 54 F.3d 1108, 1113 (3d Cir. 1995) (**BINDING — 3d Cir.**):

> "The overriding purpose of the Bankruptcy Code is to relieve debtors from the weight of oppressive indebtedness and provide them with a fresh start. Exceptions to discharge are strictly construed against creditors and liberally construed in favor of debtors."

#### DOES ANY BURDEN SHIFT TO THE DEBTOR IN A DSO CHARACTERIZATION DISPUTE?

**No.** I found no Third Circuit or Bankr. D.N.J. authority applying a burden-shifting framework to the § 523(a)(5)/§ 101(14A) characterization question. The burden is unitary and rests on the creditor throughout. *Gianakas* itself confirms this by describing the district court's cure of a possible misallocation below, 917 F.2d at 761:

> "Faced with this inconsistency, the district court undertook to review the bankruptcy court's findings in light of the burden of proof placed upon Karen Gianakas . . . . Because the district court placed the burden of proof on the party that must bear it and thereafter reached the same result that the bankruptcy court reached, Paul was not prejudiced even if the bankruptcy court had misplaced the burden of proof."

That is meaningfully favorable: it establishes that misallocating the § 523(a)(5) burden to the debtor is error, and that the Third Circuit will police it.

#### THE CANDID COUNTERWEIGHT — do not brief narrow construction without knowing this

Several courts hold that the ordinary "construe narrowly against the creditor" canon is **relaxed or reversed** for the marital-obligation exceptions. *In re Crosswhite*, 148 F.3d 879, 881-82 (7th Cir. 1998) (PERSUASIVE — 7th Cir.):

> "That policy of protecting and favoring the debtor is tempered, however, when the debt arises from a divorce or separation agreement. See 4 Lawrence P. King, Collier on Bankruptcy ¶¶ 523.05, 523.11[2] (15th ed. rev.1998) (stating that, with respect to enforcement of obligations for spousal and child support, Congress 'has overridden the general bankruptcy policy in which exceptions to discharge are construed narrowly' against a creditor). . . . Because of this Congressional determination, a § 523(a)(5) exception from discharge is construed more liberally than other § 523 exceptions."

The Third Circuit has recognized the same congressional preference in *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 761:

> "These provisions reflect the congressional preference for the rights of spouses to alimony, maintenance or support over the rights of debtors to a 'fresh start.'"

`[VERIFY BEFORE FILING] — whether the Third Circuit has ever squarely held that the Cohn narrow-construction canon is relaxed for § 523(a)(5). I found no such holding. ### 3(c) AMOUNT AND ACCOUNTING — **THE REAL BURDEN-SHIFTING DOCTRINE**

This is the most useful part of the memo.

#### The framework — *Allegheny International*, VERBATIM

***In re Allegheny International, Inc.***, 954 F.2d 167, 173-74 (3d Cir. 1992) (**BINDING — 3d Cir.**):

> "The burden of proof for claims brought in the bankruptcy court under 11 U.S.C.A. § 502(a) rests on different parties at different times. Initially, the claimant must allege facts sufficient to support the claim. If the averments in his filed claim meet this standard of sufficiency, it is 'prima facie' valid. In other words, a claim that alleges facts sufficient to support a legal liability to the claimant satisfies the claimant's initial obligation to go forward. The burden of going forward then shifts to the objector to produce evidence sufficient to negate the prima facie validity of the filed claim. It is often said that the objector must produce evidence equal in force to the prima facie case. In practice, the objector must produce evidence which, if believed, would refute at least one of the allegations that is essential to the claim's legal sufficiency. If the objector produces sufficient evidence to negate one or more of the sworn facts in the proof of claim, the burden reverts to the claimant to prove the validity of the claim by a preponderance of the evidence. **The burden of persuasion is always on the claimant.**"

(internal citations omitted; emphasis added).

**Bankr. D.N.J. has applied this framework to a former spouse's DSO claim.** *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. 782, 785 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2011) (Kaplan, J.) (PERSUASIVE), quoting *Allegheny International* at 173-74 and concluding:

> "Thus, as the objecting party, Debtor carries the burden to overcome the rebuttable presumption that Creditor has a valid claim. If Debtor produces sufficient evidence to overcome such burden, then it is incumbent upon Creditor to prove the validity of her claim by a preponderance of the evidence."

*Anthony* is the template. In that case the debtor objected to an ex-spouse's proof of claim and **partially won**: the court sustained the DSO classification of alimony and child support but **reclassified unpaid condominium maintenance fees as general unsecured claims**, and allowed post-petition interest on child-support arrears only, not on alimony. 453 B.R. at 787-89.

#### The statutory and rule scaffolding

11 U.S.C. § 502(a):

> "(a) A claim or interest, proof of which is filed under section 501 of this title, is deemed allowed, unless a party in interest, including a creditor of a general partner in a partnership that is a debtor in a case under chapter 7 of this title, objects."

11 U.S.C. § 502(b) (opening and (b)(1)):

> "(b) Except as provided in subsections (e)(2), (f), (g), (h) and (i) of this section, if such objection to a claim is made, the court, after notice and a hearing, shall determine the amount of such claim in lawful currency of the United States as of the date of the filing of the petition, and shall allow such claim in such amount, except to the extent that—
> (1) such claim is unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor, under any agreement or applicable law for a reason other than because such claim is contingent or unmatured;"

**Note § 502(b)(5) — an easy trap and a real limitation:**

> "(5) such claim is for a debt that is unmatured on the date of the filing of the petition and that is excepted from discharge under section 523(a)(5) of this title;"

That is, **future, not-yet-due DSO payments are DISALLOWED as claims** — but disallowance does not make them dischargeable. The debtor gets nothing from § 502(b)(5) except a smaller claim in the estate; the obligation rides through the case intact. Do not confuse claim disallowance with discharge.

Fed. R. Bankr. P. 3001(f):

> "(f) Proof of Claim as Prima Facie Evidence of a Claim and Its Amount. A proof of claim signed and filed in accordance with these rules is prima facie evidence of the claim's validity and amount."

Fed. R. Bankr. P. 3007(a)(1):

> "(1) Time to Serve. An objection to a claim and a notice of the objection must be filed and served at least 30 days before a scheduled hearing on the objection or any deadline for the claim holder to request a hearing."

`[VERIFY BEFORE FILING] — Rule 3007 was restyled effective December 1, 2024. Confirm the operative text and the D.N.J. local-rule overlay (D.N.J. LBR 3007-1) before filing. The version quoted is the current restyled text from law.cornell.edu.`

#### **KEY POINT: prima facie validity attaches only to a properly documented claim**

Rule 3001(f) confers prima facie effect only on a proof of claim "signed and filed **in accordance with these rules**." A proof of claim asserting a lump number with no supporting order, no ledger, and no arrears computation is vulnerable at the threshold: it may never earn the presumption at all, in which case the claimant bears the burden of production from the outset. And even if it does earn the presumption, *Allegheny International* is explicit that **"[t]he burden of persuasion is always on the claimant."** 954 F.2d at 174.

Where a creditor asserts a number **never reduced to a state-court judgment**, the debtor's path is:
1. Object under § 502(b) / Rule 3007, specifying which essential allegation is refuted.
2. Produce evidence — payment records, wage-garnishment history, bank records, the decree's own terms — that "if believed, would refute at least one of the allegations that is essential to the claim's legal sufficiency." *Allegheny Int'l*, 954 F.2d at 173-74.
3. On that showing, the burden of production reverts to the claimant to prove the amount by a preponderance. *Id.* at 174; *Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 785.

The bankruptcy court **does** determine the amount in that posture: § 502(b) commands that the court "shall determine the amount of such claim . . . as of the date of the filing of the petition." *Anthony* did exactly that, computing the allowed amount including pre- and post-petition interest on child-support arrears at the judgment rate. 453 B.R. at 788-89.

#### Where the amount is FIXED — Rooker-Feldman and § 1738

Where the number **has** been reduced to a final state-court judgment, the debtor cannot relitigate it. Two independent bars:

**Rooker-Feldman.** *Great Western Mining & Mineral Co. v. Fox Rothschild LLP*, 615 F.3d 159, 166 (3d Cir. 2010) (**BINDING — 3d Cir.**):

> "Breaking down the holding of *Exxon Mobil*, we conclude that there are four requirements that must be met for the *Rooker-Feldman* doctrine to apply: (1) the federal plaintiff lost in state court; (2) the plaintiff 'complain[s] of injuries caused by [the] state-court judgments'; (3) those judgments were rendered before the federal suit was filed; and (4) the plaintiff is inviting the district court to review and reject the state judgments."

A claim objection that says "the Family Part got the arrears math wrong" satisfies all four and is barred. A claim objection that says "the Family Part never fixed this number, and the creditor's arithmetic since the order is wrong" satisfies none of them and is a proper § 502(b) contest. **That distinction is the whole game on amount.**

**Full faith and credit, 28 U.S.C. § 1738.** `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING] — I did not pull § 1738's text or a Third Circuit case applying it to a DSO amount in bankruptcy. The doctrinal proposition (state judgments get the preclusive effect the rendering state would give them) is settled, but pull Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, 470 U.S. 373 (1985) and confirm New Jersey preclusion law before relying on it.`

**But dischargeability itself is never precluded.** *Brown v. Felsen*, 442 U.S. 127, 138-39 (1979) (**BINDING — SCOTUS**):

> "In sum, we reject respondent's contention that res judicata applies here and we hold that the bankruptcy court is not confined to a review of the judgment and record in the prior state-court proceedings when considering the dischargeability of respondent's debt."

So: **the state court fixes the amount; the bankruptcy court decides the character and the discharge.** Those are different questions and different records.

### 3(d) SEVERABILITY / PARTIAL CHARACTERIZATION

**Yes — a hybrid obligation can be split.** The textual hook is in § 101(14A)(B): the debt must be "in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support," which is a quality that can attach to part of a payment stream. Pre-BAPCPA § 523(a)(5) said so even more explicitly ("but not to the extent that . . .").

Authority:

- *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. 782, 787 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2011) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.) — the court sustained DSO treatment of alimony and child-support components and reclassified the condominium-fee component: "Thus, the Court, on this record, cannot find that payment of unpaid condominium fees are in the nature of domestic support obligations. Accordingly, Debtor's request to reclassify unpaid condominium fees as general unsecured claims is granted."

- *Jenkins v. Jenkins (In re Jenkins)*, 94 B.R. 355, 359 n.10 (Bankr. E.D. Pa. 1988) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. E.D. Pa., within the Third Circuit):
  > "The double payments were partially in the nature of support and partially in the nature of a property settlement. The discharge of the property settlement component will leave the plaintiff with a nondischargeable right to receive approximately 142 monthly payments of $250.00 beginning with the effective date of the divorce decree."

- *MacDonald*, 69 B.R. at 270 (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.), analyzing *Stein v. Fellerman*, 144 N.J. Super. 444 (App. Div. 1976), where a single New Jersey property settlement agreement was parsed provision by provision — weekly spousal and child payments held support, mortgage/tax/insurance provisions analyzed separately by their termination triggers.

**Evidentiary showing that supports apportionment.** Drawing the indicators together:
1. The obligation is expressed in a stream of periodic payments rather than a fixed sum (favors support for the periodic portion).
2. Some component terminates on death or remarriage; some does not (the terminating component is support; the non-terminating component is property). *MacDonald*, 69 B.R. at 270.
3. Some component funds "daily necessities such as food, housing and transportation." *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763.
4. The decree or agreement itself allocates, or the state court's fee/support findings allocate. Cf. *Cantwell v. Bearden (In re Bearden)*, 330 B.R. 214 (Bankr. N.D. Ill. 2005) (PERSUASIVE) (state court's allocation of fees between spouses reflected its assessment of relative resources) `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING] — pinpoint.`
5. Expert or forensic accounting testimony tracing the components.

**CANDID FLAG.** Apportionment is well-supported but is a **priority/Chapter 13 remedy, not a Chapter 7 discharge remedy**, for the reason in § 3(a): the severed property-settlement portion is caught by § 523(a)(15) in Chapter 7 anyway. In Chapter 13, apportionment is genuinely valuable — the severed portion is discharged under § 1328(a)(2).

### 3(e) INTEREST, FEES, AND ADD-ONS

#### Interest — nondischargeable, by the definition's own terms

§ 101(14A) defines a DSO as "a debt that accrues before, on, or after the date of the order for relief in a case under this title, **including interest that accrues on that debt as provided under applicable nonbankruptcy law notwithstanding any other provision of this title**, that is—". The interest clause is inside the definition. Interest on a DSO is therefore itself a DSO.

*In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. 782, 788-89 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2011) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.) applied this and looked to New Jersey law to set the rate and scope:

> "In light of the foregoing, it is clear that N.J. Court Rule 5:7-5(g), made applicable to Debtor's bankruptcy case by § 101(14A), entitles creditors to post-petition interest on pre-petition child support arrears. . . . With respect to alimony, however, N.J. Court Rule 5:7-5(a) provides the state court with discretion to award interest on such obligations. Nothing presented to the Court herein suggests that the state court elected to exercise its discretion to assess interest on the Debtor's alimony obligations; this Court declines to do so as well."

**This is a genuine, concrete, D.N.J.-specific debtor win available on the interest question.** Under *Anthony*, interest on **child support** arrears runs by rule; interest on **alimony** arrears runs only if the Family Part exercised its discretion to award it. If the state court never made that election, *Anthony* says the bankruptcy court should not make it either. `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING] — confirm current text of N.J. Ct. R. 5:7-5(a) and (g); the rule has been amended since 2011.`

#### Counsel fees — generally nondischargeable, and the law here is unfavorable

*In re Lawrence*, 237 B.R. 61, 84-85 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1999) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.):

> "The overwhelming majority of courts have concluded that attorney's fees and costs imposed in connection with a divorce or post-divorce action between former spouses are in the nature of support under Code section 523(a)(5)."

And *id.* at 85-86:

> "As is apparently true in most other states, New Jersey law considers the financial circumstances of the parties in imposing attorney's fees and costs in a divorce case. N.J.Stat.Ann. 2A:34-23; Boardman v. Boardman, supra. A substantial equitable distribution award to a party does not necessarily preclude an award of fees to such party. Shaffer v. Shaffer, 154 N.J.Super. 491, 381 A.2d 1221 (App.Div.1977). It follows that such fees and costs are in the nature of support and are therefore nondischargeable under Code section 523(a)(5)."

*Lawrence*, 237 B.R. at 85, quoting *Matter of Joseph*, 16 F.3d 86, 88 (5th Cir. 1994):

> "An attorney's fee award granted pursuant to a divorce decree does not elude discharge because it tangentially relates to an award of support and maintenance; rather, the attorney's fee award is deemed nondischargeable if the award itself reflects a balancing of the parties' financial needs."

**Note the irony and use it carefully.** Because New Jersey fee awards under N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23 turn expressly on "the respective financial circumstances of the parties," a fee award is *more* likely to be characterized as support — which is bad for the debtor generally. But it also means: **if the state court made no need-based findings, and instead awarded fees purely as a sanction or on a fee-shifting rationale unrelated to relative need, the *Joseph/Lawrence* rationale does not reach it.** That is a narrow but real characterization argument on the fee component.

Fees payable directly to a third-party law firm are still caught in this district. *In re Prensky*, 416 B.R. 406, 410 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2009) (PERSUASIVE), aff'd (D.N.J.), discussed in *Langman*, 465 B.R. at 406-07; *Romeo v. Romeo (In re Romeo)*, 16 B.R. 531, 536 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1981) (PERSUASIVE) ("Legal fees and costs expended by defendant spouse for enforcement of the nondischarged/nondischargeable debts are similarly held to be nondischargeable"). `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING] — Prensky pinpoint and the district court affirmance citation (referred to in Langman as "Prensky II"); I did not retrieve the D.N.J. opinion.`

#### Enforcement costs and collateral consequences — the debtor loses these too

- **Exempt property remains liable.** 11 U.S.C. § 522(c)(1): exempt property is not liable for prepetition debts "except— (1) a debt of a kind specified in paragraph (1) or (5) of section 523(a) (in which case, notwithstanding any provision of applicable nonbankruptcy law to the contrary, such property shall be liable for a debt of a kind specified in such paragraph)." Added by Pub. L. 109-8, § 216(1). This is unique to DSO — a § 523(a)(15) debt does **not** reach exempt property under § 522(c)(1). One of the few places the (a)(5)/(a)(15) line still bites in Chapter 7, and it bites the debtor.
- **The stay does not stop most DSO enforcement.** 11 U.S.C. § 362(b)(2) excepts, among others, "(B) of the collection of a domestic support obligation from property that is not property of the estate;" and "(C) with respect to the withholding of income that is property of the estate or property of the debtor for payment of a domestic support obligation under a judicial or administrative order or a statute;" plus license suspension, credit reporting, and tax-refund interception.
- **First priority.** § 507(a)(1)(A) gives DSOs first priority.

### 3(f) CHAPTER 13 CONTRAST — CONFIRMED, AND STRATEGICALLY THE MOST IMPORTANT ITEM IN PART 3

11 U.S.C. § 1328(a) (VERBATIM, relevant portion):

> "(a) Subject to subsection (d), as soon as practicable after completion by the debtor of all payments under the plan, and in the case of a debtor who is required by a judicial or administrative order, or by statute, to pay a domestic support obligation, after such debtor certifies that all amounts payable under such order or such statute that are due on or before the date of the certification (including amounts due before the petition was filed, but only to the extent provided for by the plan) have been paid, unless the court approves a written waiver of discharge executed by the debtor after the order for relief under this chapter, the court shall grant the debtor a discharge of all debts provided for by the plan or disallowed under section 502 of this title, except any debt—
> (1) provided for under section 1322(b)(5);
> **(2) of the kind specified in section 507(a)(8)(C) or in paragraph (1)(B), (1)(C), (2), (3), (4), (5), (8), or (9) of section 523(a);**
> (3) for restitution, or a criminal fine, included in a sentence on the debtor's conviction of a crime; or
> (4) for restitution, or damages, awarded in a civil action against the debtor as a result of willful or malicious injury by the debtor that caused personal injury to an individual or the death of an individual."

**CONFIRMED: § 1328(a)(2) lists paragraph (5) of § 523(a). It does NOT list paragraph (15).** Therefore:
- A § 523(a)(5) **DSO survives** a Chapter 13 full-completion discharge.
- A § 523(a)(15) **property-settlement debt is DISCHARGED** on completion of a Chapter 13 plan.

**Two conditions the client must understand:**
1. § 1328(a) requires the debtor to **certify that all DSO amounts due on or before the certification date have been paid.** No certification, no discharge.
2. § 1328(b)/(c) hardship discharge does **not** discharge § 523(a)(15) debt — § 1328(c)(2) excepts all of § 523(a). A Chapter 13 debtor who fails to complete the plan and takes a hardship discharge loses this benefit entirely.

**Strategic consequence.** Chapter 13 is the only chapter in which the *Gianakas* characterization fight produces a discharge. If a meaningful portion of the obligation is genuinely property settlement rather than support, the combination of (i) apportionment under § 3(d) and (ii) Chapter 13 under § 1328(a)(2) is the only route in the Code by which any part of a divorce obligation gets discharged. **This should be evaluated before a Chapter 7 petition is filed, not after.**

## 4. STATE-LAW MODIFICATION — THE DEBTOR'S ACTUAL REMEDY FOR INABILITY TO PAY

### 4.1 The bankruptcy court does not modify support

***Gianakas***, 917 F.2d at 763 (**BINDING — 3d Cir.**):

> "We agree with the majority view, and hold that the inquiry of the bankruptcy court should be limited to the nature of the obligation at the time it was undertaken. **Where appropriate and authorized by state law, debtors can resort to state court to seek modifications of divorce settlements on the grounds of changed circumstances.**"

(emphasis added). And *id.*, quoting *Forsdick v. Turgeon*, 812 F.2d 801, 803-04 (2d Cir. 1987): an inquiry into present need "would put federal courts in the position of modifying matrimonial decrees of state courts, thus interfering with the delicate state systems for dealing with the dissolution of marriages. . . ."

The Third Circuit's directive is explicit: **the forum for inability to pay is the Family Part, not the bankruptcy court.**

### 4.2 The automatic stay does not bar a modification motion

11 U.S.C. § 362(b)(2)(A)(ii) (VERBATIM): the filing of a petition does not operate as a stay —

> "(2) under subsection (a)—
> (A) of the commencement or continuation of a civil action or proceeding—
> . . .
> (ii) for the establishment or modification of an order for domestic support obligations;"

**The debtor can file a *Lepis* motion during the bankruptcy case.** No stay relief is required.

### 4.3 The New Jersey statute — VERIFIED

N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23, as quoted verbatim in *Lepis v. Lepis*, 83 N.J. 139, 145-46 (1980) (**BINDING in N.J. state court; PERSUASIVE/controlling state law in Bankr. D.N.J.**):

> "Pending any matrimonial action brought in this State or elsewhere, or after judgment of divorce or maintenance, whether obtained in this State or elsewhere, the court may make such order as to the alimony or maintenance of the parties, and also as to the care, custody, education and maintenance of the children, or any of them, as the circumstances of the parties and the nature of the case shall render fit, reasonable and just, and require reasonable security for the due observance of such orders. * * * **Orders so made may be revised and altered by the court from time to time as circumstances may require.**"

(emphasis added). `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING] — N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23 has been amended repeatedly since 1980, notably by the 2014 alimony reform (L. 2014, c. 42), which added subsection (k) governing modification on loss of income and codified factors for modification. Pull the current statute. The 1980 text quoted above is what Lepis construed; it is not necessarily the operative text today.`

### 4.4 *Lepis v. Lepis* — CITATION VERIFIED

**Verified:** *Lepis v. Lepis*, 83 N.J. 139 (1980), argued January 7, 1980, decided June 11, 1980, opinion by Justice Pashman. Retrieved and read in full from the Caselaw Access Project (nj/83/0139).

Holding on modifiability, 83 N.J. at 146:

> "As a result of this judicial authority, alimony and support orders define only the present obligations of the former spouses. Those duties are always subject to review and modification on a showing of 'changed circumstances.'"

**The *Lepis* burden-shifting framework — this is a genuine burden-shifting doctrine and it is the one the debtor can actually use**, 83 N.J. at 157:

> "The party seeking modification has the burden of showing such 'changed circumstances' as would warrant relief from the support or maintenance provisions involved. A prima facie showing of changed circumstances must be made before a court will order discovery of an ex-spouse's financial status. When the movant is seeking modification of an alimony award, that party must demonstrate that changed circumstances have substantially impaired the ability to support himself or herself. This requires full disclosure of the dependent spouse's financial status, including tax returns."

And *id.* at 157-58:

> "Only after the movant has made this prima facie showing should the respondent's ability to pay become a factor for the court to consider. Therefore, once a prima facie case is established, tax returns or other financial information should be ordered. . . . Because financial ability of the supporting spouse may be crucial to the proper disposition of a motion for modification, we conclude that a prima facie showing of changed circumstances meets this good cause standard."

**Note carefully:** in *Lepis* the debtor-payor is the *respondent*, and the framework is designed for the *dependent* spouse seeking an increase. A payor seeking a *decrease* invokes the same "changed circumstances" standard with the burden on him as movant, and his prima facie showing unlocks discovery into the recipient's finances. `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING] — pull current N.J. case law on a payor's motion to reduce or terminate alimony based on the recipient's independent wealth or the payor's income loss; the 2014 amendments and cases such as Larbig v. Larbig, 384 N.J. Super. 17 (App. Div. 2006) govern this and were not researched in this track.`

### 4.5 THE IRONY — equitable distribution in New Jersey is NOT modifiable

***Castriota v. Castriota***, 268 N.J. Super. 417, 421-22 (App. Div. 1993) (PERSUASIVE — N.J. App. Div.):

> "This much conceded, **equitable distribution agreements, unlike alimony and support obligations, are not subject to modification by reason of changed circumstances.** *Mahoney v. Mahoney*, 91 N.J. 488, 498, 453 A.2d 527 (1982); *Schwartzman v. Schwartzman*, 248 N.J.Super. 73, 77, 590 A.2d 246 (App.Div.), *certif. denied*, 126 N.J. 341, 598 A.2d 897 (1991); *Monte v. Monte*, 212 N.J.Super. 557, 561, 515 A.2d 1233 (App.Div.1986). Although there may be exceptions to this rule, such as where equitable distribution is a disguise for alimony, *see Connor v. Connor*, 254 N.J.Super. 591, 602, 604 A.2d 158 (App.Div.1992), the record is barren of anything requiring deviation from normal practice."

(emphasis added). `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING] — Mahoney, Schwartzman, Monte, and Connor were not independently retrieved; they are quoted from Castriota's text, which was retrieved in full.`

#### **The strategic paradox — state this to the client in exactly these terms**

The debtor faces a genuine **whipsaw**, and it must not be papered over:

| | Chapter 7 dischargeable? | Modifiable in N.J. Family Part? |
|---|---|---|
| **Characterize as SUPPORT (§ 523(a)(5))** | **No** | **YES** — N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23; *Lepis* |
| **Characterize as PROPERTY SETTLEMENT (§ 523(a)(15))** | **No** (*Ruitenberg*) | **NO** — *Castriota* |

**In Chapter 7, arguing that the obligation is a property settlement rather than support is strictly worse than doing nothing.** It does not produce a discharge (§ 523(a)(15) catches it; *Ruitenberg*), and if the argument succeeds it *destroys* the debtor's only real avenue of relief — the *Lepis* modification motion — because equitable distribution is not modifiable for changed circumstances (*Castriota*).

Note also the escape valve in *Castriota* runs the debtor's way on the *other* side: "where equitable distribution is a disguise for alimony," 268 N.J. Super. at 422 (citing *Connor*), the Family Part may treat it as modifiable. A debtor who wants relief should be arguing **toward** support characterization in the Family Part, not away from it — the precise opposite of the reflex in a bankruptcy dischargeability fight.

**In Chapter 13, the calculus flips**, because § 1328(a)(2) discharges § 523(a)(15) debt. There, property-settlement characterization is worth real money.

**Bottom line on Part 4: the chapter selection decision drives the characterization strategy, and the characterization strategy has irreversible consequences in the Family Part. These must be decided together and before filing.**

## 5. HONEST BOTTOM LINE — WHAT DOES NOT WORK

State each of these to the client without hedging.

1. **There is no undue-hardship escape hatch from a true DSO in Chapter 7.** § 523(a)(5) is nine words with no exception. § 523(a)(8)'s undue-hardship test applies to educational loans and nothing else. § 1328(b)'s hardship discharge is *narrower*, not broader — § 1328(c)(2) excepts all of § 523(a).

2. **Ability to pay is legally irrelevant to § 523(a)(5).** It has never been a defense to a support obligation under the Code. The only ability-to-pay defense that ever existed applied to *non*-support divorce debts under old § 523(a)(15)(A), and **Congress deleted it effective October 17, 2005.** Pub. L. 109-8, § 215(3)(C); § 1501(a). The House Report says the amended provision makes such debts "nondischargeable irrespective of the debtor's inability to pay such debts." H.R. Rep. No. 109-31, pt. 1.

3. **The creditor-spouse's wealth is not a discharge defense.** It is, at most, one input into *Gianakas* Indicator Two — "the parties' financial circumstances **at the time of the settlement**," 917 F.2d at 763 — and only as of that date. Her *present* wealth is affirmatively irrelevant under binding Third Circuit law, which holds "the inquiry of the bankruptcy court should be limited to the nature of the obligation at the time it was undertaken," *id.*, and which warns that a present-need inquiry "would . . . serve essentially as a penalty for a former spouse who may have struggled to gain self-sufficiency," *id.*

4. **There is no benefit/detriment balancing in bankruptcy anymore.** Deleted in 2005. Anyone citing *Crosswhite*, *Jodoin*, *Moeder*, *Gamble*, *Halper*, or *Lawrence* for a balancing defense in a post-2005 case is citing repealed law. Say so, with the Public Law cite.

5. **Winning the characterization fight probably does not produce a Chapter 7 discharge.** § 523(a)(15) catches the residue. *Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d at 649 (BINDING); *Langman*, 465 B.R. at 406; *Golio*, 393 B.R. at 61-62.

6. **The debtor cannot shelter behind exemptions.** § 522(c)(1) makes exempt property liable for a § 523(a)(5) debt "notwithstanding any provision of applicable nonbankruptcy law to the contrary."

7. **The automatic stay will not stop most DSO collection.** § 362(b)(2)(B)-(G).

8. **Arguing "property settlement, not support" in the Family Part is affirmatively harmful** — it forecloses *Lepis* modification. *Castriota*, 268 N.J. Super. at 421-22.

### What DOES work — the honest short list

1. **Claim objection under § 502(b) / Rule 3007, driving the *Allegheny International* burden-shifting framework.** 954 F.2d at 173-74 (BINDING); *Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 785 (Bankr. D.N.J.). Goes to **amount**, and the burden of persuasion sits on the claimant permanently. Strongest where the asserted number was never reduced to a state-court judgment and the proof of claim is thinly documented.
2. **Interest scope under *Anthony*.** Post-petition interest runs on N.J. child-support arrears by rule; on alimony arrears **only if the Family Part exercised its discretion to award it**. 453 B.R. at 788-89.
3. **Component-by-component reclassification** of non-support items (third-party debts, condominium/HOA fees, indemnities, sanction-based fee awards with no need findings). *Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 787; *Jenkins*, 94 B.R. at 359 n.10.
4. **Timing and claim-existence defenses.** *Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d at 651-52 (BINDING) (rejecting the *Frenville* accrual test; a spouse's equitable distribution interest is a prepetition "claim" under § 101(5) even without a final divorce decree); cf. *Berlingeri*, 246 B.R. at 199 (post-petition claims are outside § 727(b) and are not discharged at all — which cuts against the debtor). This area is fact-intensive and can cut either way.
5. **Chapter 13 instead of Chapter 7,** where a real property-settlement component exists. § 1328(a)(2).
6. **A *Lepis* motion in the New Jersey Family Part** — the only forum with power to reduce a genuine support obligation, available notwithstanding the automatic stay under § 362(b)(2)(A)(ii), and expressly endorsed by *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763.
