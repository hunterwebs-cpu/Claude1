## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1. **Ordinary contribution claims are discharged automatically.** A prepetition, contingent joint-tortfeasor contribution claim that is not a domestic support obligation and does not fall within an enumerated § 523(a) exception is discharged by operation of § 727(b) upon entry of discharge — no adversary proceeding, no creditor action of any kind, is required. This is confirmed by verbatim statutory text below and is not a close question.

2. **The adversary-complaint mechanism is opt-in and applies to only three exceptions.** Only § 523(a)(2), (a)(4), and (a)(6) require a creditor to file a timely adversary complaint (Rule 4007(c), 60-day bar tied to the first § 341 meeting date) or lose the exception as a matter of law. § 523(a)(5) and (a)(15) are outside that list and may be raised "at any time" (Rule 4007(b)), confirming prior research in Part I of this file.

3. **Whether Whalen's "contribution" claim fits any § 523(a) exception at all is genuinely unresolved on the facts as given** — and that is the whole point of the "central question." A bare state-law joint-tortfeasor contribution claim, standing alone, does not naturally allege fraud, fiduciary defalcation, or willful/malicious injury, and (on the record described) is not framed as a domestic-support or divorce-decree-based debt. But Whalen's own certification (quoted in Part IV of this file) ties "contribution" back to "the Mell MSA," which could pull it toward § 523(a)(15) instead of leaving it wholly outside § 523(a). **This is fact-dependent and cannot be resolved in the abstract** — it turns on exactly how the contribution claim is pled and characterized, addressed in Section 5 below.

4. **Core-proceeding/exclusive-jurisdiction landscape:** § 157(b)(2)(I) designates "determinations as to the dischargeability of particular debts" as core. The structural exclusivity of federal jurisdiction over § 523(a)(2)/(4)/(6) determinations (via § 523(c)(1) + Rule 4007(c)) is well recognized, including in persuasive out-of-circuit authority already in this file (*DiGeronimo*, *Tarone*). **However, the specific citation supplied for this proposition — *In re Halper*, 164 F.3d 830 (3d Cir. 1999) — does NOT support it.** That is a different Halper case about guaranty enforceability and fraudulent conveyance, addressing core-vs.-non-core status of *state-law contract claims*, not § 523 dischargeability jurisdiction. This is flagged prominently in Section 3 below — do not cite it for this proposition without correction.

5. **The "stay relief must await a dischargeability determination" theory is not a strong, well-established argument.** No Third Circuit or Bankr. D.N.J. case was located holding that stay relief must be denied or deferred because the underlying debt's dischargeable character is itself disputed. The closest authority — *Sonnax* (2d Cir., persuasive) and *Rexene* (Bankr. D. Del., persuasive but decided within this circuit) — supplies general "cause" factors (judicial economy, interference with the bankruptcy case) from which a *futility*-style argument can be built, but the case law more commonly runs the other way: courts routinely grant *limited* stay relief to liquidate a claim in another forum while expressly reserving dischargeability and collection to the bankruptcy court. The debtor's team should treat this as a **novel argument built from general principles**, not a precedent-backed rule, and should plan a fallback position (conditioned/limited relief) rather than betting everything on outright denial.

## 1. ORDINARY DISCHARGE IS AUTOMATIC — § 727(b) AND § 523(a)

### 1.1 Statutory text (verbatim, confirmed at uscode.house.gov)

**11 U.S.C. § 727(b):**

> "Except as provided in section 523 of this title, a discharge under subsection (a) of this section discharges the debtor from all debts that arose before the date of the order for relief under this chapter, and any liability on a claim that is determined under section 502 of this title as if such claim had arisen before the commencement of the case, whether or not a proof of claim based on any such debt or liability is filed under section 501 of this title, and whether or not a claim based on any such debt or liability is allowed under section 502 of this title."

11 U.S.C. § 727(b) `[CONFIRMED — uscode.house.gov, verbatim]`.

**Analysis:** § 727(b) is self-executing. Upon entry of the Chapter 7 discharge order, *every* prepetition debt is discharged **except** those "provided" for in § 523 — i.e., except a debt that a creditor has successfully placed within one of the enumerated § 523(a) exceptions (and, where required, within the required procedural window). No separate adversary proceeding, motion, or judicial act is needed to discharge an ordinary debt. The discharge is the default; nondischargeability is the thing that must be affirmatively established.

**11 U.S.C. § 523(a)** opens with the discharge-default structure:

> "A discharge under section 727 ... of this title does not discharge an individual debtor from any debt—" [followed by 19 enumerated paragraphs, (1) through (19)]

11 U.S.C. § 523(a) (introductory clause) `[CONFIRMED — uscode.house.gov]`.

### 1.2 Applying this to a joint-tortfeasor contribution claim

A "contribution" claim between former co-defendants in the B.B. lawsuit — Kim having settled and now seeking reimbursement of a share from Brad under state joint-tortfeasor contribution principles — is, at its core, a prepetition, contingent unsecured debt like any other state-law claim (e.g., a breach-of-contract debt, a judgment debt, an ordinary tort debt). Unless Kim/Whalen affirmatively brings it within one of the § 523(a) paragraphs (and, if paragraph (2), (4), or (6), does so by timely adversary complaint), **it is discharged automatically and finally upon entry of the discharge order, exactly like any other ordinary unsecured debt.** This is not a matter of degree or judicial discretion — it is the structural default Congress built into §§ 727(b) and 523(a).

This is fully consistent with — and simply confirms — the analysis already developed in Part I of this file regarding § 523(a)(5)/(a)(15) procedure, and with the *Chaudry* holding already on file: "The whole case turned on it: because the wife's Tevis claim was held to be a § 523(a)(6) debt and no timely adversary was filed, it was discharged." *In re Chaudry*, 569 B.R. 372, 376–77 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2017) (as quoted in Part I of this file).

## 2. WHICH § 523(a) EXCEPTIONS REQUIRE AN ADVERSARY PROCEEDING — AND DOES CONTRIBUTION NATURALLY FIT ANY OF THEM?

### 2.1 The § 523(c)(1) list — confirmed verbatim

**11 U.S.C. § 523(c)(1):**

> "Except as provided in subsection (a)(3)(B) of this section, the debtor shall be discharged from a debt of a kind specified in paragraph (2), (4), or (6) of subsection (a) of this section, unless, on request of the creditor to whom such debt is owed, and after notice and a hearing, the court determines such debt to be excepted from discharge under paragraph (2), (4), or (6), as the case may be, of subsection (a) of this section."

11 U.S.C. § 523(c)(1) `[CONFIRMED — uscode.house.gov, verbatim]`.

**Only paragraphs (2), (4), and (6) are listed.** `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING — the task's framing ("currently (a)(2), (a)(4), (a)(6), and certain others") should be corrected; the current statutory text of § 523(c)(1) itself names only (2), (4), and (6). There is no fourth paragraph inside (c)(1) itself. If "certain others" was meant to reference the household-goods lien-avoidance cross-reference in old Rule 4007(c) drafting history, that is not a discharge exception and should not be conflated with the (c)(1) list.]`

### 2.2 Fed. R. Bankr. P. 4007 — confirmed verbatim

**Rule 4007(b):**

> "A complaint, except one under §523(c), may be filed at any time. If a case is reopened to permit filing the complaint, no fee for reopening is required."

**Rule 4007(c):**

> "Except as (d) provides, a complaint to determine whether a debt is dischargeable under §523(c) must be filed within 60 days after the first date set for the §341(a) meeting of creditors."

Fed. R. Bankr. P. 4007(b), (c) `[CONFIRMED — uscode.house.gov, verbatim]`. Because Rule 4007(c) is keyed to "§523(c)" debts, and § 523(c) itself covers only paragraphs (2), (4), and (6), the 60-day bar applies only to those three exceptions. This is the same structural point already developed and cited in Part I of this file via *Chaudry*, *DiGeronimo* (persuasive), and *Tarone* (persuasive).

### 2.3 Fed. R. Bankr. P. 7001(6) — the vehicle, confirmed

The Rule 7001 list of matters requiring an adversary proceeding (as opposed to a motion) includes, as numbered paragraph (6):

> "a proceeding to determine the dischargeability of a debt"

Fed. R. Bankr. P. 7001(6) `[CONFIRMED — content independently corroborated via multiple sources including the official advisory committee notes at law.cornell.edu, which describe "[p]roceedings initiated pursuant to §523(c) of the Code to determine the dischargeability of a particular debt" as falling under this item; full verbatim numbered-list text should be pulled from a clean PACER/Westlaw copy before filing given intermittent source-access issues encountered in this research session — VERIFY BEFORE FILING]`.

This confirms: any creditor (or debtor) seeking a judicial determination that a debt is *or is not* dischargeable must proceed by adversary complaint, not by motion. For § 523(a)(2)/(4)/(6) claims, that complaint is also time-barred at 60 days from the first § 341 meeting date unless extended under Rule 4007(c)'s procedure. For § 523(a)(5)/(a)(15) claims, the complaint may be filed "at any time," including, as already established in Part I of this file, years after discharge, and including — because jurisdiction is concurrent for those paragraphs — in a non-bankruptcy forum in the first instance.

### 2.4 Does a garden-variety contribution claim naturally fit any § 523(a) exception?

This is the crux, and it must be answered honestly rather than assumed.

**§ 523(a)(2) (fraud):** Nothing in the fact pattern as given alleges that Brad made a false representation, false pretense, or actual fraud in connection with the B.B. lawsuit or its settlement. Absent such an allegation, (a)(2) is not naturally implicated.

**§ 523(a)(4) (fraud/defalcation in a fiduciary capacity, embezzlement, larceny):** A joint-tortfeasor contribution obligation does not, by itself, involve a fiduciary relationship between co-defendants. Unless Whalen alleges Brad owed Kim some express or technical trust-like fiduciary duty independent of ordinary tort liability (unlikely on a bare contribution theory), (a)(4) is not naturally implicated.

**§ 523(a)(6) (willful and malicious injury):** Contribution is a claim for *reimbursement of a shared, joint obligation to a third party (B.B.)* — it is not itself a claim that Brad willfully and maliciously injured Kim. Under *Kawaauhau v. Geiger*, 523 U.S. 57 (1998) (BINDING, SCOTUS), and the Third Circuit's *Conte* purposeful-infliction/subjective-certainty test (BINDING, 3d Cir.), (a)(6) requires the debtor to have desired the *specific injury* to the creditor (Kim) or known it was substantially certain to occur. A contribution claim's theory of liability runs through Brad's alleged conduct toward B.B. (the underlying tort victim), not toward Kim — Kim's claim is derivative/reimbursement-based, not a direct willful-injury claim against her. Absent a separate, specifically pled theory that Brad's conduct toward *Kim* (not B.B.) was willful and malicious, (a)(6) does not naturally fit a bare contribution claim.

**§ 523(a)(15):** This is the closest fit, but it depends entirely on characterization. The statute:

> "(15) to a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor and not of the kind described in paragraph (5) that is incurred by the debtor in the course of a divorce or separation or in connection with a separation agreement, divorce decree or other order of a court of record, or a determination made in accordance with State or territorial law by a governmental unit;"

11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(15) `[CONFIRMED — uscode.house.gov, verbatim]`.

A debt "to a former spouse ... in connection with a separation agreement" fits an *indemnification* claim under MSA § 10.2(b) comfortably — that clause is expressly part of the MSA. It is a materially harder fit for a **"contribution"** claim if that claim is genuinely being asserted as an *independent state-law joint-tortfeasor contribution theory* (e.g., under the state contribution statute governing co-defendants who settle a shared third-party liability), because that theory does not depend on the MSA at all — any co-defendant, related or not, could assert it. On that framing, the debt is "to a former spouse" only incidentally (because the co-defendant happens to be the former spouse), not because it was "incurred ... in connection with a separation agreement."

But the record undercuts a clean separation. Whalen's own certification (quoted in Part IV of this file) states Kim's cross-claims "stem from Debtor Mell's failure to honor the MSA and make the required payments to Kim, **as well as claims for contribution and indemnification, which was agreed upon in the Mell MSA**" — i.e., Whalen herself is characterizing "contribution" as something "agreed upon in the Mell MSA," not as a free-standing joint-tortfeasor doctrine. If that characterization holds, "contribution" collapses into (or overlaps with) the MSA-based indemnification theory and becomes a stronger candidate for (a)(15) treatment (no adversary deadline, concurrent state-court jurisdiction) rather than falling outside § 523(a) altogether.

**Conclusion on Section 2.4:** This cannot be resolved in the abstract on the facts given. Two internally consistent readings compete:

- **Reading A (contribution as independent state-law doctrine):** If "contribution" is truly a free-standing joint-tortfeasor reimbursement claim under state law, untethered to the MSA, it fits *no* § 523(a) exception — not (a)(2)/(4)/(6) (no fraud/fiduciary/willful-injury theory pled), not (a)(15) (not "incurred ... in connection with a separation agreement" — it would exist between any co-defendants). On this reading it is a garden-variety debt, discharged automatically under § 727(b), and any (a)(2)/(4)/(6) theory not raised by adversary complaint within the Rule 4007(c) window is waived as a matter of law.
- **Reading B (contribution as MSA-derived):** If, as Whalen's own certification suggests, "contribution" is pled or provable as something "agreed upon in the Mell MSA" — i.e., functionally indistinguishable from the § 10.2(b) indemnification obligation — it more naturally falls under (a)(15) (debt to a former spouse in connection with a separation agreement), with no adversary deadline and concurrent state-court jurisdiction, exactly as already mapped for the indemnification clause in Part I/Part IV of this file.

**What resolves it:** how Whalen actually pleads and litigates the claim — the legal theory cited (state contribution statute vs. MSA provision), the forum she selects, and whether any stay-relief motion frames the claim as MSA-derived or as independent tort-contribution law. See Section 5 below.

## 3. CORE PROCEEDING / EXCLUSIVE JURISDICTION

### 3.1 § 157(b)(2)(I) — core-proceeding designation, confirmed

**28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2)(I):**

> "determinations as to the dischargeability of particular debts"

is listed among the core proceedings a bankruptcy judge "may hear and determine ... and may enter appropriate orders and judgments, subject to review under section 158." 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(1), (b)(2)(I) `[CONFIRMED — uscode.house.gov, verbatim]`.

This confirms that a dischargeability determination — whichever § 523(a) paragraph is at issue — is, by the statute's own terms, a core proceeding, meaning the bankruptcy court may enter final judgment on it without district-court review as a first instance (subject to ordinary appeal).

### 3.2 Exclusive federal jurisdiction for § 523(a)(2)/(4)/(6) — the structural point, and a citation-accuracy warning

**What the research does support:** The exclusivity of federal jurisdiction over § 523(a)(2)/(4)/(6) determinations is a well-recognized structural consequence of § 523(c)(1) and Rule 4007(c) — because Congress required these three exceptions to be raised by a "request of the creditor" resulting in a court "determin[ation]," and because the discharge is otherwise automatic and final under § 727(b), a debt of this kind can be excepted from discharge *only* by the bankruptcy court making that determination (or by default — the 60-day bar). *In re Tarone*, 434 B.R. 41, 50 (Bankr. E.D.N.Y. 2010) (PERSUASIVE — out of circuit) (as quoted in Part I of this file).

### 3.4 Practical significance for the contribution claim

If the contribution claim is ultimately litigated as an (a)(2)/(4)/(6) theory (Reading A variant where Whalen tries to except it from discharge on a fraud/defalcation/willful-injury theory), **only the bankruptcy court can make that determination**, and it can only do so on a timely Rule 7001(6) adversary complaint filed within the Rule 4007(c) window (already run or about to run — the 60-day clock from the first § 341 date should be confirmed against the actual meeting date in this case; that date is a factual predicate to flag for confirmation, see Section 5). If, instead, the claim is litigated as an (a)(15)-type theory (Reading B), a non-bankruptcy forum is not jurisdictionally barred from reaching the dischargeability question, which materially changes the stay-relief calculus in Section 4 below — because there, at least, "wait for the bankruptcy court to decide dischargeability first" is not a jurisdictional imperative, only (at most) a discretionary preference.

## 4. THE CENTRAL QUESTION: SHOULD STAY RELIEF BE WITHHELD/DEFERRED PENDING A DISCHARGEABILITY DETERMINATION?

### 4.1 The § 362(d)(1) "cause" standard — statutory text

**11 U.S.C. § 362(d)(1):**

> "for cause, including the lack of adequate protection of an interest in property of such party in interest"

11 U.S.C. § 362(d)(1) `[CONFIRMED — uscode.house.gov, verbatim]`. "Cause" is undefined by the Code and is determined case-by-case; "including" signals the list is illustrative, not exhaustive.

### 4.2 Multi-factor tests — what this circuit actually uses

**No single Third Circuit precedential opinion "adopting" a named multi-factor test (Sonnax or otherwise) for § 362(d)(1) "cause" was located in this research session.** `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING — Westlaw/PACER search recommended before representing any single test as "the Third Circuit's adopted test."]` What was found:

**(a) *Sonnax* — persuasive, Second Circuit, widely cited nationally (including, per secondary sources, in some New Jersey bankruptcy opinions, though a specific Bankr. D.N.J. pinpoint citation was not independently confirmed in this session):**

> "(1) whether relief would result in partial or complete issue resolution; (2) lack of connection with or interference with bankruptcy case; (3) whether other proceeding involves debtor as fiduciary; (4) whether specialized tribunal with necessary expertise has been established to hear cause of action; (5) whether debtor's insurer has assumed full responsibility for defending it; (6) whether the action primarily involves third parties; (7) whether litigation in another forum would prejudice the interests of other creditors; (8) whether the judgment claim arising from the other action is subject to equitable subordination; (9) whether movant's success in the other proceeding would result in a judicial lien avoidable by the debtor; (10) the interests of judicial economy and the expeditious and economical resolution of litigation; (11) whether the parties are ready for trial in the other proceeding; and (12) impact of the stay on the parties and the balance of harms."

*In re Sonnax Industries, Inc.*, 907 F.2d 1280, 1286 (2d Cir. 1990) (PERSUASIVE — Second Circuit) `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING — reconstructed from multiple secondary/treatise sources after repeated direct full-text fetch failures in this session (CourtListener and OpenJurist returned empty/403 responses); pull the actual slip opinion from Westlaw/PACER and confirm this twelve-factor list word-for-word, including factor (12)'s exact phrasing, before quoting it in any filing.]` *Sonnax* itself is notable on the facts: the Second Circuit affirmed *denial* of stay relief, crediting the district court's finding that "the balance of harms and judicial economy favored keeping the case in the bankruptcy court" `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING — paraphrase from secondary source, not independently confirmed against slip opinion text]`. This is useful as an example of judicial-economy reasoning cutting against stay relief, but *Sonnax* was a Chapter 11 reorganization case where continued litigation elsewhere threatened to interfere with plan formulation — not a case about the dischargeable character of the underlying debt being unresolved.

**(b) *Rexene* — persuasive, but decided in Bankr. D. Del., i.e., within the Third Circuit (closer in-circuit analog than *Sonnax*):**

> "It will often be more appropriate to permit proceedings to continue in their place of origin, when no great prejudice to the bankruptcy estate would result, in order to leave the parties to their chosen forum and to relieve the bankruptcy court from any duties that may be handled elsewhere."

*Izzarelli v. Rexene Prods. Co. (In re Rexene Prods. Co.)*, 141 B.R. 574, 576 (Bankr. D. Del. 1992) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D. Del., within the Third Circuit) `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING — quoted from secondary-source reconstruction; confirm against Leagle/Westlaw full text]`. *Rexene*'s three-factor test is commonly summarized as: (i) whether continuation of the other litigation would cause great prejudice to the estate or debtor; (ii) whether the hardship to the movant from continued stay considerably outweighs the hardship to the debtor; and (iii) whether the movant has a probability of prevailing on the merits of the other litigation. `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING]`

**(c) Standard of appellate review — confirmed direction, not fully pinned down:** Secondary-source material indicates the Third Circuit reviews a bankruptcy court's denial (or grant) of stay relief for abuse of discretion, citing *In re Stone Resources, Inc.* (3d Cir.) `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING — full citation and precedential status not independently confirmed in this session]`.

### 4.3 Is there authority for "decide dischargeability first" as a matter of "cause"?

**No case was found — in the Third Circuit, in Bankr. D.N.J., or in the broader search performed — squarely holding that a bankruptcy court should or must resolve dischargeability (or require it be raised by adversary complaint) before granting a creditor stay relief to liquidate a claim elsewhere.** This is a real gap, not merely an unexplored corner: multiple targeted searches for "stay relief," "dischargeability unresolved," "futility," "judgment-proof," and "must decide first" in combination returned no on-point authority.

What the research *does* show, and what cuts in different directions:

**Factors that could support the argument, built from general principles rather than direct holding:**
- *Sonnax* factor (2) ("lack of connection with or interference with the bankruptcy case") and factor (10) ("interests of judicial economy") are the natural doctrinal hooks. If a claim's *entire* legal viability as a collectible debt depends on a threshold characterization question (dischargeable vs. not) that only the bankruptcy court can authoritatively resolve for the (a)(2)/(4)/(6) branch (see Section 3.2 above — exclusive jurisdiction), there is a logical argument that permitting years of state-court/family-court litigation over liability and damages, before that threshold question is even framed, is an inefficient use of judicial resources and risks producing a judgment that the bankruptcy court could render moot or worthless.
- Because § 157(b)(2)(I) makes dischargeability determinations core, and because (on Reading A in Section 2.4) an (a)(2)/(4)/(6) theory can be litigated *only* in the bankruptcy court, there is a structural argument that stay relief to litigate the *liability and amount* of a contribution claim in another forum, without first pinning down which dischargeability track applies, invites a second, potentially wasted round of litigation in the bankruptcy court regardless of the state-court outcome.

**Authority and practice that cuts the other way, and must be disclosed:**
- Courts frequently grant *limited* stay relief — to liquidate a claim's existence and amount in a pending or more appropriate forum — while expressly withholding permission to *collect* or *enforce* any resulting judgment, and expressly reserving the dischargeability question to the bankruptcy court (or to whatever forum has jurisdiction over it under Section 3 above). This is a common, unremarkable pattern, particularly where a specialized tribunal or a case nearer trial exists — precisely *Sonnax* factors (1) and (4) and (11). On this practice, "unresolved dischargeability" is not a reason to withhold stay relief outright; it is a reason to *condition* it.
- One secondary source located in this session states plainly: "nowhere does Section 362 limit the automatic stay to nondischargeable claims" `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING — secondary source paraphrase]` — i.e., courts have rejected the inverse argument (that a claim's apparent nondischargeability justifies ignoring the stay). The logical corollary cuts against the debtor's theory too: a claim's *dischargeable* character does not, by itself, convert "cause" analysis into a discharge-only inquiry; "cause" under § 362(d)(1) is a distinct, multi-factor test that does not on its face incorporate a dischargeability gate.
- To the extent Kim's contribution claim would need to be liquidated for purposes of a distribution in this bankruptcy case, the ordinary vehicle for that is the § 502 claims-allowance process *within* the bankruptcy case itself (proof of claim, objection, if necessary a contested matter or estimation under § 502(c)) — not litigation in an outside forum. This actually strengthens a *narrower*, more defensible version of the debtor's argument: not "the court must decide dischargeability before anyone can litigate anything," but rather "there is no need for *stay relief to a non-bankruptcy forum* at all, because (i) if Whalen wants to except the debt from discharge under (a)(2)/(4)/(6), her sole vehicle is a Rule 7001(6) adversary complaint in *this* court (Section 3.2 — exclusive jurisdiction), not a motion for stay relief to litigate in family court or state court; and (ii) if the claim is a garden-variety dischargeable debt, it can be handled (if it needs to be handled at all) through the ordinary § 502 claims process in this case, again without need for relief from the stay to litigate it elsewhere." This reframing does not require finding a case squarely on point — it follows from combining §§ 502, 523(c)(1), 727(b), and Rule 7001(6), which are individually well-established.

**Honest bottom line on Section 4.3:** The debtor's theory as framed in the task ("stay relief must await a dischargeability determination") is **not a strong, precedent-backed argument** in this circuit or district on the authority located. It is, at best, a colorable argument assembled from (i) general "cause" factors that happen to include judicial economy and interference with the bankruptcy case, and (ii) the structural exclusive-jurisdiction point in Section 3, combined into an inference that a court has not been shown to have actually drawn in a reported opinion. The more defensible, better-supported position is the narrower one: relief from stay is unnecessary (and therefore "cause" is not shown, or is affirmatively negated) to the extent the claim can and should proceed, if at all, through the mechanisms Congress specifically built for it inside the bankruptcy case — the § 502 claims process for an ordinary dischargeable debt, or a Rule 7001(6) adversary complaint in the bankruptcy court itself for an (a)(2)/(4)/(6) theory. That argument does not require, and should not be oversold as requiring, an outright rule that dischargeability must always be decided first.

### 4.4 Futility as a factor

No case was located expressly listing "futility because the debt will be discharged and the judgment will be worthless" as a named factor in either the *Sonnax* or *Rexene* formulations, and no Third Circuit or Bankr. D.N.J. case was found applying such a factor. `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING]` The concept is not absent from bankruptcy practice generally — parties and courts routinely observe that litigating a purely dischargeable, no-asset-case debt to judgment elsewhere serves little purpose *for collection* — but this observation was not found reduced to an articulated "cause" factor in reported authority located in this session. Where it *does* surface doctrinally is as an input to *Rexene* factor (i) (prejudice to the estate/debtor) and *Sonnax* factor (10) (judicial economy): a debtor can argue that requiring the estate (or the debtor's post-discharge fresh start) to bear the cost, distraction, and reputational burden of foreign-forum litigation over a debt that will, on the debtor's view of the law, be discharged regardless of outcome, is itself a form of prejudice and inefficiency relevant to those existing factors — again, an argument built from the factors, not a freestanding rule.

## 5. PRACTICAL FRAMING

### 5.1 Strongest supportable one-paragraph statement of the argument

> Kim Mell's asserted "contribution" claim is, on its face, an ordinary prepetition unsecured debt that will be discharged automatically under 11 U.S.C. § 727(b) unless she affirmatively brings it within a § 523(a) exception — and if her theory is fraud, fiduciary defalcation, or willful and malicious injury under § 523(a)(2), (4), or (6), the only forum with jurisdiction to except it from discharge is this Court, by timely adversary complaint under Fed. R. Bankr. P. 7001(6) and 4007(c), not a motion for relief from the automatic stay to litigate liability and damages in a non-bankruptcy forum. Granting stay relief to pursue a claim of contested and unresolved dischargeable character, before any such adversary complaint has been filed or any characterization has been made, risks precisely the inefficiency the "cause" factors under § 362(d)(1) are meant to guard against — duplicative litigation whose result may be advisory at best if the debt is ultimately (and by default) discharged. At minimum, the Court should require Whalen to specify the legal and factual basis on which she contends the contribution claim survives discharge before authorizing litigation of it elsewhere, and any relief granted should be limited to liquidating the claim's existence and amount, with collection and dischargeability expressly reserved to this Court.

### 5.2 Honest caveats and weaknesses

- **No case says this.** As developed in Section 4, this argument is assembled from general "cause" factors and structural jurisdictional principles; it is not a rule any located Third Circuit or Bankr. D.N.J. opinion has articulated. Opposing counsel can fairly respond that "cause" is a flexible, multi-factor, non-exhaustive standard that does not require pre-resolving dischargeability, and that courts routinely grant liquidation-only stay relief precisely to avoid the delay this argument would impose.
- **The (a)(15) risk.** If Whalen's "contribution" theory is, or can be recharacterized as, MSA-derived (Reading B in Section 2.4), the exclusive-jurisdiction predicate in Section 3.2 does not apply — (a)(15) determinations are not confined to this Court, undercutting the "only this Court can decide it" premise of the argument.
- **This is a Chapter 7, not Chapter 11/13, case.** The judicial-economy and claims-process arguments in Section 4.3 are strongest where there is an active claims-allowance process the debtor can point to as the "proper channel." Confirm the asset/no-asset status and whether a bar date and claims process are actually in motion in this case (Part V/Schedule research in this file indicates a disputed asset case with significant non-exempt value, so a claims process is plausible — confirm current docket status).
- **Best realistic outcome may be conditioned relief, not denial.** Given the weight of practice described in Section 4.3 (routine grants of liquidation-only relief), debtor's counsel should be prepared for the Court to grant limited stay relief (liquidate only, no collection, dischargeability reserved) rather than deny relief outright, and should affirmatively propose that conditioned form of order as a fallback.

### 5.3 What we need to establish factually before this argument can be made with confidence

1. **How exactly does Whalen plead/characterize the "contribution" claim?** Specifically: does she cite a state contribution statute (independent of the MSA) or does she (consistent with her certification language already on file) describe it as "agreed upon in the Mell MSA"? This single characterization question drives the Section 2.4 analysis and therefore the Section 3.2/4.3 jurisdictional-channel argument.
2. **Is there an actual stay-relief motion on file yet?** The task states Whalen is "reportedly seeking" relief — confirm whether a motion has actually been filed, and if so, obtain and review it in full: what relief is requested (liquidate only vs. liquidate-and-collect), what forum is named (family court, law division, the B.B. lawsuit itself), what legal basis for "cause" is argued, and whether any § 523(a) paragraph is invoked or implied.
3. **Where does Whalen intend to pursue the claim?** A motion seeking to return to the B.B. lawsuit's forum (a law/civil forum, presumably not the Family Part) is postured differently than a motion seeking to litigate contribution as an ancillary matter in the matrimonial Family Part docket — the latter is more naturally read as MSA-adjacent (again pushing toward Reading B/§ 523(a)(15)).
4. **The § 341 meeting date and whether the Rule 4007(c) 60-day window has already run.** If it has run without an (a)(2)/(4)/(6) adversary complaint on file, the exclusive-jurisdiction/(a)(2)(4)(6) branch of the argument disappears (that route is already foreclosed by operation of law) and the entire dispute collapses into: is this an (a)(15)-type debt (concurrent jurisdiction, no bar date) or a purely dischargeable ordinary debt (discharged, full stop)? That materially changes which version of this memo's argument is available.
5. **Asset/claims-process status of the Chapter 7 case** (trustee's asset/no-asset determination, bar date if any) — needed to make the § 502 claims-process alternative in Section 4.3 concrete rather than theoretical.
