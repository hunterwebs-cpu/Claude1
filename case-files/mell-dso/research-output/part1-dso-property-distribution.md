# ATTORNEY WORK PRODUCT — PRIVILEGED AND CONFIDENTIAL

## TRACK 1 RESEARCH MEMORANDUM

**Subject:** Whether property-distribution / equitable-distribution obligations arising under a Marriage Settlement Agreement (MSA) or Property Settlement Agreement (PSA) qualify as a "domestic support obligation" under 11 U.S.C. § 101(14A) and are therefore nondischargeable under § 523(a)(5), versus being § 523(a)(15) "property settlement" debts

**Controlling circuit:** Third Circuit — anticipated forum Bankr. D.N.J.
**Posture:** PRELIMINARY / EDUCATIONAL. **No case facts have been supplied and none are assumed.** Every application section below is expressly hypothetical and flags the facts that would be needed to resolve it.
**Date of research:** August 11, 2026

---

## 0. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY — THE BOTTOM LINE IN NINE POINTS

1. **The controlling test is *Gianakas*.** *Gianakas v. Gianakas (In re Gianakas)*, 917 F.2d 759, 762–63 (3d Cir. 1990) (BINDING) supplies the Third Circuit's three-indicator test for whether a marital obligation is "in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support." It has never been overruled and it remains the operative test post-BAPCPA.

2. **BAPCPA did not change the "nature of support" standard.** It moved the operative language from old § 523(a)(5)(B) into new § 101(14A)(B), but the phrase "in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support ... without regard to whether such debt is expressly so designated" is materially identical. Courts in this District have expressly so held. *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. 782, 786 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2011).

3. **Federal law governs; the label does not control — but the label is evidence.** *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762 ("a question of federal, not state, law"; "the court must look beyond the label"). At the same time, the label is the *first* thing the first *Gianakas* indicator looks at, and courts in this Circuit have called it "[t]he most compelling indication" where it is corroborated by structural features. *In re Miller*, 501 B.R. 266, 282–83 (Bankr. E.D. Pa. 2013).

4. **The default rule for equitable distribution is § 523(a)(15), not § 523(a)(5).** *In re Clouse*, 446 B.R. 690, 707 n.58 (Bankr. E.D. Pa. 2010) ("Equitable distribution is not included within the definition of 'domestic support obligations.'"); *Berse v. Langman (In re Langman)*, 465 B.R. 395, 405 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2012); *Maldonado v. Sanabria (In re Sanabria)*, 275 B.R. 204, 207–08 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2002); *In re Chaudry*, 569 B.R. 372, 376 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2017). The Third Circuit itself has said, albeit in a passage resting partly on party concession, that "equitable distribution claims are nondischargeable in Chapter 7" under § 523(a)(15), and recorded the creditor-spouse's concession that such a claim "is not entitled to priority as a domestic support obligation." *In re Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d 647, 649 (3d Cir. 2014).

5. **In Chapter 7 both are nondischargeable — but the distinction is not academic.** The single largest practical consequence in Chapter 7 is § 522(c)(1): **exempt property remains liable for a § 523(a)(5) DSO but is shielded from a § 523(a)(15) property-settlement debt.** Priority under § 507(a)(1)(A) matters only in an asset case. Chapter 13 consequences (§ 1328(a)(2)) matter only on conversion or a later filing.

6. **Procedure is the sleeper issue and it cuts *against* the debtor.** Neither § 523(a)(5) nor § 523(a)(15) appears in § 523(c)(1). Therefore neither is subject to the 60-day deadline of Fed. R. Bankr. P. 4007(c); both may be raised "at any time" under Rule 4007(b), including years post-discharge and including in state court. *In re Chaudry*, 569 B.R. 372, 374 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2017). This is a change from pre-BAPCPA law, under which (a)(15) *was* in § 523(c)(1) and *was* time-barred. Contrast *In re DiGeronimo*, 354 B.R. 625, 633–35 (Bankr. E.D.N.Y. 2006) (pre-BAPCPA case; (a)(15) claim time-barred).

7. **Burden is on the creditor-spouse, at preponderance.** *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 761; *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 287–88 (1991); *In re Langman*, 465 B.R. at 403.

8. **The creditor-spouse's best counter-arguments are real and must be taken seriously.** They are (i) the statutory text of § 101(14A)(C)(i), which expressly lists a "property settlement agreement" as a *source* of a DSO; (ii) § 101(14A)(B)'s "without regard to whether such debt is expressly so designated"; (iii) *Gianakas* itself, which held a mortgage obligation sitting in the property-transfer portion of a settlement agreement to be support; and (iv) the *Gianakas*/*Buccino* observation that "property division often achieves the same goal as alimony, i.e., support," 917 F.2d at 763.

9. **There is no precedential post-BAPCPA Third Circuit opinion applying *Gianakas* to § 101(14A).** A CourtListener search of Third Circuit opinions filed after October 17, 2005 citing *Gianakas* returns no domestic-relations decision. The post-BAPCPA authority applying *Gianakas* to § 101(14A) is all bankruptcy-court-level within the Circuit. This is worth knowing before a brief asserts otherwise.

# PART I — THE STATUTORY ARCHITECTURE

## I.A. 11 U.S.C. § 101(14A) — verbatim (current text)

Source: official U.S. Code, verified against uscode.house.gov and Cornell LII (retrieved Aug. 11, 2026). **CONFIRMED.**

> **(14A)** The term "domestic support obligation" means a debt that accrues before, on, or after the date of the order for relief in a case under this title, including interest that accrues on that debt as provided under applicable nonbankruptcy law notwithstanding any other provision of this title, that is—
>
> **(A)** owed to or recoverable by—
> &nbsp;&nbsp;**(i)** a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor or such child's parent, legal guardian, or responsible relative; or
> &nbsp;&nbsp;**(ii)** a governmental unit;
>
> **(B)** in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support (including assistance provided by a governmental unit) of such spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor or such child's parent, without regard to whether such debt is expressly so designated;
>
> **(C)** established or subject to establishment before, on, or after the date of the order for relief in a case under this title, by reason of applicable provisions of—
> &nbsp;&nbsp;**(i)** a separation agreement, divorce decree, or property settlement agreement;
> &nbsp;&nbsp;**(ii)** an order of a court of record; or
> &nbsp;&nbsp;**(iii)** a determination made in accordance with applicable nonbankruptcy law by a governmental unit; and
>
> **(D)** not assigned to a nongovernmental entity, unless that obligation is assigned voluntarily by the spouse, former spouse, child of the debtor, or such child's parent, legal guardian, or responsible relative for the purpose of collecting the debt.

**Four-element framework (as stated by this District):**

> "Thus, the elements that must be satisfied for a domestic support obligation to arise are as follows: (i) the payee of the obligation must be either a governmental unit or a person with a particular relationship to the debtor or a child of the debtor; (ii) the nature of the obligation must be support; (iii) the source of the obligation must be an agreement, court order, or other determination; and (iv) the assignment status of the obligation must be consistent with paragraph (D)."

*In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. 782, 786 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2011) (Kaplan, J.) (PERSUASIVE — same court as anticipated forum; adopted verbatim in *In re Langman*, 465 B.R. 395, 404 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2012)).

**CRITICAL DRAFTING NOTE — the trap in subparagraph (C):** § 101(14A)(C)(i) lists "a separation agreement, divorce decree, or **property settlement agreement**" as permissible *sources* of a DSO. This does **not** mean that obligations created by a PSA are DSOs. Subparagraph (C) is a *source* element; subparagraph (B) is the *nature* element. The conjunction "and" at the end of (C) means all four elements must be satisfied independently. A creditor-spouse who argues "the statute says property settlement agreement, therefore my PSA obligation is a DSO" has collapsed (C) into (B). Expect this argument; it is the single most common textual overreach in this area.

## I.B. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(5) — verbatim (current text)

> **(a)** A discharge under section 727, 1141, 1192, 1228(a), 1228(b), or 1328(b) of this title does not discharge an individual debtor from any debt—
> ...
> **(5)** for a domestic support obligation;

**CONFIRMED** (uscode.house.gov / Cornell LII, Aug. 11, 2026).

## I.C. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(15) — verbatim (current text)

> **(15)** to a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor and not of the kind described in paragraph (5) that is incurred by the debtor in the course of a divorce or separation or in connection with a separation agreement, divorce decree or other order of a court of record, or a determination made in accordance with State or territorial law by a governmental unit;

**CONFIRMED** (uscode.house.gov / Cornell LII, Aug. 11, 2026).

Note the structural relationship: (a)(15) is defined **residually** — "not of the kind described in paragraph (5)." The two paragraphs are mutually exclusive. A debt cannot be both. This matters because a creditor-spouse pleading both in the alternative is pleading in the alternative, not cumulatively; and because a finding that a debt *is* a DSO removes it from (a)(15) entirely (with all the § 522(c)(1) and § 507(a)(1) consequences that follow), and vice versa.

## I.D. The BAPCPA 2005 restructuring

### I.D.1. Pre-BAPCPA § 523(a)(5) — verbatim

Source: **official U.S. Code, 2000 edition**, govinfo.gov, USCODE-2000-title11-chap5-subchapII-sec523. **CONFIRMED.**

> **(5)** to a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor, for alimony to, maintenance for, or support of such spouse or child, in connection with a separation agreement, divorce decree or other order of a court of record, determination made in accordance with State or territorial law by a governmental unit, or property settlement agreement, but not to the extent that—
> **(A)** such debt is assigned to another entity, voluntarily, by operation of law, or otherwise (other than debts assigned pursuant to section 408(a)(3) of the Social Security Act, or any such debt which has been assigned to the Federal Government or to a State or any political subdivision of such State); or
> **(B)** such debt includes a liability designated as alimony, maintenance, or support, unless such liability is actually in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support.

This is the text *Gianakas* construed. Compare it to current § 101(14A)(B). Old (a)(5)(B)'s "designated as alimony, maintenance, or support, unless such liability is actually in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support" became new § 101(14A)(B)'s "in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support ... without regard to whether such debt is expressly so designated." The substantive command — **look past the label to the actual nature** — is identical, and arguably broader now, because the current text is symmetrical (it disregards designation in *both* directions, whereas the old text was drafted asymmetrically as a limit on debts *designated* as support).

### I.D.2. Pre-BAPCPA § 523(a)(15) — verbatim

Source: **official U.S. Code, 2000 edition**, govinfo.gov. **CONFIRMED.**

> **(15)** not of the kind described in paragraph (5) that is incurred by the debtor in the course of a divorce or separation or in connection with a separation agreement, divorce decree or other order of a court of record, a determination made in accordance with State or territorial law by a governmental unit unless—
> **(A)** the debtor does not have the ability to pay such debt from income or property of the debtor not reasonably necessary to be expended for the maintenance or support of the debtor or a dependent of the debtor and, if the debtor is engaged in a business, for the payment of expenditures necessary for the continuation, preservation, and operation of such business; or
> **(B)** discharging such debt would result in a benefit to the debtor that outweighs the detrimental consequences to a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor.

> "First, BAPCPA eliminated two balancing tests that had formerly provided debtors with defenses to nondischargeability for non-support debts based on (1) whether the debtor would be able to pay the debt and (2) whether discharging the debt would result in a benefit to the debtor that would outweigh the detrimental consequences to the former spouse or child."

> "[A]s acknowledged by the parties, under the changes implemented by the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005 ('BAPCPA'), Pub.L. No. 109-8, § 215(3), 119 Stat. 23 (2005), equitable distribution claims are nondischargeable in Chapter 7."

*In re Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d 647, 649 (3d Cir. 2014) (Ambro, J.) (**BINDING** — precedential Third Circuit). *Caveat*: the phrase "as acknowledged by the parties" signals that this rested in part on concession; it is nonetheless a published statement of the Court of Appeals on the operation of § 523(a)(15) post-BAPCPA and is quotable.

# PART II — WHY THE § 523(a)(5) / § 523(a)(15) DISTINCTION STILL MATTERS

Both are nondischargeable in Chapter 7. Post-BAPCPA courts sometimes say the distinction is "of no practical consequence" in Chapter 7. That statement is over-broad and, in an asset case or an exemption fight, flatly wrong. Here is the precise accounting.

## II.A. The over-broad statement, and why it must be qualified

> "In individual Chapter 7 and 11 cases, the distinction between a domestic support obligation and other types of obligations arising out of a marital relationship is of no practical consequence in determining the dischargeability of the debt...."

*In re Golio*, 393 B.R. 56, 61 (Bankr. E.D.N.Y. 2008), quoted in *In re Langman*, 465 B.R. at 406 (PERSUASIVE, out-of-circuit as to *Golio*; quoted approvingly by Bankr. D.N.J.).

Read the qualifier: "**in determining the dischargeability of the debt**." *Golio* says the distinction does not change the *dischargeability outcome*. It does not say the distinction is irrelevant to priority, to exempt-property liability, to stay analysis, to trustee duties, or to Chapter 13 treatment. Do not let the sentence be quoted without its qualifier.

The Third Circuit's own framing is consistent with this narrower reading — in *Ruitenberg* the Court treated the DSO/non-DSO line as dispositive of *priority* even while both categories were nondischargeable:

> "(Candace initially sought priority status for the Claim under 11 U.S.C. § 507(a)(1)(A) and (B), but later conceded that the Claim, even if pre-petition, is not entitled to priority as a domestic support obligation.)"

*In re Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d 647, 649 (3d Cir. 2014) (**BINDING**).

## II.B. (a) Priority — § 507(a)(1)(A) vs. § 507(a)(1)(B)

**Verbatim text (CONFIRMED):**

> "(a) The following expenses and claims have priority in the following order:
> **(1) First:**
> **(A)** Allowed unsecured claims for domestic support obligations that, as of the date of the filing of the petition in a case under this title, are owed to or recoverable by a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor, or such child's parent, legal guardian, or responsible relative, without regard to whether the claim is filed by such person or is filed by a governmental unit on behalf of such person, on the condition that funds received under this paragraph by a governmental unit under this title after the date of the filing of the petition shall be applied and distributed in accordance with applicable nonbankruptcy law.
> **(B)** Subject to claims under subparagraph (A), allowed unsecured claims for domestic support obligations that, as of the date of the filing of the petition, are assigned by a spouse, former spouse, child of the debtor, or such child's parent, legal guardian, or responsible relative to a governmental unit (unless such obligation is assigned voluntarily by the spouse, former spouse, child, parent, legal guardian, or responsible relative of the child for the purpose of collecting the debt) or are owed directly to or recoverable by a governmental unit under applicable nonbankruptcy law, on the condition that funds received under this paragraph by a governmental unit under this title after the date of the filing of the petition be applied and distributed in accordance with applicable nonbankruptcy law.
> **(C)** If a trustee is appointed or elected under section 701, 702, 703, 1104, 1202, or 1302, the administrative expenses of the trustee allowed under paragraphs (1)(A), (2), and (6) of section 503(b) shall be paid before payment of claims under subparagraphs (A) and (B), to the extent that the trustee administers assets that are otherwise available for the payment of such claims."

**Precision point, and a correction to the framing of the research question.** The § 507(a)(1)(A) vs. (a)(1)(B) split is **not** the (a)(5)/(a)(15) split. Both (A) and (B) are DSO subcategories: (A) is for DSOs held by the private payee (spouse/former spouse/child/etc.), (B) is for DSOs assigned to or owed directly to a *governmental unit*. The distinction between them matters in a case with a state IV-D agency or Probation Division claim, not in a private MSA dispute.

**The distinction that actually matters for this track is DSO vs. no-DSO:**
- A § 523(a)(5) DSO owed to the former spouse → **first priority**, § 507(a)(1)(A).
- A § 523(a)(15) property-settlement debt → **no priority at all**; general unsecured, § 726(a)(2).

**Does this matter in Chapter 7?** *Only in an asset case.* In a no-asset Chapter 7 there is nothing to distribute and priority is a nullity. In an asset case it is decisive: a first-priority DSO claim is paid in full (after § 507(a)(1)(C) trustee administrative expenses) before any general unsecured creditor sees a dollar, while a § 523(a)(15) claim shares pro rata. Illustration in this District: *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. 782, 787 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2011) (reclassifying condominium-fee obligation from priority DSO to "general unsecured claim").

## II.C. (b) Chapter 13 — § 1328(a)(2)

**Verbatim text (CONFIRMED):**

> "(a) ... the court shall grant the debtor a discharge of all debts provided for by the plan or disallowed under section 502 of this title, except any debt—
> (1) provided for under section 1322(b)(5);
> **(2) of the kind specified in section 507(a)(8)(C) or in paragraph (1)(B), (1)(C), (2), (3), (4), (5), (8), or (9) of section 523(a);**
> ..."

Paragraph **(5)** is listed. Paragraph **(15)** is **not**. Therefore in a completed Chapter 13, a § 523(a)(5) DSO survives discharge and a § 523(a)(15) property-settlement debt is **discharged**. This is the sharpest consequence of the distinction anywhere in the Code.

Judicial statement (PERSUASIVE, out-of-circuit but a clean articulation):

> "A debt for a domestic support obligation is 'never dischargeable' while a debt arising out of divorce under § 523(a)(15) is dischargeable in Chapter 13 bankruptcy."

*Edwards v. Colin (In re Colin)*, 556 B.R. 520, 523–24 (Bankr. N.D. Ala. 2016), quoting *Edwards v. Colin (In re Colin)*, 546 B.R. 455, 458 (Bankr. N.D. Ala. 2016) (PERSUASIVE — N.D. Ala.; cited here for the proposition only, not as controlling).

**Does this matter in Chapter 7?** Not directly. It matters (i) if the case converts to Chapter 13 under § 706(a), (ii) if the case is dismissed and refiled under Chapter 13, (iii) if the debtor's counsel is weighing chapter choice at all, and (iv) as a policy backdrop the court is aware of when the characterization is close. In a Chapter 7 that stays a Chapter 7, § 1328(a)(2) is inert.

## II.D. (c) Automatic stay — § 362(b)(2)

**Verbatim text (CONFIRMED):**

> "(b) The filing of a petition under section 301, 302, or 303 of this title ... does not operate as a stay—
> ...
> **(2) under subsection (a)—**
> **(A)** of the commencement or continuation of a civil action or proceeding—
> &nbsp;&nbsp;(i) for the establishment of paternity;
> &nbsp;&nbsp;**(ii) for the establishment or modification of an order for domestic support obligations;**
> &nbsp;&nbsp;(iii) concerning child custody or visitation;
> &nbsp;&nbsp;**(iv) for the dissolution of a marriage, except to the extent that such proceeding seeks to determine the division of property that is property of the estate;** or
> &nbsp;&nbsp;(v) regarding domestic violence;
> **(B) of the collection of a domestic support obligation from property that is not property of the estate;**
> **(C)** with respect to the withholding of income that is property of the estate or property of the debtor for payment of a domestic support obligation under a judicial or administrative order or a statute;
> **(D)** of the withholding, suspension, or restriction of a driver's license, a professional or occupational license, or a recreational license, under State law, as specified in section 466(a)(16) of the Social Security Act;
> **(E)** of the reporting of overdue support owed by a parent to any consumer reporting agency as specified in section 466(a)(7) of the Social Security Act;
> **(F)** of the interception of a tax refund, as specified in sections 464 and 466(a)(3) of the Social Security Act or under an analogous State law; or
> **(G)** of the enforcement of a medical obligation, as specified under title IV of the Social Security Act;"

**This matters enormously in Chapter 7, and it is the provision most likely to be litigated first.** Note three things:

1. **§ 362(b)(2)(A)(iv) contains an express carve-*back* for property division.** A matrimonial proceeding may proceed unstayed *for dissolution*, "except to the extent that such proceeding seeks to determine the division of property that is property of the estate." So a state-court proceeding to fix or enforce an equitable-distribution award against estate property **is stayed**. A proceeding to establish or modify a DSO **is not**.

2. **§ 362(b)(2)(B) permits DSO collection only from non-estate property.** DSO collection from estate property remains stayed.

3. **§ 362(b)(2)(C) is broader than (B)** and permits income withholding for a DSO even from property of the estate.

Best in-circuit exposition:

> "Under subparagraph (A), determinations regarding the existence and amount of a DSO are not subject to the stay. Under subparagraph (B), the actual collection of a DSO is not stayed, but there is an important qualification. Collection is not stayed only if the DSO is collected from a source other than property of the bankruptcy estate. Under subparagraph (C), the 'withholding of income that is property of the estate or property of the debtor' for payment of a [DSO] 'under a judicial or administrative order or a statute' is not subject to the automatic stay."

*In re Miller*, 501 B.R. 266, 278–79 (Bankr. E.D. Pa. 2013) (Frank, J.) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. E.D. Pa., within the Third Circuit; a careful and frequently cited treatment).

Original *Gianakas* framing of the same point under the pre-BAPCPA text (BINDING):

> "Concomitantly, '[t]he filing of a petition ... [in bankruptcy] does not operate as a stay ... of the collection of alimony, maintenance, or support from property that is not property of the estate.' 11 U.S.C. Sec. 362(b)(2) (1988). These provisions reflect the congressional preference for the rights of spouses to alimony, maintenance or support over the rights of debtors to a 'fresh start' free of debts."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 761.

**Practical upshot for this track:** if the obligation is characterized as equitable distribution rather than DSO, § 362(b)(2) provides **no** exception, and any state-court enforcement against estate property (or, post-discharge, against the debtor personally without a nondischargeability determination) is exposed. If it is characterized as a DSO, the creditor-spouse gets three separate stay exceptions.

## II.E. (d) The § 522(c)(1) exemption shield — *the biggest Chapter 7 consequence*

**Verbatim text (CONFIRMED):**

> "(c) Unless the case is dismissed, property exempted under this section is not liable during or after the case for any debt of the debtor that arose, or that is determined under section 502 of this title as if such debt had arisen, before the commencement of the case, except—
> **(1) a debt of a kind specified in paragraph (1) or (5) of section 523(a)** (in which case, notwithstanding any provision of applicable nonbankruptcy law to the contrary, such property shall be liable for a debt of a kind specified in such paragraph);
> (2) a debt secured by a lien that is— ...
> (3) a debt of a kind specified in section 523(a)(4) or 523(a)(6) ... owed by an institution-affiliated party of an insured depository institution to a Federal depository institutions regulatory agency ...; or
> (4) a debt in connection with fraud in the obtaining or providing of any scholarship, grant, loan, tuition, discount, award, or other financial assistance ..."

**Paragraph (5) is listed. Paragraph (15) is not.** Therefore:

- **§ 523(a)(5) DSO** → the debtor's **exempt property is liable**. Homestead exemption, retirement carve-outs beyond ERISA-qualified plans, wildcard exemptions — all reachable.
- **§ 523(a)(15) property-settlement debt** → nondischargeable as a personal liability, but **exempt property is immune**. The creditor-spouse can pursue post-petition wages and after-acquired assets, but not the exempt homestead equity.

Supporting authority (PERSUASIVE, out-of-circuit but directly on point):

> "That section provides, in part, that exempt property, although not liable for certain prepetition debts of the debtor, is liable for debts of the kind specified in § 523(a)(5), i.e., domestic support obligations. 11 U.S.C. §§ 522(c)(1), 523(a)(5)."

*Tarone v. Tarone (In re Tarone)*, 434 B.R. 41, 50 (Bankr. E.D.N.Y. 2010) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. E.D.N.Y.).

**This is the argument to lead with in a Chapter 7 case if the debtor has meaningful exempt equity.** It converts an apparently abstract labeling dispute into a concrete dollars-and-cents fight over the homestead.

## II.F. (e) Trustee, collection, and ancillary consequences

**(1) Trustee notice duty — Chapter 7 specific.** 11 U.S.C. § 704(a)(10): the trustee shall, "if with respect to the debtor there is a claim for a domestic support obligation, provide the applicable notice specified in subsection (c)." § 704(c) prescribes a two-part notice to the DSO claimant and to the State child support enforcement agency. **No analogous duty exists for a § 523(a)(15) claim.** A DSO characterization therefore triggers affirmative trustee obligations and, practically, agency attention.

**(2) Preference safe harbor.** 11 U.S.C. § 547(c)(7): the trustee may not avoid a transfer "to the extent such transfer was a bona fide payment of a debt for a domestic support obligation." Pre-petition payments on an *equitable distribution* obligation enjoy **no** such safe harbor and are exposed to preference analysis under § 547(b) if made within the reach-back period to a non-insider (90 days) or insider (1 year). This is a live, and frequently overlooked, downside to the creditor-spouse of *losing* the DSO argument — and a live risk to a creditor-spouse who received installment payments pre-petition.

**(3) Post-petition interest.** § 101(14A) expressly includes "interest that accrues on that debt as provided under applicable nonbankruptcy law notwithstanding any other provision of this title." In this District, that clause has been held to import New Jersey's post-judgment interest rules and to entitle a DSO creditor to post-petition interest on pre-petition arrears. *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. 782, 788–89 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2011) (applying N.J. Ct. R. 5:7-5(g) and N.J.S.A. 2A:17-56.23a to child-support arrears; declining to award interest on alimony where the state court had not exercised its discretion under R. 5:7-5(a)). A § 523(a)(15) debt gets no comparable statutory interest hook.

**(4) § 707(b)(3) means-test and dismissal ancillaries.** § 707(a)(3) (added by BAPCPA) bars dismissal of a Chapter 7 filed by a violent-crime convict where "the filing of a case under this chapter is necessary to satisfy a claim for a domestic support obligation." Rarely applicable, but it confirms the Code's pattern of treating DSOs as a favored class.

**(5) Chapter 13-only consequences (inert in Chapter 7 unless conversion occurs):** § 1325(a)(8) (plan confirmation conditioned on post-petition DSO currency), § 1307(c)(11) (cause for dismissal for DSO nonpayment), § 1328(a) (discharge conditioned on DSO certification), § 1322(a)(2) (full payment of § 507 priority claims). See *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 788 n.4 (collecting these).

# PART III — THE CORE QUESTION: THE "IN THE NATURE OF SUPPORT" TEST

## III.A. *In re Gianakas*, 917 F.2d 759 (3d Cir. 1990) — **BINDING**

**Full citation:** *Gianakas v. Gianakas (In re Gianakas)*, 917 F.2d 759 (3d Cir. 1990) (No. 90-3258), decided October 19, 1990.
**Panel:** Sloviter, Becker, and Rosenn, Circuit Judges. **Author: Sloviter, Circuit Judge.**
**Precedential:** Yes. Published in F.2d. **BINDING on Bankr. D.N.J.**
**Source of full text:** Public.Resource.Org, `law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/917/917.F2d.759.90-3258.html`; star pagination cross-verified against the Caselaw Access Project static archive (`static.case.law/f2d/917/html/0759-01.html`), which carries embedded page-break markers for pages 760, 761, 762, 763, and 764. **Verification status: CONFIRMED, including pinpoint pages.**

### III.A.1. The posture and the holding

The debtor's settlement agreement, incorporated in a 1983 divorce decree, required him to convey the marital home to his ex-wife and to "assume and pay until satisfied the second mortgage" on that home. He stopped paying, filed Chapter 7, and argued the obligation was a dischargeable property settlement. The bankruptcy court, district court, and Third Circuit all held it was in the nature of support.

> "This is the first opportunity this court has had to analyze which factors should be considered by the bankruptcy court in determining whether an obligation arising out of a divorce settlement is in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support for the purposes of the Bankruptcy Code."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762.

### III.A.2. **THE THREE-FACTOR TEST — VERBATIM, with pinpoints**

**The setup and the statement of the test (917 F.2d at 762):**

> "We believe that whether an obligation is in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support, as distinguished from a property settlement, depends on a finding as to the intent of the parties at the time of the settlement agreement. See In re Yeates, 807 F.2d at 878; Tilley v. Jessee, 789 F.2d 1074, 1077 (4th Cir.1986); In re Miller, 34 B.R. 289, 292 (Bankr.E.D.Pa. 1983). That intent can best be found by examining three principal indicators. The many other factors referred to by various courts are merely elements of these indicators."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762.

**FIRST INDICATOR (917 F.2d at 762–63):**

> "First, the court must examine the language and substance of the agreement in the context of surrounding circumstances, using extrinsic evidence if necessary. See In re Yeates, 807 F.2d at 878; In re Miller, 34 B.R. at 292. However, it is likely that 'neither the parties nor the divorce court contemplated the effect of a subsequent bankruptcy when the obligation arose.' In re Wisniewski, 109 B.R. 926, 929 (Bankr.E.D.Wis.1990). Therefore, the parties and the state courts may not have focused on whether a particular obligation was to serve as support or as a property settlement unrelated to support. In re Alloway, 37 B.R. 420, 425 (Bankr.E.D.Pa.1984). As the Pennsylvania Superior Court noted, even an obligation designated as property settlement may be related to support because state courts often will adjust alimony awards depending on the nature and amount of marital assets available for distribution. Buccino, --- Pa.Super. at ----, 580 A.2d at 18-19. In fact, 'property division often achieves the same goal as alimony, i.e., support.' Id."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762–63 (the page break falls between "may not have focused on" and "whether a particular obligation").

**SECOND INDICATOR (917 F.2d at 763):**

> "Because the language of the agreement alone may not provide a sufficiently conclusive answer as to the nature of an obligation, the second indicator to which we must look to assist in ascertaining the parties' intent is the parties' financial circumstances at the time of the settlement. The facts that one spouse had custody of minor children, was not employed, or was employed in a less remunerative position than the other spouse are aspects of the parties' financial circumstances at the time the obligation was fixed which shed light on the inquiry into the nature of the obligation as support. See Shaver v. Shaver, 736 F.2d 1314, 1317 (9th Cir.1984)."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763.

**THIRD INDICATOR (917 F.2d at 763):**

> "Third, the court should examine the function served by the obligation at the time of the divorce or settlement. An obligation that serves to maintain daily necessities such as food, housing and transportation is indicative of a debt intended to be in the nature of support. See In re Yeates, 807 F.2d at 879."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763.

### III.A.3. The temporal rule — inquiry is fixed as of the time the obligation was undertaken

This is a rule of substantial defensive value and it is often overlooked.

> "We agree with the majority view, and hold that the inquiry of the bankruptcy court should be limited to the nature of the obligation at the time it was undertaken. Where appropriate and authorized by state law, debtors can resort to state court to seek modifications of divorce settlements on the grounds of changed circumstances."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763. And, quoting *Forsdick v. Turgeon*, 812 F.2d 801, 803–04 (2d Cir. 1987):

> "As explained in Forsdick, 812 F.2d at 803-04, an inquiry into present need 'would put federal courts in the position of modifying matrimonial decrees of state courts, thus interfering with the delicate state systems for dealing with the dissolution of marriages....' It would also serve essentially as a penalty for a former spouse who may have struggled to gain self-sufficiency."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763. The Third Circuit expressly **rejected** the Sixth Circuit's *In re Calhoun*, 715 F.2d 1103 (6th Cir. 1983) "present need" and "manifestly unreasonable" overlay. *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763.

Applied in this District:

> "[T]he Third Circuit has expressly rejected the view that a debt may be designated a domestic support obligation based on the parties' present needs."

*In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. 782, 787 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2011), citing *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763.

**Both edges of this rule:** a creditor-spouse cannot bootstrap present impoverishment into a DSO finding; a debtor cannot bootstrap the creditor-spouse's present affluence into a property-settlement finding.

### III.A.4. The result — mortgage assumption is support

> "The great weight of authority holds that a spouse's assumption of mortgage debts which enable members of the family to remain in the marital residence is an obligation in the nature of support, maintenance or alimony. See, e.g., In re Sweeny, 99 B.R. 192 (Bankr.D.Conn.1989); Matter of Coverdale, 65 B.R. 126 (Bankr.M.D.Fla.1986); In re Wright, 51 B.R. 630 (Bankr.S.D.Ohio 1985); In re Thomas, 21 B.R. 571 (Bankr.E.D.Pa.1982). Education and medical expenses are in a similar category. See Draper v. Draper, 790 F.2d at 54."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 764.

And the ad hoc caveat:

> "Although each determination of the nature of an obligation involves an ad hoc inquiry, in light of the record in this case we agree with the district court that it was not error for the bankruptcy court to have concluded, even though the burden of proof was upon Karen, that Paul's obligation to make payments on the second mortgage was in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 764.

### III.A.5. Standard of review (relevant if this reaches appeal)

> "The findings of fact by the bankruptcy court on the underlying or historical facts, such as the intent of the parties at the time of the agreement, are subject to review for clear error. Of course, questions of law, which would include identification of the factors that are relevant to the determination, are subject to plenary review."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 761–62 (page break falls inside the preceding sentence).

# PART IV — FEDERAL LAW GOVERNS; THE LABEL IS EVIDENCE BUT NOT DISPOSITIVE

## IV.A. Proposition 1 — federal bankruptcy law, not state law, governs

**BINDING, verbatim:**

> "Moreover, although the decree or settlement establishing the obligation almost invariably arises in the context of a state court proceeding, whether the obligation is in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support for the purposes of the Bankruptcy Code is a question of federal, not state, law. H.R.Rep. No. 595, 95th Cong., 1st Sess. 364 (1977), reprinted in 1978 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin.News 5963, 6320; S.R. No. 989, 95th Cong., 2d Sess. 79 (1978), reprinted in 1978 U.S.Code Cong. & Admin.News 5787, 5865; Sylvester v. Sylvester, 865 F.2d 1164, 1166 (10th Cir.1989)."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762.

**And the corollary — a state-law non-support characterization does not end the inquiry:**

> "As the court noted in In re Yeates, 807 F.2d 874, 878 (10th Cir.1986), 'a debt could be in the "nature of support" under section 523(a)(5) even though it would not legally qualify as alimony or support under state law.'"

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762.

**The Third Circuit has applied the federal-law rule beyond § 523(a)(5), which broadens its reach:**

> "In Gianakas, the Court of Appeals did not limit its holding to matters under § 523(a)(5), but broadly stated that 'whether the obligation is in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support **for the purposes of the Bankruptcy Code** is a question of federal, not state, law.' In re Gianakas, 917 F.2d at 762 (italics added). Moreover, I find it inappropriate to confuse bankruptcy jurisprudence by defining a single concept — spousal support — in two different ways depending on the section of the Bankruptcy Code that is to be applied."

*In re Miller*, 424 B.R. 171, 176 (Bankr. M.D. Pa. 2010) (applying *Gianakas* to a § 522(d)(10)(D) exemption dispute) (PERSUASIVE).

**In this District:**

> "Whether or not an obligation takes the form of support or alimony, for purposes of the Bankruptcy Code, is a matter of federal law. In re Gianakas, 917 F.2d 759, 762 (3d Cir.1990)."

*Maldonado v. Sanabria (In re Sanabria)*, 275 B.R. 204, 207 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2002) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.).

> "Whether something is actually in the nature of support is [therefore] a question of federal bankruptcy law, and not state law...."

*LaVergne v. LaVergne (In re LaVergne)*, 2011 WL 1878093, at *2 (Bankr. D.N.J. May 17, 2011) (Ferguson, J.), as quoted in *In re Langman*, 465 B.R. at 404.

I could not retrieve its full text through free sources. **The quotation above is taken from *Langman*'s reproduction of it. Pull the *LaVergne* slip opinion from Westlaw or PACER (Bankr. D.N.J., May 17, 2011) before citing it directly.**

## IV.B. Proposition 2 — the label the parties gave it is NOT dispositive

**BINDING, verbatim:**

> "The statute explicitly provides that a debt 'to a spouse, former spouse, or child of the debtor' is not exempted from discharge merely because it is 'designated as alimony, maintenance or support, unless such liability is actually in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support.' 11 U.S.C. Sec. 523(a)(5)(B). **Thus, the court must look beyond the label attached to an obligation by a settlement agreement to examine its true nature.** See 3 Collier on Bankruptcy ¶ 523.15 (15th ed.1990)."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762 (emphasis added).

**Post-BAPCPA statutory anchor.** The old (a)(5)(B) language *Gianakas* was construing now lives in § 101(14A)(B): "in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support ... **without regard to whether such debt is expressly so designated**." The current text is if anything *stronger* than the text *Gianakas* construed, because it disregards designation symmetrically rather than only as a limit on debts labeled "support."

**In this District:**

> "Courts have held that the 'label applied to the obligation by the [state] court or the parties is not necessarily controlling for Bankruptcy Code purposes.'"

*In re LaVergne*, 2011 WL 1878093, at *2, quoting *Werthen v. Werthen (In re Werthen)*, 329 F.3d 269, 272–74 (1st Cir. 2003), as reproduced in *In re Langman*, 465 B.R. at 404. `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING]` — same *LaVergne* caveat as above.

**A clean out-of-circuit articulation of the same rule applying the *current* statute:**

> "Subsection 101(14A), which was added to the Bankruptcy Code under BAPCPA, has codified this standard. A domestic support obligation is a debt that is 'in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support ... without regard to whether such debt is expressly so designated.'"

*In re King*, 461 B.R. 789, 797 (Bankr. D. Alaska 2010) (PERSUASIVE — out of circuit; cited for the codification point only).

## IV.C. Proposition 3 — the converse: the label and the parties' intent ARE relevant evidence

This is the proposition a debtor's brief must plant firmly, because the whole *Gianakas* test is an **intent** test and the agreement is the primary evidence of intent.

**BINDING — intent is the object of the inquiry:**

> "We believe that whether an obligation is in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support, as distinguished from a property settlement, **depends on a finding as to the intent of the parties at the time of the settlement agreement**."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762 (emphasis added).

**BINDING — the agreement's language is indicator one:**

> "First, the court must examine the **language and substance of the agreement** in the context of surrounding circumstances, using extrinsic evidence if necessary."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762 (emphasis added).

**In-circuit — the label can be the most compelling evidence when structurally corroborated:**

> "As the Gianakas court stated, the issue presented requires a determination of the parties' intent in entering the MSA, making this federal question largely one of contract interpretation. Neither party having presented any testimonial evidence, I am left to make inferences primarily from the four (4) corners of the MSA itself.
> **The most compelling indication of the purpose and nature of the ongoing $680.00 per month obligation is the label given to it in the MSA.** Significantly, the accuracy of the characterization in the MSA is vigorously reinforced by the fact that the obligation terminates when either a child reaches the age of 18, leaves high school or leaves Ms. Miller's residence — very strong indicators that it is in the nature of support."

*In re Miller*, 501 B.R. 266, 282–83 (Bankr. E.D. Pa. 2013) (Frank, J.) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. E.D. Pa.) (emphasis added).

**In this District — the *placement* of the obligation within the judgment is affirmative evidence:**

> "Indeed, the limited evidence before the Court suggests that condominium fees are not in the nature of domestic support obligations, as the FJOD specifically addresses such obligations in the portion of the order entitled 'Equitable Distribution'."

*In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. 782, 787 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2011) (PERSUASIVE — anticipated forum). **This is the single most useful Bankr. D.N.J. sentence for a debtor arguing that a lump sum sitting under an "Equitable Distribution" heading is not a DSO.**

**In this District — the parties' own trial testimony about what the payment was consideration for is dispositive evidence:**

> "Based on testimony and the terms of the agreement, the sum at issue can only be classified as a property settlement. At trial, Maldonado testified that the $350,000 sum was to be paid to her in consideration for all her rights and interest in the businesses and properties jointly owned by the parties. Additionally, the agreement made provisions for both alimony and child support, which were separate from the section providing for the $350,000.
> The court finds that the intent of the parties, at the time of the settlement agreement was to categorize the $350,000 as a property settlement. As such, the obligation at issue falls outside the scope of § 523(a)(5)...."

*In re Sanabria*, 275 B.R. 204, 207 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2002) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.).

## IV.D. Reconciling IV.B and IV.C — the operative formulation

The two propositions are not in tension; they define a **rebuttable-presumption-like** structure that should be stated to the court in these terms:

1. The label is **evidence of intent**, and often the best single piece of evidence, because it is a contemporaneous written statement by the very parties whose intent is the legal question. *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762; *In re Miller*, 501 B.R. at 282–83.
2. The label is **not conclusive**, because § 101(14A)(B) directs the court to the "nature" of the debt "without regard to whether such debt is expressly so designated," and because the parties may not have contemplated bankruptcy. *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762–63.
3. Therefore the label **controls unless the challenging party comes forward with evidence that the substance diverges from the form** — and under *Gianakas* that evidence is confined to circumstances existing "at the time it was undertaken." *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763.

**No case located states this as a formal burden-shifting presumption.** State it as the practical operation of the three indicators, not as a rule the Third Circuit has announced.

## IV.E. The parol evidence rule does not bar extrinsic evidence

*Gianakas* itself contemplates "using extrinsic evidence if necessary," 917 F.2d at 762. Out-of-circuit authority is squarely on point and useful if the creditor-spouse's evidence is extrinsic:

> "The parol evidence rule does not apply in cases such as the instant one, and factual inquiry under section 523(a)(5) is not limited to the four corners of a separation agreement or divorce decree."

*Brody v. Brody (In re Brody)*, 3 F.3d 35, 38–39 (2d Cir. 1993), quoted in *In re Colin*, 546 B.R. 455, 461 (Bankr. N.D. Ala. 2016) (PERSUASIVE — 2d Cir. as quoted; not read in full).
`[VERIFY BEFORE FILING]` — I have not read *Brody*, 3 F.3d 35, in full. The quotation is as reproduced by *Colin*. Pull before citing.

**Note the strategic asymmetry:** this rule cuts *against* a debtor whose MSA cleanly labels the obligation as equitable distribution, because it opens the door to the creditor-spouse's testimony about what the parties "really" meant. It also cuts *for* a debtor whose MSA is ambiguous. Expect the creditor-spouse to file affidavits of the kind rejected in *Colin* (affidavits from the creditor-spouse and her divorce attorney asserting they "intended the debt as support" and "did not realize the implications in bankruptcy," *Colin*, 546 B.R. at 458).

# PART V — APPLICATION TO SPECIFIC FACT PATTERNS

**READ THIS FIRST.** No case facts have been supplied. Everything below is a *conditional* analysis: "if the facts are X, the authority points toward Y." Each subsection ends with the specific facts that must be developed. Nothing here is an opinion about any actual obligation.

## V.0. The master factor checklist to apply

The three *Gianakas* indicators, broken out. The best in-circuit expansion is Judge Frank's, which imports the Collier list under the *Gianakas* umbrella:

> "A leading treatise breaks out the three (3) Gianakas factors into a more expansive set of factors for a court to consider in distinguishing DSO's from property division obligations:
> (1) the labels in the agreement or court order;
> (2) the income and needs of the parties at the time the obligation became fixed;
> (3) the amount and outcome of property division;
> (4) whether the obligation terminates on obligee's death or remarriage or on emancipation of children;
> (5) number and frequency of payments;
> (6) waiver of alimony or support rights in agreement;
> (7) availability of state court procedures to modify or enforce obligation through contempt remedy;
> (8) tax treatment of obligation.
> 4 Collier ¶ 523.11[6][a]-[h]"

*In re Miller*, 501 B.R. 266, 282 (Bankr. E.D. Pa. 2013) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. E.D. Pa.).

**Use this list, not the fifteen-factor *Buccino* list.** It is post-BAPCPA, in-circuit, tied expressly to *Gianakas*, and short enough to argue.

## V.1. A lump-sum equitable distribution payable in installments

**Baseline: PROPERTY SETTLEMENT (§ 523(a)(15)), not a DSO.**

**Directly on point in this District — the closest available authority:**

*In re Sanabria*, 275 B.R. 204 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2002). A PSA required the debtor to pay $350,000. The creditor-spouse sought nondischargeability under both (a)(5) and (a)(15). Held: **not** (a)(5); it was a property settlement falling under (a)(15).

> "Based on testimony and the terms of the agreement, the sum at issue can only be classified as a property settlement. At trial, Maldonado testified that the $350,000 sum was to be paid to her in consideration for all her rights and interest in the businesses and properties jointly owned by the parties. Additionally, the agreement made provisions for both alimony and child support, which were separate from the section providing for the $350,000."

*Sanabria*, 275 B.R. at 207.

**The three structural features that drove *Sanabria* and should be checked in every case:**
1. The sum was **stated consideration for the spouse's property interests**;
2. The agreement contained **separate, express alimony and child support provisions**; and
3. The sum was in a **separate section** from those provisions.

**Persuasive, closely analogous, and post-BAPCPA:** *Edwards v. Colin (In re Colin)*, 546 B.R. 455 (Bankr. N.D. Ala. 2016) (summary judgment denied), *final judgment*, 556 B.R. 520 (Bankr. N.D. Ala. 2016). Facts: "Under Section III of the Agreement, titled 'Personal Property Division,' Colin is obligated to pay Edwards $90,000 over a span of 120 months; Colin is required to pay at least $750 per month toward this obligation, but may prepay it or pay it in lump sum if he wishes." *Colin*, 546 B.R. at 457. Section V, "Alimony," provided both parties "forever waive alimony." *Id.* After trial: "The debt Allen Colin owes Sara Edwards is not a domestic support obligation. Therefore, it is a debt arising out of divorce under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(15)...." *Colin*, 556 B.R. at 526. (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. N.D. Ala.; useful because the fact pattern is near-identical to a typical MSA lump sum in installments.)

**The court's reasoning on installments is the key passage for this fact pattern:**

> "The finite amount of the obligation and ability to prepay or pay in lump sum also indicates the debt was not intended as support. ... So too does the absence of any provision for termination of the obligation in the event of either party's death or Edwards's remarriage. ... Moreover, under Alabama law, the obligation is likely not modifiable because it is property settlement. ... Finally, the main point in Edwards's favor that is present on the face of the Agreement itself — the fact that the obligation can be paid in installments — is undercut by the inference that Colin could not afford to pay the full amount at once."

*Colin*, 546 B.R. at 461.

**The counter-move, and it is real:** installment structure is the creditor-spouse's best textual foothold, because periodic payments *look* like support. *Colin*'s answer — that installments reflect the obligor's inability to pay a lump sum, not a support purpose — is the response, but it is fact-dependent.

**Facts needed:** total amount; whether stated as consideration for identified property interests; presence and separateness of alimony/child-support provisions; whether prepayment is permitted; whether the amount is fixed and finite; whether interest runs; termination provisions; whether the amount was derived from a valuation of marital assets or from a budget/needs analysis; tax treatment elected.

## V.2. A fixed dollar sum for the spouse's interest in the marital home

**This is the hardest fact pattern in the memo, because it is where *Gianakas* itself lives.**

**The distinction that matters is between:**
- **(A) An obligation to *pay the mortgage* so the spouse and children can remain in the residence** → *Gianakas* holds this is **support**, 917 F.2d at 764 ("The great weight of authority holds that a spouse's assumption of mortgage debts which enable members of the family to remain in the marital residence is an obligation in the nature of support, maintenance or alimony."). **This is BINDING and it is bad for a debtor.**
- **(B) An obligation to *buy out* the spouse's equity — a fixed dollar sum representing her half interest, paid so that the debtor keeps the house** → this is a purchase price for a property interest, functionally identical to *Sanabria*'s $350,000 buyout of business interests, and it does not "serve to maintain daily necessities such as food, housing and transportation" for the payee. *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763.

**Frame the third indicator carefully.** The third *Gianakas* indicator asks about "the function served by the obligation." In pattern (A) the function is *housing the payee*. In pattern (B) the function is *cashing out the payee's capital interest so the payor can keep the asset*. That is a distinction of substance, not semantics, and it is the debtor's central argument.

**Counter-authority to expect, and it is strong:** *In re King*, 461 B.R. 789 (Bankr. D. Alaska 2010) (PERSUASIVE — out of circuit). A state court entered a **lump sum judgment** in the ex-wife's favor arising from a second mortgage on *her* home, in a section captioned "Findings of Fact and Conclusions of Law Regarding Property Division." Held: **support**.

> "Although application of the Shaver and Combs factors leads to mixed results, I find that Marcus's debt to Angel is in the nature of support. A bankruptcy court is to look to the nature of the obligation without regard to whether the debt is designated support or property settlement. The labels used by the state court, e.g., 'an evidentiary hearing regarding property issues' and 'findings of fact and conclusions of law regarding property division,' are not dispositive. It is clear that the primary concern of the superior court was the preservation of Angel's home."

*In re King*, 461 B.R. at 800.

**How to distinguish *King*:** (i) the payee there was at risk of *losing her home* and the state court said so expressly; (ii) the parties had "kept their finances separate during their short marriage and there were no other assets or debts to divide other than the second mortgage," *id.*, so there was no genuine property division to speak of; (iii) the state court found she "needed monthly payments from the debtor in order to retain the home," *id.*; (iv) the payment ran *toward a mortgage on the payee's residence*, i.e., pattern (A), not a buyout of the payor's residence, pattern (B).

**Facts needed:** who occupies the residence post-divorce; whether minor children live there; whether the sum is a mortgage assumption or a buyout of equity; whether the payee could retain housing without the payment; whether the payment amount tracks an appraised equity split (property indicator) or a housing budget (support indicator); whether a separate alimony provision already addresses housing costs.

## V.3. A share of a business interest or a retirement account

**Baseline: PROPERTY SETTLEMENT (§ 523(a)(15)). This is the strongest fact pattern for the debtor in the memo.**

**Business interest — directly on point in this District.** *In re Sanabria*, 275 B.R. at 207: the $350,000 "was to be paid to her in consideration for all her rights and interest in the businesses and properties jointly owned by the parties" → property settlement, not (a)(5). This is a Bankr. D.N.J. holding on precisely this fact pattern.

**Retirement account — persuasive, post-BAPCPA.** In *Colin*, the debtor was "obligated to pay Edwards 45% of his retirement account pursuant to Section VI of the Agreement," an obligation of roughly $165,000, alongside the $90,000 "Personal Property Division" obligation. *Colin*, 546 B.R. at 457. Neither was held to be a DSO. *Colin*, 556 B.R. at 526.

**Why this pattern is strong for the debtor under all three indicators:**
1. **Language and substance:** a percentage share of a specifically identified asset is, on its face, a division of a capital asset, not a periodic transfer for maintenance.
2. **Financial circumstances:** a spouse receiving a capital share is receiving her own accrued marital property, which is conceptually the opposite of a need-based transfer.
3. **Function:** the function of transferring a share of a 401(k) or a partnership interest is to *divide* an asset, not "to maintain daily necessities such as food, housing and transportation." *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763.

**Watch for two complications:**
- **QDRO mechanics.** If the retirement share is implemented by a Qualified Domestic Relations Order, the transferred portion may never become property of the debtor's estate at all, in which case the dischargeability question may be moot as to that portion and live only as to any residual money obligation. **No Third Circuit or Bankr. D.N.J. authority located on the QDRO/DSO intersection.** Flag as an open question.
- **New Jersey exempts qualified plans.** If the underlying account is exempt, the § 522(c)(1) analysis in Part II.E becomes the whole ballgame.

**Facts needed:** whether the share is expressed as a percentage of an identified account/entity or as a dollar sum; whether a QDRO was entered; whether the account is ERISA-qualified; whether the transfer was completed pre-petition; whether the interest was valued as of a date certain.

## V.4. A "hold harmless" / indemnification clause on marital debt

**Baseline: it depends entirely on *what debt* is being indemnified. Congress legislated specifically about this category, and the legislative history is unusually useful.**

**The 1994 Committee Report, quoted at length in this District:**

> "Subsection (e) adds a new exception to discharge for some debts arising out of a divorce decree or separation agreement that are not in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support. In some instances, divorcing spouses have agreed to make payments of marital debts, holding the other spouse harmless from those debts, in exchange for a reduction in alimony payments. In other cases, spouses have agreed to lower alimony based on a larger property settlement. **If such 'hold harmless' and property settlement obligations are not found to be in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support, they are dischargeable under current law.** The non-debtor spouse may be saddled with substantial debt and little or no alimony or support. This subsection will make such obligations nondischargeable in cases where the debtor has the ability to pay them and the detriment to the non-debtor spouse from their nonpayment outweighs the benefit to the debtor of discharging such debts. ... The Committee believes that payment of support needs must take precedence over property settlement debts. ..."

H.R. Rep. No. 103-835, 103d Cong., 2d Sess. 54, *reprinted in* 1994 U.S.C.C.A.N. 3363, as quoted in *Halper v. Halper (In re Halper)*, 213 B.R. 279, 282 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1997) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.).

**Two things follow.**

1. **Congress's own premise was that hold-harmless clauses are generally *not* in the nature of support.** That is why (a)(15) was needed at all. This is a strong structural argument for the debtor on characterization, and it comes from the House Report rather than from a case.
2. **BAPCPA then deleted the ability-to-pay and detriment conditions**, so today a hold-harmless obligation is nondischargeable in Chapter 7 *regardless* of characterization. The fight is therefore about the § 522(c)(1)/§ 507(a)(1)/§ 362(b)(2) consequences, not about discharge.

**The exception that swallows the rule — indemnification of housing debt.** Where the "marital debt" indemnified is a mortgage on the residence occupied by the payee and children, *Gianakas* controls and the obligation is support. 917 F.2d at 764. *Gianakas* was itself a mortgage-assumption case.

**In this District:** *Buglione v. Berlingeri (In re Berlingeri)*, 246 B.R. 196 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2000) involved precisely this contest — the debtor argued his obligations "to pay the professional fees or to hold Buglione harmless with respect to matrimonial debts" were "elements of equitable distribution which are subject to discharge because he does not have the ability to pay them," invoking pre-BAPCPA § 523(a)(15)(A). *Berlingeri*, 246 B.R. at 198. The court never reached the characterization question, holding instead that the claims arose post-petition and were nondischargeable under § 727(b). *Id.* at 199.

**⚠ *Berlingeri* is no longer good law on its actual holding.** It rested on the *Frenville* accrual test, which the Third Circuit overruled in *JELD-WEN, Inc. v. Van Brunt (In re Grossman's)*, 607 F.3d 114 (3d Cir. 2010), and *Ruitenberg* said so explicitly: "But contrary to Orr's contention, both cases relied on Frenville's accrual test. See Howell, 311 B.R. at 176; Berlingeri, 246 B.R. at 199. ... the accrual test was abandoned by Grossman's even in this context." *In re Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d 647, 651 (3d Cir. 2014) (**BINDING**). **Cite *Berlingeri* only for the framing of the parties' arguments, never for its holding on when an equitable distribution claim arises.**

**Facts needed:** identity of the underlying indemnified debt (mortgage on payee's residence? credit cards? business debt? tax liability?); whether the payee is in fact exposed to the creditor; whether the indemnity was traded for reduced alimony (the House Report's paradigm); whether the indemnified debt funds daily necessities.

## V.5. Termination on death or remarriage vs. no termination

**This is the single most probative structural feature after the label, and both directions are recognized in this Circuit.**

**Authority for the factor:** *In re Miller*, 501 B.R. 266, 282 (Bankr. E.D. Pa. 2013) (Collier factor (4): "whether the obligation terminates on obligee's death or remarriage or on emancipation of children"); *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762 n.1 (*Buccino* factor: "whether the debt terminates on the occurrence of death, remarriage, etc.").

**Termination provision present → strong support indicator.** In-circuit application:

> "Significantly, the accuracy of the characterization in the MSA is vigorously reinforced by the fact that the obligation terminates when either a child reaches the age of 18, leaves high school or leaves Ms. Miller's residence — **very strong indicators that it is in the nature of support**."

*In re Miller*, 501 B.R. at 283 (emphasis added) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. E.D. Pa.).

**No termination provision → property-division indicator.** Persuasive, post-BAPCPA:

> "So too does the absence of any provision for termination of the obligation in the event of either party's death or Edwards's remarriage."

*In re Colin*, 546 B.R. at 461 (listing this among the factors indicating the debt was *not* support) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. N.D. Ala.).

And, on the other side of the same coin, in a case that nonetheless found support:

> "The debtor's obligation to pay does not terminate on the death or remarriage of Angel."

*In re King*, 461 B.R. at 799 (acknowledging the factor cut against a support finding, but finding support anyway on the totality) (PERSUASIVE — out of circuit; useful as counter-authority, see Part VIII).

**No Third Circuit or Bankr. D.N.J. decision located that turns squarely on the death/remarriage factor.** Say so; do not overclaim.

**New Jersey-specific reinforcement worth developing:** under New Jersey law, alimony is generally terminable and modifiable on changed circumstances, while equitable distribution awards are final and not subject to modification. If the MSA's own terms make the obligation non-modifiable and non-terminable, that is evidence the parties treated it as an equitable distribution award as a matter of New Jersey law — and *Gianakas* permits the court to consider "the language and substance of the agreement in the context of surrounding circumstances," 917 F.2d at 762, which includes the state-law backdrop against which the parties bargained. **This argument is available but I located no Bankr. D.N.J. case making it expressly.** See Part VI.

**Facts needed:** exact termination language; whether the obligation survives the payee's death and passes to her estate; whether it survives the payor's death and binds his estate; whether the MSA makes it modifiable; whether it is enforceable by contempt (Collier factor (7)).

## V.6. A payee who was financially independent or high-earning at the time of the agreement

**This goes to the *second* *Gianakas* indicator and it is measured strictly at the time of the agreement.**

**BINDING statement of the indicator:**

> "The facts that one spouse had custody of minor children, was not employed, or was employed in a less remunerative position than the other spouse are aspects of the parties' financial circumstances at the time the obligation was fixed which shed light on the inquiry into the nature of the obligation as support."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763.

**Read the negative:** where the payee spouse had custody of no minor children, *was* employed, and was employed comparably to or better than the payor, the second indicator points away from support. That is the inference *Gianakas* itself licenses.

**Contrast with *Gianakas*'s own facts, which are the paradigm on the other side:** "Karen had no income at the time of the divorce," had four children, received $800/month child support plus $200/month alimony, and "testified that if she had to pay the additional $225 per month on the second mortgage indefinitely, she could not continue to live in the home." *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763–64.

**Similar reasoning applied by a court in this Circuit:** "the Report makes numerous findings showing the substantial disparity in the parties' employment and their respective potential for future earnings. Miller had substantial real estate holdings and business interests at the time of the divorce, while Debtor had no real estate, no business interests and no marketable skills to enable her to retain employment at more than the minimum wage." *In re Miller*, 424 B.R. 171, 177–78 (Bankr. M.D. Pa. 2010) (finding support). The mirror-image facts would point the other way.

**⚠ THE TRAP — do not argue present financial independence.** *Gianakas* forecloses it: "the inquiry of the bankruptcy court should be limited to the nature of the obligation at the time it was undertaken," 917 F.2d at 763, and an inquiry into present need "would ... serve essentially as a penalty for a former spouse who may have struggled to gain self-sufficiency," *id.* A debtor's brief that argues "she is doing fine now" invites a citation to *Gianakas* against itself. Frame every affluence argument as of the date of the MSA.

**A caution that cuts the other way, from a court in this Circuit:** even substantial earning capacity in the payee does not by itself convert a labeled support obligation into a property division. "[E]ven assuming that the Debtor's earning capacity was less than Ms. Miller's when they entered into the MSA, that fact, by itself, does not establish that the obligation labeled as support in the MSA was truly a disguised form of a division of marital assets." *In re Miller*, 501 B.R. at 283–84.

**Facts needed:** both parties' income, assets, and employment as of the MSA date; custody arrangements as of that date; whether alimony was awarded, waived, or limited in duration; whether the payee was represented by counsel; the payee's age, health, employability, and educational level as of that date (*Buccino* factors, subsidiary under *Gianakas*).

## V.7. Payments structured with interest and a payment schedule

**Baseline: PROPERTY-DIVISION indicator — but this is the weakest of the structural arguments and it must be handled carefully.**

**The argument for the debtor:** a fixed principal amount, amortized over a defined term, bearing a stated rate of interest, with a prepayment right, is the structure of a **purchase-money obligation**, not of a support obligation. Support is not typically expressed as principal-plus-interest; it is expressed as a periodic amount responsive to need. The presence of interest signals that the parties valued a capital asset as of a date certain and agreed to deferred payment of its price.

**Support in the case law:** the Collier factor list adopted in this Circuit includes "(5) number and frequency of payments" and "(3) the amount and outcome of property division." *In re Miller*, 501 B.R. at 282. *Colin* treated finiteness plus a prepayment right as indicating non-support: "The finite amount of the obligation and ability to prepay or pay in lump sum also indicates the debt was not intended as support." *Colin*, 546 B.R. at 461.

**The counter, and it is genuine:** § 101(14A) expressly contemplates that a DSO carries interest — "including interest that accrues on that debt as provided under applicable nonbankruptcy law notwithstanding any other provision of this title." In this District that clause has been applied to award post-petition interest on support arrears. *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 788–89. So the *existence* of interest cannot itself defeat DSO status. The argument must be sharpened: it is not that interest exists, but that interest was **bargained for as the time value of a fixed purchase price**, which is a capital-transaction structure.

**Also genuine:** a periodic payment schedule superficially resembles support. *Colin*'s answer is that installments reflect inability to pay a lump sum. *Colin*, 546 B.R. at 461. That answer is available only if the record supports it.

**I located no Third Circuit or Bankr. D.N.J. case holding that the presence of interest is a property-division indicator.** State the argument as one of structure and inference under *Gianakas* indicator one, supported by *Colin* and Collier, not as settled Third Circuit law.

**Facts needed:** stated rate and whether it matches a statutory judgment rate or a negotiated rate; amortization schedule; acceleration and default clauses; whether secured by a lien or mortgage on specific property (a strong property-division indicator, since support is not typically collateralized); prepayment rights; whether the principal amount corresponds to an appraisal or valuation.

## V.8. Attorney's fee awards in the matrimonial action

**Baseline: SUPPORT (§ 523(a)(5)) — this is the fact pattern where the debtor is most likely to lose in this District. Be candid about it.**

**Bankr. D.N.J., directly:**

> "In determining whether an obligation imposed in connection with a divorce is in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support under Code section 523(a)(5), the courts consider the financial circumstances of the parties and the purpose served by the obligation. In re Gianakas, 917 F.2d 759, 763 (3d Cir.1990). **The overwhelming majority of courts have concluded that attorney's fees and costs imposed in connection with a divorce or post-divorce action between former spouses are in the nature of support under Code section 523(a)(5).** Macy v. Macy, 114 F.3d 1 (1st Cir.1997); Matter of Hudson, 107 F.3d 355 (5th Cir.1997); In re Strickland, 90 F.3d 444 (11th Cir.1996); In re Kline, 65 F.3d 749 (8th Cir.1995); Matter of Joseph, 16 F.3d 86 (5th Cir.1994); In re Jones, 9 F.3d 878 (10th Cir.1993); In re Spong, 661 F.2d 6 (2nd Cir.1981) ... But see, Adams v. Zentz, 963 F.2d 197 (8th Cir.1992)."

*Lawrence v. Lawrence (In re Lawrence)*, 237 B.R. 61, 84–85 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1999) (Stripp, J.) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.) (emphasis added).

**And the New Jersey-law reason why:**

> "As is apparently true in most other states, New Jersey law considers the financial circumstances of the parties in imposing attorney's fees and costs in a divorce case. N.J.Stat.Ann. 2A:34-23; Boardman v. Boardman, supra. A substantial equitable distribution award to a party does not necessarily preclude an award of fees to such party. Shaffer v. Shaffer, 154 N.J.Super. 491, 381 A.2d 1221 (App.Div.1977). **It follows that such fees and costs are in the nature of support and are therefore nondischargeable under Code section 523(a)(5).**"

*Lawrence*, 237 B.R. at 85–86 (emphasis added). Note that the court reached the **same result for forensic-accountant fees**. *Id.* at 86.

**This reasoning is a double-edged sword and the edge cuts for the debtor elsewhere.** *Lawrence*'s logic is: N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23 conditions fee awards on "the respective financial circumstances of the parties," therefore fee awards are need-based, therefore support. The **converse** is that N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23's *equitable distribution* clause is **not** need-conditioned — it directs the court to "effectuate an equitable distribution of the property ... which was legally and beneficially acquired by them" — and therefore equitable distribution awards are not need-based and not support. **Use *Lawrence*'s own methodology against the creditor-spouse on the equitable distribution claim.**

**Two important limits on the attorney's-fee rule.**

1. **Fees owed by the debtor's own spouse to her own lawyer are not covered at all.** *In re Langman*, 465 B.R. 395 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2012). The § 101(14A)(A) payee element fails:

> "Ms. Langman's debt to Ms. Berse does not meet this requirement. First, it is a debt that was incurred by Ms. Langman in furtherance of her own maintenance or support (or for that of her own children). Second, pursuant to Judge Sivilli's Order establishing the lien, the debt is owed to or recoverable by Ms. Berse alone, not Ms. Langman or her children."

*Langman*, 465 B.R. at 409.

2. **Fees payable to a third-party law firm on behalf of the ex-spouse *are* generally covered**, under this Circuit's "substance over form" line. *Langman*, 465 B.R. at 406–07, collecting *Falk & Siemer, LLP v. Maddigan (In re Maddigan)*, 312 F.3d 589, 593 (2d Cir. 2002); *Romeo v. Romeo (In re Romeo)*, 16 B.R. 531, 536 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1981); and *Clair, Griefer LLP v. Prensky (In re Prensky)*, 416 B.R. 406, 410 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2009).

**Allocation is the practical battleground.** In *Langman*, "Debtors' counsel conceded that some of Ms. Berse's attorney's fees were attributable to services in pursuit of Ethel Langman's alimony and support claims, in addition to claims for equitable distribution — such as obtaining pendente lite support orders. Debtors' counsel, however, insisted that such services constituted only a small portion of the services performed by Ms. Berse." *Langman*, 465 B.R. at 401. **Expect to litigate what fraction of a fee award is attributable to support litigation versus equitable-distribution litigation.** Obtain the underlying fee certifications and time entries.

**Facts needed:** the state-court order awarding fees and its stated basis (need-based? bad-faith-based? R. 4:42-9(a)(1)?); the fee application and time entries; allocation between support and equitable-distribution work; whether payable to the spouse or directly to counsel; whether a charging lien was entered.

# PART VI — BANKR. D.N.J. / D.N.J. AND N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23

## VI.A. The New Jersey statutory architecture is genuinely bifurcated

**N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23** contains, in one section, three functionally distinct grants:

1. **Alimony and maintenance** — need-based, modifiable on changed circumstances.
2. **Equitable distribution** — as quoted by this District: "where a judgment of divorce ... is entered the court may make such award or awards to the parties ... [as is appropriate] to effectuate an equitable distribution" of property acquired during the marriage. *Lawrence*, 237 B.R. at 79, quoting N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23. And, in fuller form: "[i]n all actions where a judgment of divorce ... is entered the court may make such award or awards to the parties ... to effectuate an equitable distribution of the property, both real and personal, which was legally and beneficially acquired by them or either of them during the marriage." *In re Berlingeri*, 246 B.R. 196, 199 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2000), quoting N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23 (West 1999).
3. **Counsel and expert fees** — expressly need-conditioned: fees may be awarded "when the respective financial circumstances of the parties make the award reasonable and just," and "the court shall consider the factors set forth in the court rule on counsel fees, the financial circumstances of the parties, and the good or bad faith of either party." *Lawrence*, 237 B.R. at 84, quoting N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23.

**N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23.1** supplies the equitable-distribution factor list and a presumption of joint contribution: "It shall be a rebuttable presumption that each party made a substantial financial or nonfinancial contribution to the acquisition of income and property [during the marriage]." Quoted by the Third Circuit in *In re Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d 647, 649–50 (3d Cir. 2014) (**BINDING**) and applied at length in *Lawrence*, 237 B.R. at 67–80.

**Nothing in N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23.1's factor list is a need or support factor in the alimony sense.** The factors are contribution-, duration-, and asset-focused. That is the doctrinal basis for arguing that a New Jersey equitable distribution award is, by state-law design, a division of jointly-acquired capital rather than a need-based transfer.

## VI.B. Do bankruptcy courts in this District treat the state-law distinction as evidence? — **Yes, but obliquely.**

**The best direct instance:** *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 787, where Judge Kaplan relied on the **placement of the obligation within the Final Judgment of Divorce** to infer that it was not support:

> "Indeed, the limited evidence before the Court suggests that condominium fees are not in the nature of domestic support obligations, as the FJOD specifically addresses such obligations in the portion of the order entitled 'Equitable Distribution'. See FJOD, p. 5-6."

That is the state-law distinction operating as evidence of the parties' (and the state court's) intent — precisely what *Gianakas* indicator one contemplates. **It is not, however, a holding that the state-law category is dispositive**, and *Anthony* pairs it with the observation that the creditor "failed to allege any facts from which the Court could determine that such fees are in the nature of support." *Id.*

**The second instance — *Lawrence*'s method.** As explained at Part V.8, *Lawrence* reasoned *from* the need-conditioned text of N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23's fee provision *to* the conclusion that fee awards are support. *Lawrence*, 237 B.R. at 85–86. The same method, applied to the equitable-distribution clause (which is not need-conditioned), yields the opposite conclusion for equitable distribution. **This is an argument by parity of reasoning, not a holding.** Present it as such.

**The third instance — *Chaudry*'s structural holding.** Judge Gravelle held that equitable distribution and support claims share a defining feature that Tevis (marital tort) claims lack: they cannot exist outside a divorce:

> "The existence of a Tevis claim must be distinguished from the existence of other types of claims normally associated with a divorce proceeding like claims for equitable distribution, alimony, and child support. The latter claims do not exist until a divorce complaint is filed. ... Unlike a Tevis claim, which exists at the time the assault occurs, an equitable distribution or support claim cannot exist absent a divorce proceeding. **Debts for equitable distribution and support cannot be incurred other than in connection with a divorce or separation agreement. Consequently, they fall squarely within the exception to discharge outlined in § 523(a)(15) of the Code.**"

*In re Chaudry*, 569 B.R. 372, 376 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2017) (Gravelle, J.) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. D.N.J.) (emphasis added). Note the framing: equitable distribution debts fall within **§ 523(a)(15)**.

**The fourth instance — the Third Circuit's own treatment.** *Ruitenberg* analyzed a New Jersey equitable distribution interest entirely as a property/claim question under § 101(5) and § 541, never as a support question, and recorded the creditor's concession that it "is not entitled to priority as a domestic support obligation." 745 F.3d at 649. It also held:

> "[W]e hold that a non-debtor spouse has an allowable pre-petition claim against the debtor's bankruptcy estate for equitable distribution of marital property when the parties are in divorce proceedings before the bankruptcy petition is filed."

*In re Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d at 653 (**BINDING**).

**And the Butner point, which the Third Circuit addressed head-on:**

> "We also reject Orr's contention that allowing a pre-petition claim in this case conflicts with New Jersey law and thereby disregards the rule of Butner v. United States, 440 U.S. 48, 55 (1979). Even though a state court in New Jersey may not order equitable distribution until a judgment of divorce has been entered, see Carr, 576 A.2d at 875, courts in the State recognize that certain rights to the marital property arise even before an order is entered."

*Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d at 652–53 (**BINDING**).

## VI.C. The clean statement of the Bankr. D.N.J. rule

> "This provision has been read to encompass a range of matrimonial debts, including obligations arising out of property settlement agreements and equitable distribution judgments. In re Clouse, 446 B.R. 690, 707 n. 58 (Bankr.E.D.Pa.2010) (Fehling, J.) (**holding that nondischargeability claims for equitable distribution debts must be brought under § 523(a)(15), not (a)(5), as equitable distribution is not included in the definition of 'domestic support obligation' under § 101(14A)**); accord Lawrence v. Lawrence (In re Lawrence), 237 B.R. 61, 83, 86-87 (Bankr.D.N.J.1999) (Stripp, J.)."

*In re Langman*, 465 B.R. 395, 405 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2012) (emphasis added).

**And the source it relies on, verbatim:**

> "Equitable distribution is not included within the definition of 'domestic support obligations.' The dischargeability of domestic support obligations and of equitable distribution is separately established in Sections 523(a)(5) and (a)(15), 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(5) & (15). See Kennedy v. Kennedy (In re Kennedy), Adv. No. 10-1020, Bankr.No. 09-12289-TPA, 2010 WL 3781066, at *3 (Bankr.W.D.Pa., Sept.15, 2010)."

*In re Clouse*, 446 B.R. 690, 707 n.58 (Bankr. E.D. Pa. 2010) (Fehling, J.) (PERSUASIVE — Bankr. E.D. Pa., within the Third Circuit).

**And *Lawrence*'s own treatment of a New Jersey equitable distribution claim, which it analyzed exclusively under (a)(15):**

> "Ms. Lawrence has met her burden of proving that her equitable distribution claim as determined by this court is presumptively nondischargeable under Code section 523(a)(15). The debtor has failed to introduce any evidence whatsoever that the claim should nevertheless be discharged under one of the exceptions in Code section 523(a)(15)(A) or 523(a)(15)(B). **The court holds that Ms. Lawrence's monetary claim for equitable distribution is therefore non-dischargeable in bankruptcy.**"

*Lawrence*, 237 B.R. at 83 (emphasis added).

**Note carefully:** *Lawrence* found the equitable distribution claim nondischargeable — but under **(a)(15)**, not (a)(5), and it separately found the *attorney's fees* nondischargeable under **(a)(5)**. The opinion is a clean illustration of the two-track treatment. It is not, and should not be cited as, authority that equitable distribution is a DSO.

# PART VII — BURDEN OF PROOF AND PROCEDURE

## VII.A. Who bears the burden — the creditor-spouse

**BINDING, and it is *Gianakas* itself:**

> "A party, such as appellee here, who objects to the discharge of a particular debt has the burden of proving nondischargeability. Bankruptcy Rule 4005; Matter of Long, 794 F.2d 928, 930 (4th Cir.1986); In re Kleppinger, 27 B.R. 530, 531 (Bankr.M.D.Pa.1982)."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 761.

*Gianakas* applied this rigorously. The bankruptcy court had made inconsistent statements about burden allocation; the Third Circuit found no prejudice only because "the district court placed the burden of proof on the party that must bear it and thereafter reached the same result." *Id.* And the framing of the ultimate question was burden-referenced: "whether Karen met her burden of proof to show that Paul's obligation to pay the second mortgage was in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support." *Id.*

**Note on the cited rule.** *Gianakas* cites "Bankruptcy Rule 4005." Rule 4005 by its terms addresses the burden of proof at trial **on an objection to discharge under § 727**, not on a § 523 dischargeability complaint. The allocation *Gianakas* announced is nonetheless the settled rule and is now grounded in *Grogan*. **Do not repeat the Rule 4005 citation without noting this; cite *Grogan* and the in-District cases instead.**

## VII.B. The standard — preponderance of the evidence

**BINDING, SCOTUS:** *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 287–88 (1991), holding that the standard of proof for the dischargeability exceptions in § 523(a) is the ordinary preponderance-of-the-evidence standard.

**As applied in this District:**

> "The United States Supreme Court has held that the primary purpose of the Bankruptcy Code is to provide a 'fresh start' to the 'honest but unfortunate debtor.' Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991). As a result, in a trial on the merits, **the creditor bears the burden of proving that a debt is nondischargeable under § 523(a) under a preponderance of the evidence standard, and 'exceptions to discharge are strictly construed against creditors and liberally construed in favor of debtors.'** Ins. Co. of Am. v. Cohn (In re Cohn), 54 F.3d 1108, 1113 (3d Cir.1995); see also Grogan, 498 U.S. at 287-88."

*In re Langman*, 465 B.R. 395, 403 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2012) (emphasis added). *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1113 (3d Cir. 1995) is **BINDING** Third Circuit authority for the strict-construction canon.

`[VERIFY BEFORE FILING]` — I have not read *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, in full. The quotation is as reproduced by *Langman*. Pull it; it is a frequently cited Third Circuit case and the quoted proposition should verify easily.

**The countervailing canon in the matrimonial context — expect it:**

> "This policy of safeguarding the debtor 'is tempered, however, when the debt at issue arises from a divorce or separation agreement. ... The § 523(a)(5) and (a)(15) exceptions from discharge are thus construed more liberally than other Section 523 exceptions.'"

*In re Langman*, 465 B.R. at 403 n.2, quoting *Prensky II*, 2010 WL 2674039, at *3 (D.N.J. 2010), which in turn cites *In re Crosswhite*, 148 F.3d 879, 881 (7th Cir. 1998). `[VERIFY BEFORE FILING]` — *Prensky II* full text not retrieved.

**Practical framing:** the burden is the creditor's, the standard is preponderance, and the strict-construction canon is weakened (not eliminated) in the matrimonial context. Do not overclaim strict construction here.

## VII.C. Does the burden shift?

**On the § 523(a)(5) characterization question: no.** No case located holds that the burden shifts once the creditor-spouse makes a prima facie showing on the "nature of support" question. The creditor bears it "from start to finish." *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 761 (quoting with approval the bankruptcy court's correct statement: "The burden of proof remains on the movant in this proceeding from start to finish.").

**On the pre-BAPCPA § 523(a)(15) affirmative defenses: yes, and this is now obsolete.** Under the pre-2005 statute the burden shifted:

> "Once the non-debtor spouse meets her burden of proving that the debt was incurred in the course of a divorce by agreement or order and is not in the nature of alimony, maintenance or support, the burden shifts to the debtor to prove that one of the exceptions based on inability to pay or comparative detriment applies. In re Koons, 206 B.R. 768 (Bankr.E.D.Pa.1997)."

*Lawrence*, 237 B.R. at 83. Accord *Sanabria*, 275 B.R. at 207–08, citing *In re Crosswhite*, 148 F.3d 879, 884–85 (7th Cir. 1998).

**BAPCPA deleted both defenses, so there is nothing left to shift to.** Any brief citing *Lawrence*, *Sanabria*, *Koons*, or *Crosswhite* for burden-shifting under (a)(15) is citing dead law. Flag it if the other side does.

**One genuine burden-shifting context survives: claim objections.** If the dispute arises on an objection to a proof of claim rather than in an adversary proceeding, the *Allegheny International* framework applies:

> "[A] claim that alleges facts sufficient to support a legal liability to the claimant satisfies the claimant's initial obligation to go forward. The burden of going forward then shifts to the objector to produce evidence sufficient to negate the prima facie validity of the filed claim. ... If the objector produces sufficient evidence to negate one or more of the sworn facts in the Proof of Claim, the burden reverts to the claimant to prove the validity of the claim by a preponderance of the evidence."

*In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 785, quoting *In re Machne Menachem, Inc.*, 2011 WL 742046, at *1 (M.D. Pa. Feb. 18, 2011), which quotes *In re Allegheny Int'l, Inc.*, 954 F.2d 167, 173–74 (3d Cir. 1992) (**BINDING** as to *Allegheny International*).

**This matters procedurally.** In *Anthony* the issue reached the court on the *debtor's* motion to reclassify the ex-spouse's proof of claim, so the debtor bore the initial burden of going forward — and won anyway, because "Creditor has failed to allege any facts from which the Court could determine that such fees are in the nature of support." 453 B.R. at 787.

## VII.D. Must the creditor file an adversary proceeding? — **The critical procedural point**

### VII.D.1. The rule

**Neither § 523(a)(5) nor § 523(a)(15) is listed in § 523(c)(1).** Section 523(c)(1) reaches only paragraphs **(2), (4), and (6)**. That has three consequences.

**Consequence 1 — no 60-day deadline.** Fed. R. Bankr. P. 4007(c) applies only to "a complaint to determine whether a debt is dischargeable under §523(c)." Rule 4007(b) governs everything else:

> "**(b) Time to File; No Fee for a Reopened Case.** A complaint, except one under §523(c), may be filed at any time. If a case is reopened to permit filing the complaint, no fee for reopening is required."

Fed. R. Bankr. P. 4007(b) (**CONFIRMED verbatim**, restyled rule, Cornell LII).

> "**(c) Chapter 7, 11, 12, or 13—Time to File a Complaint Under §523(c); Notice of Time; Extension.** Except as (d) provides, a complaint to determine whether a debt is dischargeable under §523(c) must be filed within 60 days after the first date set for the §341(a) meeting of creditors. The clerk must give all creditors at least 30 days' notice of the time to file in the manner provided by Rule 2002. On a party in interest's motion filed before the time expires, the court may, after notice and a hearing, extend the time to file."

Fed. R. Bankr. P. 4007(c) (**CONFIRMED verbatim**).

> "**(a) Who May File a Complaint.** A debtor or any creditor may file a complaint to determine whether a debt is dischargeable."

Fed. R. Bankr. P. 4007(a) (**CONFIRMED verbatim**).

**Consequence 2 — the debt survives without any adversary complaint.** Because § 523(a)(5) and (a)(15) debts are excepted from discharge by operation of law and are not subject to the § 523(c)(1) "shall be discharged ... unless" default rule, they survive the discharge automatically. No complaint is required to preserve them.

**Consequence 3 — bankruptcy court jurisdiction is concurrent, not exclusive.** § 523(c)(1) is what confers *exclusive* federal jurisdiction over (a)(2), (a)(4), and (a)(6). For (a)(5) and (a)(15), state courts have concurrent jurisdiction.

### VII.D.2. Direct authority from this District

> "Section 523(a)(15) excepts from discharge debts, 'incurred by the debtor in the course of a divorce or separation or in connection with a separation agreement, divorce decree or other order of a court of record.' **Debts under § 523(a)(15) are not subject to any temporal requirement for the filing of an adversary complaint objecting to their discharge. See F.R.B.P. 4007(c). If the debt is characterized as one arising under § 523(a)(15), then the claim is not dischargeable regardless of whether an adversary proceeding is filed within a certain timeframe.**"

*In re Chaudry*, 569 B.R. 372, 374 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2017) (Gravelle, J.) (emphasis added) (PERSUASIVE — forum court, post-BAPCPA, directly on point).

*Chaudry* contrasts this with § 523(a)(6), which **is** in § 523(c)(1):

> "A debtor may be discharged from a § (a)(6) debt unless the creditor requests, upon notice and a hearing, a determination from the court that the debt may be excepted from discharge. 11 U.S.C. § 523(c)(1). Objections to the dischargeability of a debt are properly brought by way of an adversary proceeding. F.R.B.P. 4007(a); F.R.B.P. 7001(4). In a Chapter 7 case, a complaint to determine the dischargeability of a § 523(a)(6) debt must be filed no later than 60 days after the first date set for the meeting of creditors under § 341(a). F.R.B.P. 4007(c)."

*Chaudry*, 569 B.R. at 374. The whole case turned on it: because the wife's Tevis claim was held to be a § 523(a)(6) debt and no timely adversary was filed, it was discharged; had it been an (a)(15) debt, it would have survived. *Id.* at 376–77.

### VII.D.3. The pre-BAPCPA contrast — do not confuse the two eras

Under the pre-2005 statute, **(15) WAS in § 523(c)(1)**, and the deadline applied:

> "Interestingly, unlike § 523(a)(5) a party must timely commence an adversary proceeding to except such obligations from discharge. 11 U.S.C. 523(c)(1)."

*In re Halper*, 213 B.R. 279, 282 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1997) (referring to pre-BAPCPA § 523(a)(15)).

And the pre-BAPCPA rule enforced against a creditor:

> "The time within which to seek a determination of the non-dischargeability of a debt under § 523(a)(15) is no later than 60 days of the first date set for the meeting of creditors. See Fed. R. Bankr.P. 4007(c). ... [T]he Court holds that it is too late now — and was too late at the time that the Defendants sought to enforce the debt post-discharge — for the Defendants to argue that the Debtor's obligations to Weissberg are non-dischargeable under Section 523(a)(15), as that basis is time-barred."

*DiGeronimo v. Weissberg (In re DiGeronimo)*, 354 B.R. 625, 633 (Bankr. E.D.N.Y. 2006) (PERSUASIVE — out of circuit; **pre-BAPCPA case, applying the pre-BAPCPA statute**).

*DiGeronimo* also stated the negative-implication rule that survives BAPCPA in structure if not in list:

> "The Bankruptcy Rules provide that any request to determine dischargeability under Sections 523(a)(2), (4), (6), or (15) must be filed within 60 days after the first date set for the meeting of creditors. See Fed. R. Bankr.P. 4007(c). **By negative implication, there is no time limit within which to file determinations as to non-dischargeability under Sections 523(a)(1), (3), (5), (7) through (14), or (16) through (18).** In re Taibbi, 213 B.R. 261, 273 (Bankr.E.D.N.Y.1997). **This also implies that bankruptcy courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction to determine non-dischargeability under those sections.** Id."

*DiGeronimo*, 354 B.R. at 633 (emphasis added). Post-BAPCPA, (15) simply moves from the first list to the second.

**Concurrent state-court jurisdiction over § 523(a)(5), confirmed:**

> "State courts have concurrent jurisdiction to determine the dischargeability of a debt under § 523(a)(5), DiGeronimo v. Weissberg (In re DiGeronimo), 354 B.R. 625, 641 (Bankr.E.D.N.Y.2006), and therefore it follows that the State Court has concurrent jurisdiction to determine whether the exempt property is liable for such debt under § 522(c)(1). ... Although the Bankruptcy Code contains certain exceptions to this general rule (e.g., § 523(c)(1), conferring exclusive jurisdiction on federal courts to determine the dischargeability of debts under § 523(a)(2), (a)(4), and (a)(6)), no such exception applies here."

*In re Tarone*, 434 B.R. 41, 50 (Bankr. E.D.N.Y. 2010) (PERSUASIVE — out of circuit).

### VII.D.4. **The procedural consequences, stated plainly**

**For the debtor (this is uncomfortable and must be said internally):**
1. **There is no deadline to run out.** A creditor-spouse who never files an adversary proceeding does not lose. She can raise (a)(5)/(a)(15) in the New Jersey Family Part years after discharge, and the Family Part has concurrent jurisdiction to decide it.
2. **A "no adversary filed, therefore discharged" argument is available only for § 523(a)(2), (4), and (6) claims** — for example a marital tort / Tevis claim, as in *Chaudry*. It is **not** available against an equitable-distribution or DSO claim.
3. **The debtor may want to be the plaintiff.** Rule 4007(a): "A debtor or any creditor may file a complaint to determine whether a debt is dischargeable." An affirmative declaratory adversary lets the debtor choose the forum (bankruptcy court rather than the Family Part), choose the timing, and obtain a binding characterization that fixes the § 522(c)(1), § 507(a)(1), and § 362(b)(2) consequences before the state court acts. The tradeoff is that the debtor loses the burden-of-proof advantage only if the court treats him as the movant; under *Gianakas*, the party asserting nondischargeability still bears the burden regardless of caption, 917 F.2d at 761 — but **confirm this is how a declaratory posture is treated before relying on it.**
4. If the case is reopened to file the complaint, **no reopening fee is required**. Rule 4007(b).

**For the creditor-spouse:**
1. Nothing is lost by delay as to (a)(5)/(a)(15) — but everything else is at risk. In particular, a marital-tort component must be brought under (a)(6) within 60 days or it is gone. *Chaudry*, 569 B.R. at 376–77.
2. A DSO determination should be pursued promptly if she wants the § 522(c)(1) exempt-property remedy and the § 362(b)(2) stay exceptions to be available while the case is open.

# PART VIII — COUNTER-AUTHORITY: STEEL-MANNING THE CREDITOR-SPOUSE

The strongest version of the creditor-spouse's case has five pillars. Each is set out at full strength, then answered.

## VIII.A. Pillar 1 — "§ 101(14A)(C)(i) expressly names the property settlement agreement"

**The argument.** Congress wrote into the definition of "domestic support obligation" that the obligation may be "established ... by reason of applicable provisions of ... a separation agreement, divorce decree, **or property settlement agreement**." § 101(14A)(C)(i). Congress plainly contemplated that PSAs generate DSOs. A rule that PSA obligations are categorically non-DSO reads (C)(i) out of the statute.

**The answer — four independent grounds.**
1. **Subparagraph (C) is a *source* element, not a *nature* element.** The four subparagraphs are joined by "and." (B) independently requires that the debt be "in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support." An obligation can satisfy (C) and fail (B). *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 786 (listing the four elements as separate requirements); *In re Langman*, 465 B.R. at 404 (same).
2. **(C)(i) has obvious work to do without the creditor's reading.** Alimony and child support are routinely set forth *inside* PSAs. (C)(i) ensures that a support obligation does not lose DSO status merely because the parties documented it in a PSA rather than in a separate order. That is the whole function of the clause.
3. **The creditor's reading proves too much.** If (C)(i) made PSA obligations DSOs, then every obligation in every PSA — including a plain agreement to convey a car — would be a first-priority DSO reaching exempt property. That is not a plausible reading.
4. **It would render § 523(a)(15) a nullity.** § 523(a)(15) covers debts "incurred by the debtor ... in connection with a separation agreement, divorce decree or other order of a court of record" that are "not of the kind described in paragraph (5)." If every PSA obligation were a paragraph (5) debt, (a)(15) would have no field of operation. Statutes are not read to nullify neighboring provisions.

## VIII.B. Pillar 2 — "The label is irrelevant: § 101(14A)(B) says 'without regard to whether such debt is expressly so designated'"

**The argument.** The statute directs the court to disregard designations. The MSA's "Equitable Distribution" heading is exactly the kind of designation Congress told courts to ignore. *Gianakas* agrees: "the court must look beyond the label attached to an obligation by a settlement agreement to examine its true nature," 917 F.2d at 762. The debtor's whole case rests on a label the statute says to ignore.

**The answer — three grounds.**
1. **"Without regard to" means "not conclusive," not "inadmissible."** *Gianakas* makes the *language of the agreement* the **first** of the three indicators: "First, the court must examine the language and substance of the agreement." 917 F.2d at 762. A rule that made the agreement's language irrelevant would contradict indicator one.
2. **In-circuit courts treat the label as highly probative.** "The most compelling indication of the purpose and nature of the ongoing $680.00 per month obligation is the label given to it in the MSA." *In re Miller*, 501 B.R. at 282–83.
3. **This District has relied on placement within an "Equitable Distribution" heading as evidence of non-support.** *In re Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 787. If the statute forbade attention to designation, *Anthony* would have been wrongly decided.

**The precise reconciliation:** the label is not *dispositive*, but it *is* evidence, and it carries the day unless the party attacking it produces evidence of divergent substance as of the time of the agreement.

## VIII.C. Pillar 3 — "*Gianakas* itself held that an obligation sitting in the property portion of a settlement was support"

**The argument, and it is the creditor's best.** *Gianakas* is binding, and in *Gianakas* the very obligation held to be support — assumption of the second mortgage — was structurally a property-transfer provision. It appeared alongside the conveyance of the marital home. The Third Circuit still held it was support, at 764, and said "[t]he great weight of authority holds that a spouse's assumption of mortgage debts which enable members of the family to remain in the marital residence is an obligation in the nature of support." The lesson is that structural placement loses to functional reality.

**The answer — this is a distinguishing exercise, not a refutation. Four grounds:**
1. **Function.** In *Gianakas* the payment kept a **non-owner payee and four minor children housed**. 917 F.2d at 763–64. A buyout of the payee's equity in a residence the **payor** keeps has the opposite function: it liquidates the payee's capital interest, not her housing.
2. **The financial-circumstances indicator.** "Karen had no income at the time of the divorce" and had custody of four children. *Id.* at 763. That is the second indicator at its strongest. Where the payee is employed, childless or without custody, and comparably situated, the second indicator points the other way. *Id.*
3. **The obligation ran to a *third-party lender*.** *Gianakas* involved assumption of a mortgage debt owed to a bank, which meant that non-payment threatened foreclosure and loss of shelter. A direct-pay buyout obligation between spouses carries no such consequence.
4. **The separate-provisions point.** *Gianakas* also included express alimony and child support. But there the alimony was $200/month and terminated in 1988; the court found the second-mortgage payment necessary because the express support was inadequate to keep her housed. *Id.* at 763–64. Where an MSA contains a **fully adequate and independent** alimony/child support scheme, the *Buccino/Gianakas* subsidiary factor "whether the support award would have been adequate absent the liability in question," 917 F.2d at 762 n.1, points strongly toward property division. That is exactly what *Sanabria* relied on: "the agreement made provisions for both alimony and child support, which were separate from the section providing for the $350,000." 275 B.R. at 207.

## VIII.D. Pillar 4 — "Property division *is* support: *Gianakas* said so"

**The argument.** *Gianakas* adopted the Pennsylvania Superior Court's observation:

> "As the Pennsylvania Superior Court noted, even an obligation designated as property settlement may be related to support because state courts often will adjust alimony awards depending on the nature and amount of marital assets available for distribution. Buccino, 580 A.2d at 18-19. In fact, 'property division often achieves the same goal as alimony, i.e., support.' Id."

*Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763. The Third Circuit has therefore endorsed the proposition that property division and support are functionally interchangeable, and a larger property settlement in exchange for reduced alimony is the paradigm.

**The answer — four grounds.**
1. **Read the sentence in place.** It appears inside the discussion of **indicator one**, as a *caution against over-reading the label*. It is a reason to look at substance; it is not a holding that property division *is* support. If it were, the very sentence *Gianakas* is construing — "as distinguished from a property settlement," 917 F.2d at 762 — would be incoherent.
2. **It is a quotation of a state intermediate appellate court's generalization about state practice**, not a holding of the Third Circuit. It is quoted, with attribution, from *Buccino*.
3. **The "trade" premise is factually testable and the creditor must prove it.** The argument only works if the record shows the parties actually traded reduced alimony for a larger property settlement. That is the paradigm Congress described in the 1994 House Report ("In other cases, spouses have agreed to lower alimony based on a larger property settlement," H.R. Rep. No. 103-835 at 54, quoted in *Halper*, 213 B.R. at 282). Absent evidence of that trade, the argument is a syllogism without a minor premise. **The creditor bears the burden.** *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 761.
4. **Congress's own premise was the opposite.** The reason § 523(a)(15) exists at all is Congress's finding that hold-harmless and property-settlement obligations "are not found to be in the nature of alimony, maintenance, or support" and therefore "are dischargeable under current law." H.R. Rep. No. 103-835 at 54. Congress legislated on the premise that property division is *not* support.

## VIII.E. Pillar 5 — the best *cases* the creditor-spouse will cite

### VIII.E.1. *In re King*, 461 B.R. 789 (Bankr. D. Alaska 2010)

**PERSUASIVE — out of circuit.** Best available case for the proposition that a **lump-sum judgment entered under a "Property Division" caption is a DSO**.

> "Although application of the Shaver and Combs factors leads to mixed results, I find that Marcus's debt to Angel is in the nature of support. A bankruptcy court is to look to the nature of the obligation without regard to whether the debt is designated support or property settlement. The labels used by the state court, e.g., 'an evidentiary hearing regarding property issues' and 'findings of fact and conclusions of law regarding property division,' are not dispositive. It is clear that the primary concern of the superior court was the preservation of Angel's home."

*King*, 461 B.R. at 800.

**Distinguishable on six independent grounds:**
1. Out-of-circuit; the court applied the Ninth Circuit's *Shaver* and the BAP's *Combs* factors, not *Gianakas*.
2. The obligation ran to a **second mortgage on the payee's own residence** — squarely *Gianakas* pattern (A), which is support even in the Third Circuit.
3. The state court **made express findings** that the payee "was at substantial risk of losing her home" and "needed monthly payments from the debtor in order to retain the home." *Id.*
4. **There was no meaningful property division to speak of**: "The parties had kept their finances separate during their short marriage and there were no other assets or debts to divide other than the second mortgage." *Id.*
5. The court **acknowledged that the factors were "mixed"** and that the obligation did **not** terminate on death or remarriage. *Id.* at 799. It found support despite those factors, not because of them.
6. Marital duration was short and there was a pre-existing note in which the debtor had agreed to assume the entire balance on dissolution. *Id.* at 800.

### VIII.E.2. *Prensky II* — *Clair, Griefer LLP v. Prensky*, 2010 WL 2674039 (D.N.J. June 30, 2010) (Wolfson, J.)

**PERSUASIVE — D.N.J. district court, the appellate court for Bankr. D.N.J. This is the creditor's most dangerous local authority.** As described in *Langman*:

> "The District Court went further and held that 'even if the Bankruptcy Court had characterized the [indebtedness] as a § 523(a)(5) domestic support obligation, the same outcome would result,' despite the debtor's argument that the indebtedness was not in the 'nature of support' because it was not so characterized in the divorce court's decree. Judge Wolfson rejected that argument, citing § 101(14A)(B) and holding that the substance of the divorce court's decision, which contained references to 'the less monied spouse' and its intent to level the playing field, created a nondischargeable domestic support obligation pursuant to § 523(a)(5)."

*In re Langman*, 465 B.R. at 407, describing *Prensky II*.

**Distinguishable on four grounds:**
1. **The passage is expressly alternative and hypothetical.** The court had already affirmed nondischargeability under § 523(a)(15); the DSO discussion begins "even if." It is dicta.
2. **The state court's own language supplied the support finding.** The decree referred to "the less monied spouse" and to leveling the playing field — express need-based language. *Id.* An MSA that uses valuation and asset-allocation language rather than need language is not analogous.
3. **The obligation was a matrimonial *counsel fee* award**, not an equitable-distribution buyout. Counsel fee awards are the one category this District treats as presumptively support. *Lawrence*, 237 B.R. at 84–85.
4. It was punitive in part — the fee award's stated purpose included penalizing the debtor "for failing to abide by numerous matrimonial court orders, including pendente lite support orders." *In re Prensky*, 416 B.R. 406, 410 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2009), as described in *Langman*, 465 B.R. at 407.

`[VERIFY BEFORE FILING]` — **I could not retrieve the full text of *Prensky II*, 2010 WL 2674039 (D.N.J. 2010).** Everything above is as characterized by *In re Langman*, 465 B.R. at 407, whose full text I did read. **This is a district court decision in the anticipated forum's appellate chain and it must be pulled and read in full from Westlaw or PACER before any brief addresses it.** Treat as the single highest-priority verification item in this memo.

### VIII.E.3. *In re Golio*, 393 B.R. 56 (Bankr. E.D.N.Y. 2008) — the policy passage

**PERSUASIVE — out of circuit, but quoted approvingly by Bankr. D.N.J.** The creditor will quote:

> "The enactment of subsection 523(a)(15), and the increase in the scope of the discharge exception effected by the 2005 amendments, expresses Congress's recognition that the economic protection of dependent spouses and children under state law is no longer accomplished solely through the traditional mechanism of support and alimony payments.... Property settlement arrangements are often 'important components of the protection afforded individuals who, during the marriage, depended on the debtor for their economic well-being.'"

*Golio*, 393 B.R. at 61, quoted in *Langman*, 465 B.R. at 406.

**The answer.** This passage supports **nondischargeability**, which the debtor concedes in Chapter 7. It does **not** support DSO *characterization*. Read in full, *Golio* says the opposite: it says the distinction is "of no practical consequence in determining the dischargeability of the debt" *because* (a)(15) already covers property settlements. *Golio*, 393 B.R. at 61. That is the debtor's position, not the creditor's.

### VIII.E.4. *In re Yeates*, 807 F.2d 874 (10th Cir. 1986), as adopted in *Gianakas*

The creditor will cite the *Gianakas*-endorsed proposition that "a debt could be in the 'nature of support' under section 523(a)(5) even though it would not legally qualify as alimony or support under state law." *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762, quoting *Yeates*, 807 F.2d at 878.

**The answer.** True, and conceded. It establishes that state-law labels are not conclusive — which is a rule about *conclusiveness*, not about *outcome*. It does not tell the court what the answer is; it tells the court to apply the three indicators. Apply them.

### VIII.E.5. Cases the creditor may cite that do NOT help her

- ***In re Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d 647 (3d Cir. 2014)** — she will cite it for "equitable distribution claims are nondischargeable in Chapter 7," 745 F.3d at 649. **That sentence is about § 523(a)(15), and the same paragraph records the concession that the claim "is not entitled to priority as a domestic support obligation."** The case affirmatively helps the debtor on characterization.
- ***In re Lawrence*, 237 B.R. 61 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1999)** — she will cite the holding that "Ms. Lawrence's monetary claim for equitable distribution is therefore non-dischargeable in bankruptcy," 237 B.R. at 83. **That holding is expressly under § 523(a)(15).** *Id.*
- ***In re Chaudry*, 569 B.R. 372 (Bankr. D.N.J. 2017)** — she will cite "[d]ebts for equitable distribution and support cannot be incurred other than in connection with a divorce or separation agreement," 569 B.R. at 376. **The very next sentence assigns them to § 523(a)(15).**

## VIII.F. The counter-authority I could NOT find — stated for candor

- **No Third Circuit decision, precedential or non-precedential, holding that a lump-sum equitable distribution obligation is a § 523(a)(5) DSO.** None located.
- **No Bankr. D.N.J. decision holding that a New Jersey equitable distribution award is a DSO.** None located. The District's decisions run uniformly the other way: *Sanabria*, *Lawrence*, *Langman*, *Chaudry*, *Anthony*.
- **No case located holding that a QDRO-implemented retirement division is a DSO.** The QDRO/DSO intersection appears to be unaddressed in this Circuit.
- **No Third Circuit or Bankr. D.N.J. case turning squarely on the death/remarriage termination factor.** In-circuit support for the factor is *In re Miller*, 501 B.R. at 282–83 (Bankr. E.D. Pa.) and *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 762 n.1.
- **No case located holding that the presence of contractual interest is a property-division indicator.** The argument is available as inference under indicator one; it is not settled law.

# APPENDIX B — THE TOP OPEN QUESTIONS REQUIRING CASE FACTS

1. **What does the MSA actually say, and where does the obligation sit?** Section heading, defined terms, whether it recites consideration for identified property interests, whether alimony and child support appear separately and adequately. Under *Gianakas* indicator one and *Anthony*, 453 B.R. at 787, this is the first and often the last question.

2. **What were both parties' financial circumstances on the date of the MSA?** Income, employment, assets, custody, health, employability. *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763. Note that post-MSA circumstances are **irrelevant** under *Gianakas*, 917 F.2d at 763 — but the creditor will try to introduce them and the debtor may be tempted to as well. Neither should.

3. **Are there termination, modification, survivorship, security, interest, or prepayment provisions?** Collier factors (4), (5), and (7), as adopted in *In re Miller*, 501 B.R. at 282. Whether the obligation is secured by a lien on specific property is a particularly strong property-division signal and is not addressed in any located case.

4. **Is there an alimony waiver, a reduced alimony award, or evidence of a trade?** The creditor's Pillar 4 argument (Part VIII.D) lives or dies here. If alimony was waived outright, *Colin* is a near-perfect analogue for the debtor. If alimony was reduced in exchange for a larger property settlement, the 1994 House Report paradigm quoted in *Halper*, 213 B.R. at 282, is squarely in play.

5. **Chapter, and whether it will stay that way.** Chapter 13 conversion makes § 1328(a)(2) dispositive and changes the value of the whole analysis.

6. **Is there meaningful exempt property?** If yes, § 522(c)(1) is the leading argument (Part II.E). If no, the fight collapses to priority (asset case only) and stay exceptions.

7. **Asset or no-asset case?** Determines whether § 507(a)(1)(A) priority matters at all.

8. **Is there a marital tort (Tevis) component?** If so, *Chaudry* controls and the Rule 4007(c) 60-day deadline applies to it — a hard deadline that does **not** apply to the equitable-distribution component. Docket this immediately.

9. **Were there pre-petition payments on the obligation within the preference reach-back?** If the obligation is *not* a DSO, § 547(c)(7) does not protect them.

10. **What is the state of the matrimonial proceeding, and is there a final judgment of divorce?** *Ruitenberg*, 745 F.3d at 653, controls whether the equitable distribution interest is a pre-petition claim, which in turn drives whether § 727(b) or § 523 governs.
