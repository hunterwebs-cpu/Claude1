**5. Dischargeability, and why the "it's all support" framing matters much more than I first thought — this is an asset case with a pool over $1 million.**
I understand Kim is going to try to frame everything — Capital Account, Gulfstream, WNFW, the boat, all of it — as child support to lock in the strongest protection. Before I respond to that, I want to check my understanding against what you told me: I thought you said only child support is nondischargeable in Chapter 7. That doesn't match what I'm reading — §101(14A) defines "domestic support obligation" to include spousal support by name, and §523(a)(5) makes any DSO nondischargeable with no ability-to-pay exception. Separately, §523(a)(15) makes property-settlement debts to a former spouse nondischargeable in Chapter 7 too, post-BAPCPA (the old ability-to-pay defenses under (a)(15) were deleted by the 2005 amendments). Multiple courts, including one in this District (Langman, 465 B.R. at 406, quoting Golio, 393 B.R. at 61), have said the DSO/property-settlement distinction is "of no practical consequence in determining the dischargeability of the debt" in Chapter 7 — because both categories are nondischargeable anyway.

So calling something child support instead of property settlement doesn't change whether it's dischargeable — either way it isn't. But with an asset pool over $1 million, I don't think that means the label doesn't matter. I think it means the fight has moved to two other places, and given the size of the estate, I want to make sure we're treating these as the real battleground:

(a) §507(a)(1)(A) — priority. If Kim's claim is characterized as a DSO, it gets paid first, ahead of every other unsecured creditor in the case. If it's an (a)(15) property settlement, she's an ordinary unsecured creditor splitting the pool with everyone else. On an estate this size, that's not academic — that's the difference between her taking a large cut off the top versus sharing pro rata with the rest of the creditors.

(b) §522(c)(1) — exempt property. A DSO can reach property I've claimed as exempt; an (a)(15) property-settlement debt cannot touch exempt property at all. If any real part of the estate is in exempt form, the DSO label is what decides whether Kim can reach it or not.

Given the size of this estate, I think this is now the central legal question in the case, not a side issue — can you tell me what exemptions I've actually claimed and how much of the pool is exempt versus not, and who else is in the creditor pool and roughly what they're owed? I want to understand what's actually at stake under each characterization before we finalize a position. It also makes me wonder whether Kim opposing Nagel's motion to dismiss is connected to this — if the case gets dismissed she loses the priority and exemption advantages a DSO characterization gives her here and becomes an ordinary judgment creditor in state court instead. Let me know if that read is off base.

Can you also clarify what you meant by "only child support" — were you speaking to Chapter 13 specifically (where §1328(a)(2) discharges (a)(15) debts on plan completion but not (a)(5)), or something else? I want to make sure I'm not working from a wrong premise before this goes further.
