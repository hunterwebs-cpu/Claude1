# NJ Law on Interpretation of Attorney Retainer / Fee Agreements

**ATTORNEY WORK PRODUCT — PRIVILEGED AND CONFIDENTIAL**
**Internal research memo — not for filing**

**Matter:** *In re Mell* — Chapter 7 fee dispute (Bankr. D.N.J.)
**Question presented:** How does NJ law interpret an attorney retainer that states both a "flat" $5,000 fee and that the firm "would bill for hourly services," where the firm now bills hourly for work the client believes the flat fee covered — and where some line items predate the signed retainer.
**Date:** July 29, 2026

---

## BOTTOM LINE

The client's interpretation is **strong** under New Jersey law, for three reasons that stack:

1. **The construction rule runs against the firm.** New Jersey applies a special, one-directional canon to lawyer–client fee agreements: they are **construed against the drafting attorney** and read **"as a reasonable person in the circumstances of the client would have construed it."** The firm drafted this retainer, so the ambiguity it created (flat fee *and* hourly billing, with no clear line between them) is charged to the firm, not the client. This is a binding NJ Supreme Court rule (*Cohen*; reaffirmed in *Starkey*).

2. **The client's understanding of scope is admissible, not barred.** Even if the firm calls its retainer "clear," New Jersey admits extrinsic evidence of the surrounding circumstances and the parties' understanding to interpret a contract — *"even when the contract on its face is free from ambiguity"* (*Conway*, quoting *Schwimmer*). So the client's understanding of what the flat fee "covers" (petition, schedules, § 341 meeting, trustee conferences) is properly before the court.

3. **The pre-retainer line items are independently vulnerable.** A written fee agreement does not govern work done before it existed; and under **RPC 1.5(b)** the basis/rate of the fee must be communicated in writing "before or within a reasonable time after commencing the representation." Charging *hourly* for pre-agreement work — at a rate never communicated in writing when that work was done — is at best recoverable only in *quantum meruit* (reasonable value, four-part test), not as a contract entitlement, and the firm bears the burden of proving reasonableness.

**Three best authorities for the client:**
- ***Cohen v. Radio-Electronics Officers Union, Dist. Council 3***, 146 N.J. 140, 156, 679 A.2d 1188 (1996) — construe against the lawyer; read as a reasonable client would. **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).**
- ***Conway v. 287 Corporate Center Assocs.***, 187 N.J. 259, 268–70, 901 A.2d 341 (2006) — extrinsic evidence of the parties' understanding is admissible even for facially clear terms. **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).**
- ***Starkey v. Estate of Nicolaysen***, 172 N.J. 60, 66–68, 796 A.2d 238 (2002) — "fee agreements between lawyers and clients will be construed against the lawyers"; RPC 1.5(b) timing; *quantum meruit* fallback. **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).**

**Single strongest binding authority (verbatim holding):** *Cohen*, 146 N.J. at 156 —
> "A court should construe an agreement between a lawyer and a client 'as a reasonable person in the circumstances of the client would have construed it.'"
(quoting Restatement of the Law Governing Lawyers). The Court adopted the Restatement's three reasons "for construing agreements between lawyers and clients against the lawyer": lawyers write them; lawyers are better able to detect and repair omissions; and lawyers have a fiduciary duty to inform clients of the representation's risks, "including those unresolved by the client-lawyer agreement." *Id.* at 156–57.

## QUESTION 1 — General framework: are fee agreements ordinary contracts, or specially scrutinized?

**Answer:** They are enforceable contracts, but subject to **heightened scrutiny** and a **one-directional construction rule** that favors the client. Three sub-rules:

**(a) The attorney bears the burden of proving the agreement fair and reasonable.**
*Cohen*, 146 N.J. at 155–56:
> "We remain especially vigilant when attorneys and clients contract with each other. … Consequently, courts scrutinize contracts between attorneys and clients to ensure that they are fair. … [T]he attorney bears the burden of establishing the fairness and reasonableness of the transaction."
- **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).** Directly relevant: it is the firm's burden to show the hourly-billing reading is fair, not the client's burden to disprove it.

**(b) Agreements are construed against the drafting lawyer and read as a reasonable client would read them.**
*Cohen*, 146 N.J. at 156:
> "A court should construe an agreement between a lawyer and a client 'as a reasonable person in the circumstances of the client would have construed it.' … Those principles apply as readily to retainer agreements as to other agreements between lawyers and clients."

*Cohen*, 146 N.J. at 156 (adopting Restatement's rationale, verbatim):
> "Three reasons support this rule. First, lawyers almost always write such agreements … and an agreement traditionally is interpreted against its author. Second, lawyers are more able than most clients to detect and repair omissions in client-lawyer agreements. Third, lawyers have a fiduciary obligation to inform clients about the risks of the representation, including those unresolved by the client-lawyer agreement."
- **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).** This is the anchor authority for the client. (The Restatement provision Cohen cited as "§ 29A" of the 1996 Proposed Final Draft was finalized as **Restatement (Third) of the Law Governing Lawyers § 18**.)

**(c) The NJ Supreme Court has restated the rule flatly.**
*Starkey v. Estate of Nicolaysen*, 172 N.J. 60, 67, 796 A.2d 238 (2002):
> "The general rule of construction is that fee agreements between lawyers and clients will be construed against the lawyers." (citing *Cohen*, 146 N.J. at 156, and *Vaccaro v. Estate of Gorovoy*, 303 N.J. Super. 201, 207, 696 A.2d 724 (App. Div. 1997) (noting that attorneys cannot benefit from failure to comply with the requirements of RPC 1.5 by enforcing an agreement that violates that rule)).
- **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).** This is the cleanest one-sentence statement of the client-favorable canon; *Vaccaro* is the App. Div. companion.

**Fiduciary framing (reinforcing):** *Kamaratos v. Palias*, 360 N.J. Super. 76, 84, 821 A.2d 531 (App. Div. 2003), quoting *In re Honig*, 10 N.J. 74, 78 (1952):
> "All fiduciaries are held to a duty of fairness, good faith and fidelity, but an attorney is held to an even higher degree of responsibility in these matters than is required of all others."
- **Binding (App. Div. / NJ Sup. Ct.).**

**How it supports the client:** The firm drafted the retainer; the firm carries the burden; the ambiguity the firm created is resolved against the firm and in favor of the reasonable client's reading (flat fee covers the core Chapter 7 work).

## QUESTION 2 — Ambiguity and *contra proferentem* generally

**Rule:** Ambiguous contract terms are construed against the drafter. In the lawyer–client context this general canon is *reinforced* (not merely applied) — see Question 1. The Restatement rationale adopted in *Cohen* expressly rests reason one on the ordinary rule that "an agreement traditionally is interpreted against its author." *Cohen*, 146 N.J. at 156.

- General NJ *contra proferentem* principle (drafter bears the risk of ambiguity it created): well-settled; commonly stated as the ambiguity being resolved against the party who drafted the instrument. Leading general-contract statements include *Pacifico v. Pacifico*, 190 N.J. 258, 267 (2007) and *In re Miller's Estate*, 90 N.J. 210 (1982). **[VERIFY BEFORE FILING — pinpoint/holding not independently pulled in this memo].** For the fee-agreement context, rely on *Cohen* and *Starkey* directly, which are binding and on point, rather than on general commercial cases.

**How it supports the client:** "Flat $5,000" and "will bill for hourly services" in the same retainer, without a defined boundary, is a textbook drafter-created ambiguity. The risk of that ambiguity falls on the firm.

## QUESTION 3 — When may a court consider extrinsic evidence? (latent ambiguity / parol evidence)

**Answer:** New Jersey follows the **expansive (Corbin) view**. Extrinsic evidence of surrounding circumstances is admissible to interpret a contract's meaning **even when the writing looks facially clear** — and specifically to determine the scope of an attorney's engagement.

**Leading authority — *Conway v. 287 Corporate Center Assocs.*, 187 N.J. 259, 901 A.2d 341 (2006):**
The case itself arose from a **retainer agreement** whose scope/bonus terms were disputed. The Court (quoting *Atlantic Northern Airlines, Inc. v. Schwimmer*, 12 N.J. 293, 301–02, 96 A.2d 652 (1953)) held, *Conway*, 187 N.J. at 269:
> "[E]vidence of the circumstances is always admissible in aid of the interpretation of an integrated agreement. This is so even when the contract on its face is free from ambiguity. The polestar of construction is the intention of the parties to the contract as revealed by the language used, taken as an entirety; and, in the quest for the intention, the situation of the parties, the attendant circumstances, and the objects they were thereby striving to attain are necessarily to be regarded. … Such evidence is adducible only for the purpose of interpreting the writing—not for the purpose of modifying or enlarging or curtailing its terms, but to aid in determining the meaning of what has been said."

*Conway*, 187 N.J. at 269–70:
> "This Court has followed Professor Corbin's expansive view. … Within the constraints described in *Schwimmer*, we allow a thorough examination of extrinsic evidence in the interpretation of contracts. Such evidence may 'include consideration of the particular contractual provision, an overview of all the terms, the circumstances leading up to the formation of the contract, custom, usage, and the interpretation placed on the disputed provision by the parties' conduct.'" (quoting *Kearny PBA Local #21 v. Town of Kearny*, 81 N.J. 208, 221, 405 A.2d 393 (1979)).

*Conway*, 187 N.J. at 270:
> "In sum, we permit a broad use of extrinsic evidence to achieve the ultimate goal of discovering the intent of the parties. Extrinsic evidence may be used to uncover the true meaning of contractual terms. It is only after the meaning of the contract is discerned that the parol evidence rule comes into play to prohibit the introduction of extrinsic evidence to vary the terms of the contract."
- **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).** *Schwimmer* is the foundational 1953 NJ Supreme Court source of the "always admissible … even when … free from ambiguity" rule.

**Retainer-specific extrinsic factors — *Cohen*, 146 N.J. at 160** (quoted in *Kamaratos*, 360 N.J. Super. at 83):
> "[C]ourts may consider the circumstances in which the agreement was made, the parties' past practices and agreements, the extent to which they actually negotiated the agreement, and the client's level of sophistication or experience in retaining and compensating lawyers."
- **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).**

**Parties' conduct as evidence of meaning — *Kamaratos*, 360 N.J. Super. at 85:**
> "The manner in which the parties by their conduct indicate their own understanding of their agreement is an appropriate source to which to turn to determine the scope and meaning of their agreement." (citing *State Troopers Fraternal Assoc. v. New Jersey*, 149 N.J. 38, 50 (1997)).
- **Binding (App. Div.).**

**How it supports the client:** The firm cannot foreclose the client's scope argument by asserting the retainer is "clear." Under *Conway/Schwimmer*, the client's understanding of what the flat fee covered — and any course of dealing, intake communications, or industry custom (flat fees in consumer Chapter 7 routinely cover petition, schedules, § 341 meeting) — is admissible to interpret the scope split. This is the doctrinal gateway that lets the client's "understanding" into evidence.

## QUESTION 4 — RPC 1.5

### (a) Reasonableness standard and factors (verbatim)

RPC 1.5(a): **"A lawyer's fee shall be reasonable."** The factors to be considered in determining the reasonableness of a fee include:
> "(1) the time and labor required, the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, and the skill requisite to perform the legal service properly;
> (2) the likelihood, if apparent to the client, that the acceptance of the particular employment will preclude other employment by the lawyer;
> (3) the fee customarily charged in the locality for similar legal services;
> (4) the amount involved and the results obtained;
> (5) the time limitations imposed by the client or by the circumstances;
> (6) the nature and length of the professional relationship with the client;
> (7) the experience, reputation, and ability of the lawyer or lawyers performing the services;
> (8) whether the fee is fixed or contingent."
- **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct. rule).** Verification: text CONFIRMED against secondary reproductions of the NJ rule; the opening clause historically reads "A lawyer's fee shall be reasonable." **[VERIFY BEFORE FILING — some sources quote the ABA-style opener "A lawyer shall not make an agreement for, charge, or collect an unreasonable fee." Confirm the current NJ opener against the official njcourts.gov rule text, especially post-2025 amendments.]**

### (b) Timing / writing requirement (verbatim)

RPC 1.5(b):
> "When the lawyer has not regularly represented the client, the basis or rate of the fee shall be communicated in writing to the client before or within a reasonable time after commencing the representation."
- **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct. rule).** Text CONFIRMED.
- **2025 amendment:** Effective **April 1, 2025**, the NJ Supreme Court amended RPC 1.5(b) to also require that a **material change** in the basis or rate of the fee be communicated to the client in writing. **[VERIFY BEFORE FILING — quote the exact amended (b) language from the official March 6/7, 2025 Notice and Order (n250307a) before relying on it.]** If applicable, this cuts hard against the firm: switching core Chapter 7 tasks from "flat" to "hourly" is a material change in the basis/rate that had to be communicated in writing.

**Judicial gloss on (b) timing — *Starkey*, 172 N.J. at 67:**
> "We are satisfied that execution of a written fee agreement thirty-three months after representation has commenced is not within a reasonable time as required by RPC 1.5(b)."
- **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).** A fee agreement executed *after* the work is not "within a reasonable time" for that earlier work.

**How it supports the client:** (i) Reasonableness factor (8) — "whether the fee is fixed or contingent" — recognizes the fixed-fee character the client relied on. (ii) For any invoice line item performed **before** the retainer was signed, the "basis or rate" of an *hourly* fee was not communicated in writing when that work was done; the firm cannot bootstrap a later writing to make pre-agreement hourly work a contractual entitlement.

## QUESTION 5 — Flat/fixed-fee scope ambiguity: who bears the risk?

**Answer:** The **drafting firm** bears the risk of ambiguity about what a flat fee "includes."

- **Construction against the drafter applies to scope, not just price.** *Cohen*'s command to read the agreement "as a reasonable person in the circumstances of the client would have construed it," 146 N.J. at 156, governs *what the fee covers*, because scope and fee are inseparable in a flat-fee deal. A reasonable Chapter 7 client paying a "flat $5,000" understands it to cover the ordinary, expected work of the filing itself.
- **The firm had the tools and duty to define scope precisely and did not.** *Cohen*, 146 N.J. at 156 (reason two: lawyers "are more able than most clients to detect and repair omissions"; reason three: fiduciary duty to inform the client of risks "including those unresolved by the client-lawyer agreement"). A firm that wanted most work billed hourly had to say so clearly; leaving the flat/hourly line undefined is the firm's omission.
- **Scope limitations are permitted but must be placed in the retainer.** *Kamaratos*, 360 N.J. Super. at 82–83:
  > "[A]ttorneys may properly place within the retainer agreement a limitation on the scope of the representation to be provided." (citing *Lerner v. Laufer*, 359 N.J. Super. 201, 217–19, 819 A.2d 471 (App. Div. 2003)).
  - **Binding (App. Div.).** The corollary favors the client: a scope/flat-fee limitation the firm wants enforced must be *in* the retainer and clear. An undefined boundary is construed against the firm.

**How it supports the client:** Because the retainer does not clearly carve the flat fee down to a narrow set of tasks, the reasonable-client reading controls: the flat $5,000 covers the direct Chapter 7 filing work (petition, schedules, § 341 meeting, trustee conferences), and only genuinely ancillary/out-of-scope matters are hourly. The firm — as drafter and fiduciary — bears the risk of the boundary it failed to draw.

## QUESTION 6 — Pre-engagement billing (work done before the retainer was signed)

Three sub-issues:

**(a) The written agreement does not govern pre-execution work.**
A contract speaks from its formation forward; work performed before the retainer existed is not "services rendered under" the agreement. Combined with *Starkey*'s holding that a writing executed after work has begun is not "within a reasonable time" for that earlier work, 172 N.J. at 67, the firm cannot treat a later-signed retainer as the *contractual* basis for charging (especially hourly) for pre-signing work. **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).**

**(b) *Quantum meruit* is the fallback — and it is the firm's burden.**
*Starkey*, 172 N.J. at 68, adopting the four-part test:
> "To recover under a theory of *quantum meruit*, a plaintiff must establish: '(1) the performance of services in good faith, (2) the acceptance of the services by the person to whom they are rendered, (3) an expectation of compensation therefor, and (4) the reasonable value of the services.'" (quoting *Longo v. Shore & Reich, Ltd.*, 25 F.3d 94, 98 (2d Cir. 1994); accord *Weichert Co. Realtors v. Ryan*, 128 N.J. 427, 437–38, 608 A.2d 280 (1992)).
- **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).**
- *Quantum meruit* "rests on the equitable principle that a person shall not be allowed to enrich himself unjustly at the expense of another." *Weichert*, 128 N.J. at 437 (quoted in *Starkey*).
- Where an attorney's fee agreement is unenforceable, recovery is limited to the **reasonable value** of services — not the firm's preferred hourly contract rate. *Glick v. Barclays De Zoete Wedd, Inc.*, 300 N.J. Super. 299, 310, 313, 692 A.2d 1004 (App. Div. 1997) (quantum meruit where attorney rendered services in good faith but lacked a compliant written fee agreement). **Binding (App. Div.).**

**(c) RPC 1.5(b)'s timing requirement bears directly on pre-agreement charges.**
*Starkey* holds that an attorney "cannot benefit from failure to comply with the requirements of RPC 1.5 by enforcing an agreement that violates that rule," 172 N.J. at 67 (quoting *Vaccaro*, 303 N.J. Super. at 207). Because the *hourly basis/rate* was not communicated in writing "before or within a reasonable time after commencing the representation" for the pre-signing work, the firm cannot enforce an hourly contract charge for that period; at most it may seek reasonable value in *quantum meruit*, subject to the reasonableness factors of RPC 1.5(a) and the firm's burden of proof.

**How it supports the client:** Every invoice line item dated **before** the retainer's execution should be reclassified out of the contract entirely. The firm's remedy, if any, is capped at the reasonable value of those specific services (which it must prove), not its hourly rate, and reasonableness is assessed against the flat-fee understanding the client reasonably held.

## QUESTION 7 — Reasonable expectations / good faith

**Answer:** New Jersey's construction rule for fee agreements *is itself* a reasonable-expectations rule, and the implied covenant of good faith reinforces it.

- **Reasonable-expectations-of-the-client standard (binding, retainer-specific):** *Cohen*, 146 N.J. at 156 — construe the agreement "as a reasonable person in the circumstances of the client would have construed it." This is the doctrinal home for reading the ambiguous flat/hourly split the way a reasonable Chapter 7 client would understand it. **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).**
- **Fiduciary duty to inform / disclose scope and fee basis:** *Cohen*, 146 N.J. at 157 — "The lawyer must explain at the outset the basis and rate of the fee. In addition, the lawyer should advise the client of … the scope of representation, and the implications of the agreement." A firm that failed to make the flat/hourly boundary clear cannot later exploit that failure. **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).**
- **Implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing:** Every NJ contract contains an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing; a party may not exercise discretion under a contract in a way that deprives the other of the reasonably expected fruits of the bargain. Leading statements: *Sons of Thunder, Inc. v. Borden, Inc.*, 148 N.J. 396 (1997); *Wilson v. Amerada Hess Corp.*, 168 N.J. 236 (2001). **Binding (NJ Sup. Ct.).** **[VERIFY BEFORE FILING — pinpoints/holdings not independently pulled in this memo.]** Applied here: even if the retainer gave the firm some discretion to bill hourly, using that discretion to swallow the core work a reasonable client expected the flat fee to cover would defeat the client's reasonably expected benefit of the bargain.

**How it supports the client:** Both the *Cohen* reasonable-client canon and the implied covenant point the same direction — the ambiguous fee term should be read to protect, not defeat, what a reasonable Chapter 7 client expected the flat $5,000 to buy.
