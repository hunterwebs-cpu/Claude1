## 1. INTRODUCTION

This memo covers five questions: the Court's power to clarify or modify conditions before any violation, and the four substantive arguments. Short version:

- **The Court has the power, and the motion is ripe.** Section 3583(e)(2) lets the Court "modify, reduce, or enlarge" conditions "at any time." The Second Circuit has said a supervisee need not show changed circumstances (*Parisi*). The Rule 32.1 advisory committee note, which *Kunz* quotes, says he "should be able to obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it." The main limit is *Lussier*: § 3583(e)(2) is not a way to attack the *legality* of the sentence itself. The motion should therefore be framed as (a) clarification of what the Judgment means, (b) relief from Probation and provider rules that go beyond the Judgment (the *Browder*/*Kunz* line), and (c) modification grounded in the § 3553(a) factors. It should not be framed as a claim that the 2018 Judgment was unlawful.
- **Argument 1 (risk notification) is the strongest argument.** The W.D.N.Y. Amended Standing Order of March 22, 2019 did not just "vacate" Standard Condition 12. It **replaced** it, in every judgment in the District, with a condition that applies only after "the court determines in consultation with your probation officer" that the supervisee poses a risk of committing further crimes against another person. *Traficante* (2d Cir. 2020), an appeal from Judge Larimer himself, holds that the order amends all W.D.N.Y. judgments and makes any duty to notify "wholly contingent on a subsequent determination by the district court." Unless the Court has made that determination, and we have found no sign that it has, nobody can presently require Jeff to tell anyone anything. The treatment contract's partner-disclosure and "informed consent" clause, and any instruction to put the conviction on a dating profile, are attempts to impose that obligation without the required court finding. *Reeves* (2d Cir. 2010) vacated a court-imposed condition that required a child-pornography possessor to tell romantic partners about his conviction. It was vague, not reasonably related to sentencing goals, and burdened intimate association.
- **Argument 2 (minor contact) is strong on text.** The condition bars only "deliberate contact with any child under 18." The Second Circuit reads conditions "to exclude inadvertent violations" (*Johnson*, 446 F.3d at 281). A ban on speaking to any adult stranger online, and a rebuke for attending a 17+ event, contradict the condition rather than carry it out (*Browder*). For family gatherings, the condition itself lets the **Court** approve contact. Jeff can therefore ask the Court directly to approve specified family gatherings, and *Jenkins*' "Thanksgiving dinners or seders or christenings" passage supports that request.
- **Argument 3 (treatment contract) is strong as to provisions that conflict with or go beyond the Judgment, and weaker as a blanket rule.** *Browder* (2d Cir. 2017) and the W.D.N.Y. remand order (Vilardo, J., 2018) hold that a supervisee may object to treatment-agreement terms that are more onerous than his sentence, and that he "cannot be forced to sign or abide by agreements that reach beyond the Court's ordered conditions." Jeff's Judgment uses the very condition language Judge Vilardo re-imposed on remand in *Browder*, including "follow the rules and regulations of that program." That phrase is the government's best textual hook. The client's proposed relief is that "participate" means attend. That is vulnerable: a later *Browder* summary order (2020) upheld a violation where the supervisee was refused admission because he would not acknowledge his offense of conviction. A defensible definition is attendance plus good-faith participation, which includes discussing the offense of conviction, but with no contract term enforceable as a supervised-release condition unless it is tethered to the Judgment.
- **Argument 4 (CIMP) is mixed.** The privilege clause in the Judgment is real and enforceable. *Kunz* (2d Cir. 2023), construing the identical W.D.N.Y. condition, says that text "constrains the supervisors in their surveillance." *Kunz* also struck a CIMP term as exceeding the authority the court delegated, and *Browder*'s remand order held that the agreement's "have access to" device reach exceeds the Judgment's "will be used" text. But *Kunz* treats most approval-type CIMP terms (encryption, operating-system changes, security software) as permissible "matters of detail," construed narrowly. And *Browder* (2d Cir.) approved full-capture keystroke monitoring in principle. The best CIMP relief is targeted: a disclosed privilege protocol, a ruling that monitoring output may be used only to detect violations of court-imposed conditions, conformity of the device-reporting clause to the Judgment's text, and *Kunz*-style constructions (or blanket approvals) for antivirus, encryption, and routine maintenance. Jeff himself has said (email of Sept. 23, 2026) that he does not want to "fight the software as a whole." The research supports that instinct.
- **Counsel.** Section 3006A(a)(1)(E) says representation "shall be provided" for a financially eligible person who "faces modification, reduction, or enlargement of a condition." Rule 32.1(c)(1) gives a right to counsel at any modification hearing. Whether (a)(1)(E) is triggered by a motion the *defendant* files is not settled by any authority I located (see NOT VERIFIED).

**Scope limits.** As instructed, this memo does not address polygraph, employment, alcohol, finances, or residence. It works from primary sources: the Judgment (Drive), the Boles standing order (Drive), the treatment contract (Drive file `1hruHnKFsf6e45kYXEHsfl7nTjlPJN9fZ`, checked against EXHIBIT-B-LINE-BY-LINE.md), the CIMP Participant Agreement and release form (COMPUTER-MONITORING-RULES-TRANSCRIPTION.md), Jeff's emails of 9/19, 9/23, and 10/4/26 (Drive), and opinions read in full from the Drive "Cases" folder or the Caselaw Access Project. ## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue A: Power to act now.** Under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2) and Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1(c), may the Court clarify or modify Jeff's conditions, and rule that Probation and provider rules not found in the Judgment are unenforceable, when no violation has been charged?
**Answer: Yes,** subject to framing. Section 3583(e)(2) authorizes modification "at any time prior to the expiration" of the term. *Parisi* holds no changed circumstances are required if the Court weighs the § 3553(a) factors. *Kunz* and *Villafane-Lozada* confirm that a supervisee may "seek recourse before the sentencing court" when Probation oversteps. A challenge to authority Probation has already exercised is ripe (*Kunz*). The limit is *Lussier*: § 3583(e)(2) does not reach claims that the original condition was illegal.

**Issue B: Risk notification.** Standard Condition 12 as printed in the Judgment reads: "If the probation officer determines that you pose a risk to another person … the probation officer may require you to notify the person about the risk." The W.D.N.Y. standing order replaced that text. Jeff is now being told to disclose his history to every potential sexual partner, prove partners' "informed consent" to treatment staff, and show his conviction on dating profiles. Does any condition authorize this?
**Answer: Probably not, and this is the motion's strongest point.** The standing order, as construed in *Traficante*, makes any notification duty depend on a prior court determination. No such determination appears in the materials reviewed. Partner disclosure was held vague and unjustified in *Reeves*. Leaving notification to the probation officer's unfettered discretion was rejected in *Peterson* and *Boles*. A private provider cannot impose by contract what Probation itself cannot (*Browder*).

**Issue C: Minor contact.** The condition reads: "The defendant shall not have deliberate contact with any child under 18 years of age, excluding his biological or adopted children, unless approved by the probation officer or by the Court." Probation has banned speaking to anyone in online games whom Jeff does not personally know, faulted him for attending a 17+ event, and the winter holidays are coming. Does the condition reach this conduct?
**Answer: No, as to adults and inadvertent contact.** "Deliberate" requires purposeful contact with a known child (*Johnson*'s inadvertence rule, *Arciniega* as described in *Johnson*). An instruction barring all speech with unverified adults contradicts the condition rather than executes it (*Browder*, *Matta*). For family gatherings, the Court may approve contact itself; *Jenkins*, *Bleau*, and *Lombardi* show the Second Circuit's concern about conditions that sweep in routine family life.

**Issue D: Treatment contract.** Special Condition (b) requires Jeff to "participate in a sex offense-specific treatment program and follow the rules and regulations of that program." Special Condition (h) requires mental-health treatment. Do these make every term of R.K.K.S.'s Treatment Contract, and Probation's records-release form, enforceable conditions of supervised release?
**Answer: No, as to terms that conflict with or materially exceed the Judgment.** That is *Browder*'s holding and Judge Vilardo's remand ruling. Terms that hand significant liberty decisions to the provider or Probation (the "Treatment Team" deciding discharge; "sexually stimulating material(s) … as defined by my treatment staff"; "other problem behavior … as determined in the sole discretion of the staff") run into *Matta*, *Peterson*, *Carlineo*, *Reeves*, *Simmons*/*Farrell*, and *Eaglin*. **But** the "rules and regulations" clause and the later *Browder* summary order mean the Court will likely require real participation, not attendance alone.

**Issue E: CIMP.** Special Condition (a) provides that "[a]ny such monitoring or examinations shall be designed to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information or any private material that is not illegal…" Is the program as run, and the Participant Agreement as written, consistent with the Judgment?
**Answer: Partly no.** The privilege clause binds the monitors (*Kunz*). Using monitoring output to enforce a rule the Court never imposed (the game-speech ban) goes beyond "impermissible/suspicious activity." The agreement's "own or have access to" device reach exceeds the Judgment (*Browder* remand). The encryption, maintenance, and security-software terms are likely to be upheld as "matters of detail," but construed narrowly and open to blanket approvals (*Kunz*).

**Issue F: Counsel.** Is Jeff entitled to appointed counsel?
**Answer: Likely, at least at any contested modification hearing.** Rule 32.1(c)(1) grants a "right to counsel" at a modification hearing. Section 3006A(a)(1)(E) covers a person who "faces modification … of a condition." Whether a defendant-initiated motion triggers (a)(1)(E) is unresolved in the materials reviewed.

## 4. GOVERNING FRAMEWORK (applies to every argument)

**4.1 Statute.** Section 3583(d) permits special conditions only "to the extent that such condition— (1) is reasonably related to the factors set forth in section 3553(a)(1), (a)(2)(B), (a)(2)(C), and (a)(2)(D); (2) involves no greater deprivation of liberty than is reasonably necessary for the purposes set forth in section 3553(a)(2)(B), (a)(2)(C), and (a)(2)(D); and (3) is consistent with any pertinent policy statements issued by the Sentencing Commission." Section 3583(e) lets the court, "after considering the factors set forth in section 3553(a)(1), (a)(2)(B), (a)(2)(C), (a)(2)(D), (a)(4), (a)(5), (a)(6), and (a)(7)," "(2) … modify, reduce, or enlarge the conditions of supervised release, at any time prior to the expiration or termination of the term of supervised release, pursuant to the provisions of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure relating to the modification of probation and the provisions applicable to the initial setting of the terms and conditions of post-release supervision." (Text from LII, read Oct. 12, 2026.) Retribution, § 3553(a)(2)(A), is not on the § 3583(e) list. *Esteras v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 2031 (2025), holds that the omitted factor may not be considered on revocation (read in full from Drive). By the same statutory structure, the § 3583(e)(2) analysis should be forward-looking.

**4.2 Probation's role.** Under 18 U.S.C. § 3603, a probation officer shall "(1) instruct … as to the conditions specified by the sentencing court"; "(2) keep informed, to the degree required by the conditions specified by the sentencing court, as to the conduct and condition of" the supervisee; and "(3) use all suitable methods, not inconsistent with the conditions specified by the court, to aid" the supervisee. (Text from LII.)

**4.3 Delegation: the core rule for Arguments 1–4.**
- *United States v. Matta*, 777 F.3d 116, 122 (2d Cir. 2015) (read in full, CAP): "The power to impose special conditions of supervised release, including participation in a substance abuse program, is vested exclusively in the district court." A court "may delegate to a probation officer decisionmaking authority over certain minor details of supervised release— for example, the selection of a therapy provider or treatment schedule. … But a district court may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *Id.* at 123 (quoting *Mike*, 10th Cir.): "any condition that affects a significant liberty interest … must be imposed by the district court and supported by particularized findings that it does not constitute a greater deprivation of liberty than reasonably necessary to accomplish the goals of sentencing." *Validation:* followed in *Browder* (2017), *Carlineo* (2021), and *Kunz* (2023); no negative treatment seen.
- *United States v. Peterson*, 248 F.3d 79, 85 (2d Cir. 2001) (per curiam) (read in full, Drive): "If Peterson is required to participate in a mental health intervention only if directed to do so by his probation officer, then this special condition constitutes an impermissible delegation of judicial authority to the probation officer." Delegating "the selection of a therapy provider and schedule" is proper. *Id.* *Validation:* relied on in *Boles* (2019) and *Kunz* (2023).
- *United States v. Browder*, 866 F.3d 504, 509 (2d Cir. 2017) (read in full, Drive): "[T]he extensive 'supervision mission' of federal probation officers includes 'execut[ing] the sentence,' but not imposing it." (quoting *Matta*).
- *United States v. Carlineo*, 998 F.3d 533, 538 (2d Cir. 2021) (read in full, Drive): a court "may not delegate decision-making authority which would make a defendant's liberty contingent on a probation officer's exercise of open-ended discretion over a program no one knows much about. … In other words, the Probation Office may supervise and execute a sentence but may not fashion a sentence's terms." *Id.* at 537: the condition "failed to inform Carlineo of even the broad contours of the prohibited (and required) conduct." *Validation:* cited approvingly in *Kunz*.
- *United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748, 767–68 (2d Cir. 2023) (read in full; client transcription checked against the Drive PDF for every quote used): a CIMP term that "contradict[s] the express language of the special conditions" "exceed[s] the authority actually delegated by the district court"; the one-device term "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court, and therefore may not be enforced as written." *Id.* at 768. *Validation:* followed in *Love*, No. 24-1706-cr (2d Cir. Apr. 8, 2025) (summary order).

**4.4 Vagueness.** *United States v. Simmons*, 343 F.3d 72, 81 (2d Cir. 2003) (read in full, CAP): a supervisee has a "due process right to conditions of supervised release that are sufficiently clear to inform him of what conduct will result in his being returned to prison" (quoting *Guagliardo*). *United States v. Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77, 81 (2d Cir. 2010) (read in full, Drive): a subjective term can be saved if "tethered to objective criteria," but the condition there "has no objective baseline." *United States v. Johnson*, 446 F.3d 272, 280–81 (2d Cir. 2006) (read in full, Drive): conditions need not be "cast in letters six feet high," but must "provide clear notice of what conduct they prohibit and … not demand the impossible."

**4.5 Heightened scrutiny for fundamental interests.** *United States v. Myers*, 426 F.3d 117, 126 (2d Cir. 2005) (read in full, Drive): "If the liberty interest at stake is fundamental, a deprivation of that liberty is 'reasonably necessary' only if the deprivation is narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest." *Reeves*, 591 F.3d at 82–83, applies this to the right to intimate association. *Validation:* *Myers* cited in *Love* (2025); *Reeves* cited in *Carlineo* (2021).

## 5. DISCUSSION AND ANALYSIS

### 5.0 The Court's overall power to clarify and modify now, without a violation

**5.0.1 Statutory power.** Section 3583(e)(2) text is quoted above. The Supreme Court has said the trial court, "as it sees fit, may modify an individual's conditions of supervised release" (*United States v. Johnson*, 529 U.S. 53, 60 (2000), as quoted in *Traficante*, 966 F.3d at 105). *Myers*, 426 F.3d at 129, adds: "Myers may also move the trial court for a modification of the conditions if circumstances change. … Facts and relationships may change over the years, and the district court may wish to re-examine the special conditions at a time closer to Myers's release, when more facts will be clear." *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 512 n.33: "If less invasive means of computer supervision arise, through technological or other innovation, the defendant or the government is free to seek modification of the condition. See 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2) …" *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 774 n.14: "Kunz may at any time ask the court to 'modify, reduce, or enlarge' a restriction under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2)." A dissent in the Supreme Court's most recent supervised-release case says the same: "By law, Hunter can move for modification of his supervised-release conditions 'at any time.' 18 U.S.C. §3583(e)(2)." *Hunter v. United States*, No. 24-1063, 608 U.S. ___ (June 18, 2026) (Thomas, J., dissenting), slip op. n.1 (read from the Drive slip opinion; the majority's holding concerns appeal waivers and is not otherwise relevant). This is a dissent and should be cited, if at all, only as an illustration.

**5.0.2 No changed circumstances required.** *United States v. Parisi*, 821 F.3d 343, 347 (2d Cir. 2016) (read in full, CAP): *Lussier* "does not require new or changed circumstances relating to the defendant in order to modify conditions of release, but simply recognizes that changed circumstances may in some instances justify a modification. … So long as the court, when modifying supervised release conditions, considers the relevant 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) sentencing factors, there is no additional requirement that it make a finding of new or changed circumstances with respect to the defendant." *Validation:* cited in *Traficante* (2020). In any event Jeff does have new circumstances. Conditions written in 2018 are being applied for the first time after his July 30, 2026 release. He now has a specific provider, a specific contract, and specific Probation directives. These are exactly the details the Court could not foresee in 2018 (compare *Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d at 193 n.11, and *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 512 n.33, on deferring computer details).

**5.0.3 Clarification and recourse against Probation overreach.** Rule 32.1 advisory committee's note (1979) (read from LII; quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 774 n.14): "The probationer should have the right to apply to the sentencing court for a clarification or change of conditions. … This avenue is important for two reasons: (1) the probationer should be able to obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it; and (2) in cases of neglect, overwork, or simply unreasonableness on the part of the probation officer, the probationer should have recourse to the sentencing court when a condition needs clarification or modification." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 774 n.14 (quoting *Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d at 153): "if the probation officer does overstep his authority," the supervisee may "seek recourse before the sentencing court." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 768, itself granted that kind of relief: Probation "may not enforce any provision of the CIMP that purports to authorize the probation officer to limit Kunz to one internet-connected device." The Browder remand order (W.D.N.Y. Mar. 14, 2018) did the same on a § 3583(e)(2)-type remand: "Browder cannot be forced to sign or abide by agreements that reach beyond the Court's ordered conditions." *Lussier* itself recognizes clarification as a proper use of Rule 32.1, quoting *Kress* (3d Cir.): Rule 32.1(b) motions "are vehicles for modifying conditions of probation [or supervised release] when changing circumstances warrant it, or when terms in the order are unclear." 104 F.3d at 36.

**5.0.4 Limits the government will raise.**
- ***Lussier*: no illegality challenges under § 3583(e)(2).** *United States v. Lussier*, 104 F.3d 32, 34 (2d Cir. 1997) (read in full, Drive): "The plain language of subsection 3583(e)(2) indicates that the illegality of a condition of supervised release is not a proper ground for modification under this provision." *Id.* at 35: "Other procedures, such as a direct appeal under 18 U.S.C. § 3742 or a collateral attack under 28 U.S.C. § 2255, are available to challenge the legality of a condition of supervised release." *Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d at 152 & n.2 (as quoted in *Kunz* at 774 n.14), recognizes "an exception to this rule for new or unforeseen circumstances, which include, among other things, technological changes." *Validation:* *Lussier* remains good law. It is cited in *Kunz* (2023), and the 2020 *Browder* summary order relied on it to deem illegality arguments forfeited. **Drafting consequence:** do not argue that the 2018 conditions were unconstitutional on their face or illegally imposed. Argue (i) what the conditions mean (clarification); (ii) that Probation and provider rules not in the Judgment cannot be enforced as conditions (execution, not imposition; *Browder*, *Kunz*); and (iii) that the § 3553(a)(1), (a)(2)(B)–(D) factors favor modification (*Parisi*). The CIMP technical points can also invoke *Villafane-Lozada*'s technology exception.
- **Ripeness.** *Traficante*, 966 F.3d at 106 (read in full, Drive), quoting *Balon*: ripeness asks "whether (1) the issues are fit for judicial consideration, and (2) withholding of consideration will cause substantial hardship to the parties." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 769: a challenge to "a specific condition, imposed right now, applicable right now, and seeming to delegate authority right now" is ripe, and "the already realized delegation of judicial power to a probation officer" is reviewable. *Id.* at 774: "Should Probation make such a decision in the future, the issue will become ripe." Here Probation and the provider have already acted: the game-speech directive, the 17+ rebuke, the partner-disclosure contract term, the profile-disclosure instruction, the signed release. *Johnson*, 446 F.3d at 279, adds that a supervisee "should not be required to await and undergo a criminal prosecution as the sole means of seeking relief" (quoting *Doe v. Bolton*), and: "Unless the self-incrimination issue raised by a condition of supervised release is deemed ripe when it is imposed, an offender who believes the Fifth Amendment protects his statements must face the threat of prison." **Adverse:** *Traficante* held a vagueness/delegation challenge to the amended risk condition **unripe** because no court determination had been made, 966 F.3d at 106–07. That is no obstacle to Argument 1 as framed here. Jeff is not attacking the standing-order text. He asks the Court to declare that, absent a determination, no notification duty exists. That is the reading *Traficante* adopted.
- ***Maiorana* does not help.** *United States v. Maiorana*, 153 F.4th 306 (2d Cir. 2025) (en banc) (read in full, Drive), overruled *Truscello* and requires oral pronouncement of non-mandatory conditions, including "standard" ones. But the new rule "does not apply retroactively on collateral review." *Id.* at 314 n.11 (as stated in the opinion's footnote; the page placement comes from the dissent's citation "Ante at 314 n.11"). Do not rely on *Truscello*.
- **Hearing.** Rule 32.1(c)(1): "Before modifying the conditions of probation or supervised release, the court must hold a hearing, at which the person has the right to counsel and an opportunity to make a statement and present any information in mitigation." (c)(2): no hearing is needed if the relief "is favorable to the person and does not extend the term," and the government, having been given notice and a reasonable opportunity, does not object. (Text from LII.)

**Conclusion on 5.0:** high confidence that the Court has power to clarify and to grant favorable modifications now. Moderate risk that the government will recast parts of the motion as *Lussier*-barred illegality attacks. Careful framing removes most of that risk.

### 5.1 Argument 1: Risk notification (Standard Condition 12)

**Issue.** Can Probation or the treatment provider require Jeff to disclose his offense history to potential sexual partners, prove their "informed consent" to treatment staff before sex, and show his conviction on dating profiles?

**Rule.**
1. ***Boles* invalidated the printed condition.** *United States v. Boles*, 914 F.3d 95, 111 (2d Cir. 2019) (read in full, Drive): "we agree with Boles that the 'risk' condition is vague and affords too much discretion to the probation officer." *Id.* at 112: "we vacate the risk condition and remand to the district court to clarify the scope of the 'risk' condition." *Boles* involved a child-pornography possessor with a prior conviction for the same offense, the same profile as Jeff. *Boles* treated the condition as "largely indistinguishable from the one we struck down in *Peterson*." *Id.* at 112. *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 86: "the court must determine, rather than leaving to the discretion of the probation officer, whether such notification is required. … [The court] may not simply leave the issues of employer notification to the probation officer's unfettered discretion."
2. ***Traficante* held that the W.D.N.Y. standing order amended every judgment, and that notification now requires a court determination first.** *United States v. Traficante*, 966 F.3d 99 (2d Cir. 2020) (read in full, Drive). This was an appeal from Judge Larimer. At 100: "we agree with Traficante that the previous risk condition can no longer be imposed on him following our decision in United States v. Boles." At 104: "the standing order amends all judgments in the district to replace the prior standard risk condition with the following new condition," and "[t]he order instead clarifies that any obligation to notify at-risk individuals is wholly contingent on a subsequent determination by the district court that the supervisee poses a specific risk to such persons. Given the conditional nature of the revised condition, the standing order can have no impact on Traficante unless and until the district court makes such a finding." At 105: if the court makes "such a risk finding and impose[s] the additional burden of notification," that "'would … enlarge' the condition, in which case the supervisee 'would be entitled to a hearing' under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32.1(c)" (quoting *Murdock*). At 107, even after a finding, "the district court still might directly order Traficante to notify the at-risk individual, or alternatively, order the probation officer to require Traficante to so notify the potential victims." *Validation:* cited in *Kunz* (2023) for its ripeness discussion; no negative treatment seen.
3. **The amended condition is limited to a risk of committing *further crimes against another person*.** Disclosure to a consenting adult sexual partner is not obviously tied to any risk that Jeff will commit a crime against that partner. His convictions are for possession (and, earlier, distribution) of child pornography.
4. ***Reeves*: partner disclosure for a child-pornography possessor.** *United States v. Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77 (2d Cir. 2010) (read in full, Drive), vacated a court-imposed condition that Reeves "notify the Probation Department when he establishes a significant romantic relationship and … inform the other party of his prior criminal history concerning his sex offenses." *Id.* at 80. It was vague: "the supervised release condition has no objective baseline." *Id.* at 81. It was not reasonably related to sentencing goals: "the requirement would almost certainly adversely affect, and could very well prematurely end, any intimate relationship he might develop, placing him at a greater risk of social isolation and thus impair, rather than enhance, his rehabilitation." *Id.* at 82. And it was not narrowly tailored to the right of intimate association: "Even assuming arguendo that the goal of the condition is the protection of children, we would conclude that it is not narrowly tailored since it applies to any significant romantic relationship, even those which would not bring Reeves into contact with children." *Id.* at 83. **Adverse language in *Reeves*:** "We have no doubt that in the appropriate circumstance a court, on the recommendation of the Probation Department, could require a defendant to notify third-parties of risks arising from the defendant's criminal record, personal history, or characteristics." *Id.* at 82. That sentence actually helps Jeff's framing, because it puts the decision with *a court* on an individualized record.
5. **A provider cannot do by contract what Probation cannot do directly.** *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 514: "The key question is not whether permission, if sought, would be granted; it is whether Browder can be required, by the Probation Office or its designee, to ask in the first place. Because such a requirement … would contradict, rather than execute, Browder's sentence, the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose it." *Id.* at 513 n.37: "treatment cannot be implemented in a way that directly conflicts with other, specific conditions of his supervised release."
6. **Online profile disclosure and First Amendment access.** *United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d 88, 96 (2d Cir. 2019) (read in full, CAP): "Packingham nevertheless establishes that, in modern society, citizens have a First Amendment right to access the Internet." *Eaglin* also vacated an internet ban that the government had defended partly as a way to keep the supervisee from "identifying potential romantic or sexual partners," because monitoring "remained … a viable option." *Id.* at 98. (*Packingham v. North Carolina*, 582 U.S. 98 (2017), is cited through *Eaglin* and *Browder* n.26 and was not independently read.)

**Application.**
- Jeff's Judgment (June 2018) predates the March 2019 standing order. Under *Traficante*, the order "amends all judgments in the district," so the operative Standard Condition 12 is the amended text. Neither Probation nor the provider may require any notification unless and until "the court determines" that Jeff poses a risk of committing further crimes against another person. No such determination appears in the materials reviewed (confirm on the docket). The present duty to notify anyone is therefore **zero**.
- The contract's second Special Conditions paragraph requires "acceptable proof of informed consent … which includes disclosure of my offense history in full prior to engaging in a sexual relationship." That is a third-party risk-notification requirement, and a broader one than *Reeves* vacated. It covers every sexual partner, not just "significant romantic" ones. It requires proof to treatment staff. It is imposed by a private provider rather than a court, with no court finding at all. The profile-disclosure instruction notifies the world. If it comes from Probation, it is an exercise of the very discretion *Boles* and *Peterson* forbid. If it comes from treatment, it is a *Browder* problem.
- "Follow the rules and regulations of that program" cannot be read to override the specific, court-controlled risk condition. A general clause should not swallow the specific provision the standing order created. And *Browder* n.37 says treatment "cannot be implemented in a way that directly conflicts with other, specific conditions."
- The *Reeves* isolation finding fits Jeff closely. The client says he goes only to work and home because he fears disclosure (EXHIBIT-B notes). That fact should come in through Jeff's own declaration.

**Relief to request.** (1) A declaration that, under the Amended Standing Order as construed in *Traficante*, no risk-notification obligation currently applies because the Court has made no determination; (2) a declaration that neither Probation nor the treatment provider may require disclosure of Jeff's offense history to sexual partners, proof of partner "informed consent," or display of his conviction on any online profile, absent a court determination and an enlargement hearing under Rule 32.1(c); and (3) a ruling that the contract's partner-disclosure and informed-consent clause is not enforceable as a condition of supervised release.

**Counter-arguments.** (a) The government may say that partner disclosure is a treatment rule, not "risk notification," and is authorized by Special Condition (b). Answer: *Browder* (conflict with a specific condition); *Matta* (significant liberty interest must be imposed by the court with findings); *Reeves* (intimate association, heightened scrutiny). (b) It may say the issue is unripe under *Traficante*. Answer: here the obligation is being *enforced now* through the contract and directives. That is an already-realized delegation (*Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 769). (c) It may ask the Court to make a risk determination now. That is a real strategic risk of filing. *Traficante* says any such finding would be an enlargement requiring a Rule 32.1(c) hearing, with counsel.

**Conclusion.** Strongest argument. High confidence on the standing-order point. Moderate-to-high on the provider-contract point.

### 5.2 Argument 2: Minor contact as applied (online games, the 17+ event, family gatherings)

**Issue.** Does "deliberate contact with any child under 18" support (i) a ban on speaking with anyone in online games whom Jeff does not personally know, (ii) criticism for attending a 17+ event because minors "could have snuck in," and (iii) restrictions on family gatherings?

**Rule.**
1. **Inadvertent contact is excluded.** *Johnson*, 446 F.3d at 281: "Generally, supervised release provisions are read to exclude inadvertent violations. In Arciniega v. Freeman, the Supreme Court assumed that a term barring 'association' with ex-convicts does not cover 'incidental contacts.' 404 U.S. 4, 4 (1971) (per curiam). This rule of construction likewise applies here …" *Johnson* approved a contact condition only because, as construed, it involved "some purposeful initiative," and "no violation occurs where incidental contact is followed by immediate removal." *Id.* (*Arciniega* is cited through *Johnson* and was not independently read.) Jeff's condition is narrower than Johnson's. It already says "deliberate."
2. **Probation cannot add restrictions that contradict the condition.** *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 514. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 766–68. *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122–23. Standard Condition 13 requires following PO instructions "related to the conditions of supervision." An instruction that forbids what the condition permits is not "related to" the condition in any executing sense. (I found no Second Circuit opinion construing the "related to" limit in Standard Condition 13 itself; see NOT VERIFIED.)
3. **A significant restriction on online communication must come from the court, with findings.** *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767: a device limit "would pose a significant burden on his liberty, and therefore would need to be imposed by the court and justified by particularized on-the-record findings." *Eaglin*, 913 F.3d at 96, recognizes the First Amendment right to access the Internet. *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 83: "Although a defendant might use the telephone to commit fraud, this would not justify a condition of probation that includes an absolute bar on the use of telephones."
4. **Places restriction.** The loitering clause covers only places within 100 feet of "school yards, playgrounds, arcades or other places primarily used by children under the age of 18." *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 86, refused to read a similar clause to bar places "where children do not congregate."
5. **Family life.** *United States v. Jenkins*, 854 F.3d 181, 194 (2d Cir. 2017) (read in full, Drive): a direct-contact condition requiring a preapproval process "would apply with full force to all routine family interaction-for example, Thanksgiving dinners or seders or christenings." *United States v. Bleau*, 930 F.3d 35, 43 (2d Cir. 2019) (read in full, Drive), remanded a no-direct-contact-without-Probation-approval condition for a child-pornography defendant with no history of contact with minors. *United States v. Lombardi*, No. 17-356-cr (2d Cir. Mar. 22, 2018) (summary order) (read in full, Drive), remanded a similar condition in light of *Jenkins*. *Myers*, 426 F.3d at 125–26, treats family relationships as protected liberty interests that trigger heightened scrutiny. **Caveat:** *Jenkins*, *Bleau*, and *Lombardi* were direct appeals that attacked imposition of the conditions. Under *Lussier* they show the policy concern, not a basis to strike Jeff's condition as illegal.

**Application.**
- **Online games.** Talking to an adult stranger is not contact with a child, let alone deliberate contact. Jeff had stated that he would not talk to minors. A rule against speaking to *anyone* not personally known, justified by the impossibility of verifying age, effectively rewrites "deliberate contact with any child" as "any communication with any person not proven to be an adult." *Browder* says Probation is "plainly without power" to impose a requirement that would "contradict, rather than execute," the sentence. And if the Court wanted a general bar on online speech with strangers, *Kunz* and *Eaglin* say that is a significant restriction requiring court findings. *Johnson*'s construction supplies the workable rule: if Jeff learns or has reason to know he is communicating with a minor, he must disengage immediately. That is not a violation if he does.
- **17+ event.** An event restricted to people 17 and older is not a place "primarily used by children." The possibility that a minor "could have snuck in" describes incidental presence, which *Johnson* excludes. As Jeff points out, a grocery store poses a greater chance of encountering minors.
- **Family gatherings.** Two points. First, being present at a gathering where minors are also present is not "deliberate contact" with them. Second, and more practically, the condition permits deliberate contact "approved by the probation officer **or by the Court**." The motion can ask the Court to approve, in advance, Jeff's attendance at specified holiday gatherings with family members, with conditions such as the presence of the children's parent or guardian and no unsupervised one-on-one contact. *Jenkins*' Thanksgiving passage is the natural citation. Whether Jeff has biological or adopted children (who are excluded from the condition entirely) needs to be confirmed.
- **The contract compounds the problem.** The contract's minors paragraph requires reporting "any accidental, incidental, or intentional contact" with minors or "vulnerable populations (as defined by treatment staff)." That is exactly the kind of more onerous provision *Browder* held a supervisee may refuse. The Judgment says "deliberate."

**Relief to request.** A clarification that (1) "deliberate contact" means knowing, purposeful contact with a person Jeff knows or has reason to believe is under 18; (2) communicating online with adults, including strangers whose age is unverified, does not violate the condition, but Jeff must end contact upon learning or having reason to believe the person is a minor; (3) attending adult-oriented or general-public venues and events that are not "primarily used by children" does not violate the condition, and incidental presence of minors is not "contact"; (4) the Court approves attendance at specified family gatherings on stated terms; and (5) Probation's game-speech directive is vacated as inconsistent with the Judgment.

**Counter-arguments.** Probation will invoke Standard Condition 13, practical supervision needs, and the fact that the Court's approval clause gives Probation discretion. Answer: discretion to *approve* deliberate contact is not discretion to forbid non-contact (*Browder*). On the PSR facts (unknown), the government could argue Jeff's history justifies a court-imposed online-communication restriction. If so, that requires a Rule 32.1(c) enlargement with findings. It cannot be done by fiat.

**Conclusion.** Strong on the text and on *Johnson*. The family-gathering request is very likely to get a hearing because the Court can simply approve.

### 5.3 Argument 3: Treatment contract and "follow the rules and regulations of that program" (Special Conditions (b) and (h))

**Issue.** Does the R.K.K.S. Treatment Contract, together with Probation's release form, become enforceable supervised-release conditions through the clause "follow the rules and regulations of that program"? Can "participate" be defined?

**Rule.**
1. ***Browder* (2d Cir. 2017).** Browder's provider terminated him because he would not sign an agreement barring contact with minors, including his own children, without approval from Probation *and* "the treatment team." The Second Circuit reversed the violation. "We agree with Browder that the treatment agreement would have subjected him to a more punitive condition governing contact with minors than did the condition to which Browder was actually sentenced by the District Court. Browder was therefore well within his rights to object to that term of the agreement." 866 F.3d at 513. Probation approval did not cure the problem, in part because "the treatment agreement actually required the approval not only of the Probation Office, but also of 'the treatment team'—something the District Court did not address and over which it had no direct oversight." *Id.* at 514. The court noted that delay in treatment caused by the objection "did not pose the sort of risk that might warrant arrest and imprisonment." *Id.* at 513. The agreement "even refers to its terms as 'conditions.'" *Id.* at 513 n.38. *Validation:* *Kunz* (2023) treats *Browder* as controlling authority on CIMP issues and distinguishes it on waiver. The later *Browder* summary order (2020) notes that the January 8, 2020 re-imposition of conditions on remand mooted challenges to the earlier conditions. *Browder*'s holdings have not been questioned.
2. **The Browder remand order** (*United States v. Browder*, No. 10-CR-263, Decision and Order (W.D.N.Y. Mar. 14, 2018) (Vilardo, J.), read in full from the Drive Google Scholar copy, which has no reporter pagination; cite by slip opinion and obtain the ECF document number): "Browder does not need permission to have contact with his own children, and he need not sign any treatment agreement requiring him to obtain such permission. Therefore, while the condition that Browder participate in mental health treatment remains, such treatment may not be contingent on his signing any agreement that forces him to obtain permission to have contact with his own children." And: "As the Second Circuit's decision on the treatment condition teaches, Browder cannot be forced to sign or abide by agreements that reach beyond the Court's ordered conditions." The order then re-imposed the condition "The defendant must participate in a sex offense-specific treatment program and follow the rules and regulations of that program …", the same language in Jeff's Judgment. **This cuts both ways.** The same judge who wrote the "rules and regulations" clause held in the same order that agreements reaching beyond the Court's conditions do not bind. That is the best evidence that the clause does not incorporate everything a provider writes.
3. **The later *Browder* summary order (adverse).** *United States v. Browder*, No. 17-1238-cr (2d Cir. Mar. 30, 2020) (summary order) (read in full from ca2.uscourts.gov). The court affirmed a later violation where Browder was denied admission to treatment because he insisted he was innocent of his offense of conviction. Its insistence-on-innocence finding "amounted to a refusal to participate in sex offender treatment as required by the terms of his supervised release" (citing *Baker*, 522 F. App'x 10, and *Iodice*, 525 F.3d 179). The order does **not** say that every provider rule is enforceable. But it does mean that "participate" includes engaging with treatment on the offense of conviction.
4. **Delegation and vagueness** (*Matta*, *Peterson*, *Carlineo*, *Reeves*, above). Under *Carlineo*, an undefined program whose requirements are "left with the Probation Office" to configure, where "one configuration could well be more onerous … than another," leaves "the Probation Office, and not the district court, … deciding the extent of [the] punishment." 998 F.3d at 538.
5. **Adult sexual material.** Jeff's Judgment prohibits only child pornography. *Eaglin*, 913 F.3d at 100: the court "erroneously treated the possession of adult pornography as reprehensible in itself, rather than as an otherwise lawful action." A wholesale adult-material ban needs record support, *id.* at 100–01 (collecting *Voelker*, *Perazza-Mercado*, *Salazar*, and *Taylor*). *Simmons*, 343 F.3d at 82: outside the § 2256 definition, the term "pornography" is "inherently vague." *Farrell v. Burke*, 449 F.3d 470, 498 (2d Cir. 2006) (read in full, CAP): the court did "in no way disagree with prior statements … that conditions … banning the possession of 'pornography,' without further definition, are facially vague," and "we cannot ignore the State's failure to provide meaningful notice of the scope of the Special Condition's prohibition or meaningful limits on an enforcing officer's discretion." **Adverse:** *Simmons*, 343 F.3d at 82, cites with approval *United States v. Phipps*, 319 F.3d 177, 193 (5th Cir. 2003) (upholding a ban on "sexually oriented or sexually stimulating materials" on plain-error review), and *United States v. Bee*, 162 F.3d 1232, 1235 (9th Cir. 1998) (ban on "sexually stimulating" material not an abuse of discretion). Neither was independently read. The distinction: those bans were imposed *by courts*. Jeff's is imposed by a provider and defined "by my treatment staff," with no objective baseline (*Reeves*).
6. **Self-incrimination.** *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 435 (1984) (read in full, Drive): "if the State, either expressly or by implication, asserts that invocation of the privilege would lead to revocation of probation, it would have created the classic penalty situation, the failure to assert the privilege would be excused, and the probationer's answers would be deemed compelled and inadmissible in a criminal prosecution." *Johnson*, 446 F.3d at 279–80, upheld compelled answers only because the condition preserved the "right to challenge in a court of law the use of [incriminating] statements" (following *Asherman v. Meachum*, 957 F.2d 978 (2d Cir. 1992) (en banc), which was not independently read). Jeff's polygraph condition contains that preservation clause. The treatment contract's relapse and "criminal behaviors" reporting provisions, routed to the PO and potentially law enforcement (Consequences ¶ 1), contain none. *Kunz* also recognizes that Probation is not the sentencing judge, and *Matta* says the PO executes the sentence.
7. **Psychotherapy records.** The Judgment's treatment conditions say nothing about records releases. Probation's statutory duty is to "keep informed, to the degree required by the conditions specified by the sentencing court." 18 U.S.C. § 3603(2). HIPAA treats psychotherapy notes as a special category. "Psychotherapy notes" means notes "documenting or analyzing the contents of conversation during a private counseling session or a group … session," and the definition **excludes** "medication prescription and monitoring, counseling session start and stop times, the modalities and frequencies of treatment furnished, results of clinical tests, and any summary of the following items: Diagnosis, functional status, the treatment plan, symptoms, prognosis, and progress to date." 45 C.F.R. § 164.501. A covered entity "must obtain an authorization for any use or disclosure of psychotherapy notes," with narrow exceptions. 45 C.F.R. § 164.508(a)(2). "An individual may revoke an authorization … at any time, provided that the revocation is in writing." *Id.* § 164.508(b)(5). The correctional-institution disclosure exception does not apply after release: "an individual is no longer an inmate when released on parole, probation, supervised release." *Id.* § 164.512(k)(5)(iii). (All read from eCFR, Oct. 12, 2026.) HIPAA does not itself limit what a court may order. But it shows that everything Probation legitimately needs (attendance, progress, compliance, diagnosis, treatment plan) is available *without* psychotherapy notes. *Jaffee v. Redmond*, 518 U.S. 1 (1996) (psychotherapist privilege) was not independently read.
8. **Viagra.** I found **no** federal or New York law prohibiting prescription of erectile-dysfunction drugs to registered sex offenders. Federal law bars Medicaid **payment** for drugs "when used for treatment of sexual or erectile dysfunction," for all recipients. 42 U.S.C. § 1396b(i)(21) (read from LII). The N.Y. State Comptroller reports that "[c]urrent federal and state laws prohibit Medicaid from paying for drugs to treat sexual or erectile dysfunction for Medicaid recipients, including registered sex offenders," and that state law separately bars payment for ED procedures and supplies for registered sex offenders (press release, June 5, 2019, read from osc.ny.gov). These are payment rules, and Jeff has employer insurance. Nothing in the Judgment restricts lawful prescriptions. (This search was not exhaustive of New York statutes; see NOT VERIFIED.)

**Application.**
- **What the clause can and cannot do.** "Follow the rules and regulations of that program" plainly reaches the program's ordinary operating rules: attendance, punctuality, assignments, conduct in group, payment arrangements. Judge Vilardo's remand order, which re-imposed this very clause, says in the same breath that a supervisee "cannot be forced to sign or abide by agreements that reach beyond the Court's ordered conditions." The contract terms that do that:
  - **Partner disclosure and informed consent:** conflicts with the court-controlled risk condition (5.1).
  - **Minor and "vulnerable population" contact, including "accidental" and "incidental" contact:** more onerous than "deliberate" (*Browder*'s exact scenario).
  - **"[S]exually stimulating material(s) of any kind as defined by my treatment staff":** a ban on lawful material the Court never imposed, with the definition delegated to the provider (*Eaglin*, *Farrell*, *Reeves*, *Carlineo*).
  - **Prior approval of "any platforms" including email, gaming, and social media:** a gatekeeping power over internet use the Court never gave. The Judgment's CIMP condition sets the monitoring regime for internet use (*Kunz*, *Eaglin*).
  - **Discharge for "other problem behavior not outlined … as determined in the sole discretion of the staff":** no objective baseline (*Reeves*, *Carlineo*). Because discharge then becomes the predicate for a violation petition, Jeff's liberty is made "contingent on" unreviewable discretion (*Matta*).
  - **Treatment Team including the PO and Probation Supervisor:** the officer who will decide whether to petition for a violation also sits on the body that decides discharge. *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 514, specifically faulted a "treatment team" approval requirement as something the court "did not address and over which it had no direct oversight." Under the Judgment, Probation supervises "the details of the defendant's participation in the program, including the selection of a provider and schedule." That is a *Peterson* "details" delegation, not authority to make clinical discharge decisions.
  - **Relapse and criminal-behavior reporting routed to the PO and law enforcement:** creates the *Murphy* penalty situation without the preservation clause the Court included for polygraph answers.
  - **Release of psychotherapy notes, with revocation "considered a violation":** reaches beyond what the conditions require Probation to know (§ 3603(2)). The Writer should be careful here. Courts commonly expect supervisees to authorize disclosures sufficient to verify participation. Ask to *limit* the release (attendance, participation, progress, diagnosis, treatment plan, test results), not to eliminate it.
  - **Viagra statement:** a provider's statement of supposed law that has no legal basis. Ask the Court to clarify that the conditions do not restrict lawful prescription medication, other than the alcohol clause.
- **"Leave treatment."** The Judgment says "The defendant is not to leave treatment until complete or as ordered by the Court." On its face that addresses Jeff's *leaving*, not a provider's discharge. Under *Browder*, a discharge caused by Jeff's reasonable objection to terms beyond the Judgment is not a violation.
- **Defining "participate."** The client wants "participate" to mean "attend." The later *Browder* summary order makes that risky. A court is likely to hold that refusing to engage with treatment about the offense of conviction is non-participation. Jeff already says he will attend, do assignments, and discuss his conduct of conviction (EXHIBIT-B notes, Requirements ¶ 2). Recommended formulation: "participate" means attending scheduled sessions absent good cause, completing assigned work, and engaging in good faith with treatment regarding the offense of conviction. A provider rule becomes enforceable as a condition of supervised release only insofar as it implements a condition in the Judgment and does not conflict with or materially enlarge one. Discharge from a program is not itself a violation; the question in any violation proceeding is whether Jeff failed to participate as so defined.
- **The provider keeps its own house.** Consistent with the client's theory, the motion should concede that R.K.K.S. may set its own admission and treatment policies as a private business. The point is only that its contract is not the Court's sentence. *Browder* supports exactly that line.

**Counter-arguments.** (a) The text says "follow the rules and regulations," so all rules bind. Answer: the Vilardo remand order construed that very text against agreements reaching beyond the conditions, and *Browder*/*Matta* limit any delegation that touches significant liberty interests. (b) *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 772 n.13, was "reticent to use the fact that Probation has memorialized its policies in a writing as a hook to strip its authority over the kinds of minor details otherwise within its purview." Answer: that footnote concerned Probation's own CIMP agreement and "minor details." The treatment contract is a private provider's document reaching major liberty interests (sex, intimate association, lawful speech and material). (c) Treatment efficacy and deference to clinicians. Answer: the Court can preserve clinical discretion over *treatment content* while denying that clinical rules are sentence terms. (d) Jeff signed the contract. Answer: *Browder* holds that refusing to sign over-broad terms is reasonable. The Writer may wish to argue that signing under threat of a violation is not a waiver (no authority located; see NOT VERIFIED).

**Conclusion.** Moderate-to-high confidence that the Court will hold specific contract terms unenforceable as conditions (partner disclosure, the "accidental/incidental" minor-contact rule, lawful-material and platform bans, sole-discretion discharge). Moderate confidence on limiting the release. Low confidence that the Court will adopt "participate = attend" without a good-faith-engagement component.

### 5.4 Argument 4: Computer and Internet Monitoring Program (Special Condition (a))

**Issue.** Does the CIMP, as administered and as written in the Participant Agreement, comply with the Judgment's limiting clauses? Is the agreement's reach (devices, antivirus, encryption, private browsing, maintenance) authorized?

**Rule.**
1. **The privilege clause binds the monitors.** *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767, construing identical text: "The special condition's text constrains the supervisors in their surveillance of Kunz by requiring them not to abuse their power and access; the CIMP provision instructs Kunz that he has no standalone expectation of privacy beyond that." The Browder remand order added the clause precisely to give "an explicit instruction to those monitoring Browder's computer: they are to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged or innocent material." It also stated: "The Probation Office and Remote.com are authorized to search for contraband, not to willy-nilly read whatever they please," and that nosing into "emails with his attorney … would violate their charge and explicit directive from this Court."
2. **Fourth Amendment narrow tailoring.** *United States v. Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d 173, 190 (2d Cir. 2004) (read in full, CAP): "the monitoring condition must be narrowly tailored, and not sweep so broadly as to draw a wide swath of extraneous material into its net," and "if other information is inadvertently gathered …, those monitoring compliance should remain conscientiously unaware of that data." *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 512: the monitoring was reasonable in part because "Remote.com, notifies the Probation Office only if it detects 'contraband,' and would not convey any information related to Browder's § 2255 motion," which "helps ensure that the monitoring remains … 'narrowly tailored.'" *Id.* at 507 n.10: "It is unclear what constraints, if any at all, are imposed on Remote.com's monitoring of probationers." **Adverse:** *Browder* (2d Cir.) and the remand order both approved capture of "any and all activity … including the capture of keystrokes," with six-month retention, as reasonable. *Kunz* n.1 notes the defendant disavowed any *Lifshitz* challenge.
3. **Agreement terms that exceed the Judgment are unenforceable.** *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 768 (one-device term). Browder remand order: "he must provide information about only those computers he will use and not all those to which he has 'access,' whatever that means." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767, on the "Internet of Things," which "has exploded the category of devices that could qualify as internet-connected." *Love* (2025 summary order) vacated a one-device limit where the government acknowledged that monitoring technology now handles multiple devices.
4. **Approval-type terms are generally "matters of detail," construed narrowly (adverse, but useful).** *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 763: the terms "are obviously and reasonably intended to preclude a defendant's active attempts to avoid monitoring, not to punish a passive defendant for innocuous day-to-day activities." The court construed them to cover only changes the supervisee himself initiates, not passive encryption such as "https," and noted that "nothing in the CIMP terms precludes Kunz from seeking, or the probation officer from granting, blanket permission for Kunz to install standard program updates." *Id.* at 763–64. *Id.* at 766: the approval provisions "are properly understood as matters of detail."
5. **"Impermissible/suspicious activity."** The Judgment triggers notification and examination on "impermissible/suspicious activity or communications." Read with *Matta* and *Browder*, "impermissible" means impermissible under the conditions the Court imposed.

**Application.**
- **Privilege protocol.** The Judgment promises monitoring "designed to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information." If Probation cannot say *how* its system is designed to do that (what is captured, whether attorney or court communications and pro se legal drafts are excluded or segregated, who reviews raw captures, and the retention period), the Court cannot tell whether its own condition is being honored. Under *Kunz* the clause is a real constraint, and under the Rule 32.1 note Jeff may seek clarification of what it requires. Ask for (i) disclosure of the vendor and configuration; (ii) a protocol excluding or quarantining communications with counsel or the Court and drafts of legal filings (for example, by application, folder, or domain, or by having such material reviewed only by a filter reviewer); and (iii) confirmation that captures are reviewed only on an alert for impermissible or suspicious activity, consistent with the model *Browder* approved. **Caution:** Jeff's communications with a non-lawyer helper are not attorney-client privileged. The Writer should frame the protected category as communications with counsel and the Court and Jeff's own pro se litigation materials (work-product-type material). Authority specific to pro se work product on monitored devices was not researched (see NOT VERIFIED).
- **Use of output to enforce non-conditions.** The PO's statement that she "noticed" game chat and told Jeff to stop shows that monitoring output was used to enforce a rule that appears nowhere in the Judgment (5.2). That is the opposite of the contraband-only model that made *Browder*'s monitoring "narrowly tailored." Ask the Court to clarify that "impermissible/suspicious activity" means activity that violates a court-imposed condition or law, and that monitoring may not be used to enforce Probation-created or provider-created rules.
- **Device scope (¶¶ 2–3).** ¶ 2's "own or have access to" exceeds the Judgment's "will be used" (notice) and "owned or operated" (surveillance). The Browder remand order rejected "access" for this reason, and Jeff's CIMP condition is the Browder remand condition. ¶ 3's sweep (blu-ray players, smart TVs, consoles) raises *Kunz*'s Internet-of-Things concern. The fix is to read it as reaching only devices Jeff will use to access the internet.
- **Antivirus and security software (¶ 16).** Under *Kunz* this is likely a "matter of detail," so outright invalidation is unlikely. But a monitored computer barred from running security software is exposed to compromise, which harms the integrity of the monitoring itself. The RemoteCOM handout in the file tells enrollees to install antivirus *before* installation and refers to "[a] list of approved antivirus software." (Whether RemoteCOM is Jeff's vendor is unknown.) Ask for a ruling, or a direction to Probation, granting blanket approval for vendor-compatible antivirus and firewall software, as *Kunz* contemplates.
- **Encryption (¶¶ 9, 17).** Ask the Court to adopt *Kunz*'s construction expressly: passive or provider-side encryption (https, encrypted messaging in transit, device-default encryption) that does not impair monitoring is permitted. Only encryption Jeff initiates to evade monitoring requires approval.
- **Private browsing (¶ 5).** This is weak. In-private browsing plausibly defeats history-based monitoring, so it fits *Kunz*'s evasion rationale. Unless the vendor captures in-private sessions anyway (unknown), consider dropping this point.
- **Routine maintenance (¶¶ 14–15, 20).** Adopt *Kunz*'s construction (only changes Jeff actively initiates) and ask for blanket approval of routine operating-system updates, restore points created automatically, and standard maintenance.
- **Monitoring as a whole.** Jeff's own instinct (9/23 email) matches the case law. A frontal attack on keystroke or full-capture monitoring is foreclosed in practice by *Browder* and the remand order. The targeted requests above are well supported.

**Conclusion.** High confidence on device-scope conformity and on a privilege-protocol clarification. Moderate confidence on the use-limitation clarification. *Kunz*-style constructions and blanket approvals are likely if requested. Outright invalidation of the approval terms is unlikely.

### 5.5 Appointment of counsel (18 U.S.C. § 3006A(a)(1)(E))

**Rule.** Section 3006A(a): "(1) Representation shall be provided for any financially eligible person who— … (E) is charged with a violation of supervised release or faces modification, reduction, or enlargement of a condition, or extension or revocation of a term of supervised release." Rule 32.1(c)(1): at a modification hearing "the person has the right to counsel." Section 3006A(a)(2), the discretionary "interests of justice" provision, covers only Class B/C misdemeanors and §§ 2241, 2254, and 2255 matters. (Text from LII.)

**Application.** If the Court sets a hearing (for example, because the government objects or the Court considers enlarging any condition, such as a risk determination under *Traficante*), Rule 32.1(c)(1) and § 3006A(a)(1)(E) strongly support appointment. Whether a *defendant-initiated* motion by itself means the defendant "faces modification" is not resolved by any authority I read. Argue that the plain text covers any proceeding in which modification of a condition is at issue, and that the government may cross-move to enlarge. Note also that Jeff signed a financial declaration in the related state SORA matter (repo file BERNECKY-FINANCIAL-DECLARATION.md, not reviewed for this memo). A CJA Form 23 will be needed.

**Conclusion.** High confidence for any contested hearing. Uncertain for a motion decided on the papers.
