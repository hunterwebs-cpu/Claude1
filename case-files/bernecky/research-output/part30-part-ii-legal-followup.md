## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1.** Does *United States v. Peterson*, 248 F.3d 79 (2d Cir. 2001), or *United States v. Loy*, 237 F.3d 251 (3d Cir. 2001), supply citable authority for a First Amendment challenge to Treatment Contract SC ¶ 8 ("I will not purchase, possess, view, produce, or use sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind **as defined by my treatment staff**") and SC ¶ 4 (no sending or receiving of "sexually explicit images or narratives without prior approval by treatment staff") as applied to lawful adult material — and if not, does other authority?

> **Short answer: Yes, but not from *Peterson*, and not from *Loy* in the form the client remembers.** *Peterson* decided nothing about pornography conditions; its only usable line on the subject is an overbreadth analogy at 248 F.3d at 83. *Loy* vacated such a condition on both vagueness and First Amendment overbreadth grounds, 237 F.3d at 267, and remains good law — but it expressly preserves a court's power to impose a "more tightly defined restriction on legal, adult pornography." *Id.* The strongest authority is **binding and in-circuit**: *Eaglin*, 913 F.3d at 99-101, struck a ban on legal adult pornography as substantively unreasonable, holding that "a blanket ban on possessing or accessing adult pornography cannot be imposed unless such a ban ... is reasonably related to the sentencing factors and reasonably necessary to accomplish the goals of sentencing," *id.* at 100, and that "[s]uch bans must be supported on the record by detailed factual findings," *id.* at 99. Here there are no findings at all, because no judge has ever seen this term. *Loy* is usable and should be used — as persuasive support on the vagueness/delegation half, where the Second Circuit has repeatedly cited it approvingly.

**Issue 2.** Is there authority that a private instrument — a provider's form contract that is not itself a judicial order — is not entitled to the narrowing, avoid-absurd-results construction that *Kunz* applies to conditions of supervised release?

> **Short answer: No case holds that, and *Kunz* itself cuts the other way** — it construed the Probation Office's CIMP Agreement charitably, and that agreement is not a judicial order either. But the narrower proposition **is** supported: a saving construction is available only where the instrument's own text supplies the narrowing and the narrowing serves the judgment. *Browder* is the best authority: the Second Circuit construed the district court's own computer-monitoring condition charitably, 866 F.3d at 510, and in the same opinion refused to rescue the private treatment agreement, holding instead that the offending term "would contradict, rather than execute," the sentence and that the Probation Office "was plainly without power to impose it," *id.* at 514. *Kunz* supplies the same limit from the other direction: the one CIMP term it could not reconcile with the judgment it did not save — that term "exceed[ed] the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court, and therefore may not be enforced as written." Argue the asymmetry; do not argue a categorical private/public rule.

**Issue 3.** Does *United States v. Von Behren*, 822 F.3d 1139 (10th Cir. 2016), hold that the Fifth Amendment privilege reaches disclosure of uncharged, other-acts conduct — and does its reasoning support treating SC ¶ 9's duty to report contact with a "potential victim" as implicating the privilege?

> **Short answer: Yes on both, with one correction.** *Von Behren* is entirely about "sexual crimes for which he was never charged," 822 F.3d at 1141, and holds that compelled answers about uncharged conduct are privileged both because they "could focus an investigation — otherwise ignorant of his past sex crimes," *id.* at 1147, and because they "could be used against him to show he has a propensity to commit such bad acts" under **Fed. R. Evid. 413 and 414** — not Rule 404(b). *Id.* at 1146. Do not write "404." The reasoning does support the ¶ 9 argument, and the support is stronger than the client realized: *Von Behren*'s own treatment contract contained a police-reporting clause naming "victims or potential victims," *id.* at 1142, and the Tenth Circuit gave that clause independent weight, *id.* at 1147.

## 4. GOVERNING FRAMEWORK

A condition of supervised release must be reasonably related to the § 3553(a) factors and must "involve[] no greater deprivation of liberty than is reasonably necessary" for the purposes of sentencing. 18 U.S.C. § 3583(d)(1)-(2). Where a condition burdens a constitutional right, the Second Circuit applies that statutory language with "heightened constitutional concerns," and "a deprivation of that liberty is 'reasonably necessary' only if the deprivation is narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest." *United States v. Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77, 82-83 (2d Cir. 2010) (quoting *United States v. Myers*, 426 F.3d 117, 126 (2d Cir. 2005)). The court will "carefully scrutinize unusual and severe conditions." *Eaglin*, 913 F.3d at 94-95 (quoting *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 82, quoting *United States v. Doe*, 79 F.3d 1309, 1319-20 (2d Cir. 1996)).

Separately, due process requires that a condition "be sufficiently clear to give the person of ordinary intelligence a reasonable opportunity to know what is prohibited, so that he may act accordingly." *Simmons*, 343 F.3d at 81. A supervisee "has a[] due process right to conditions of supervised release that are sufficiently clear to inform him of what conduct will result in his being returned to prison." *Id.* (quoting *United States v. Guagliardo*, 278 F.3d 868, 872 (9th Cir. 2002)).

## 5. DISCUSSION

### 5.1 Issue 1 — First Amendment and legal adult pornography

#### 5.1.1 *Peterson* — what it actually says

**Read in full from primary text this session** (Google Scholar PDF in the client's Drive, file ID `1QQ3pL8_T8WThlwXUnbq61dLe3qIPgiRd`, cross-checked against the Caselaw Access Project's F.3d text with star pagination).

The client is right that *Peterson* discusses legal adult pornography. It appears three times:

1. **In the facts, at 248 F.3d at 81** — "The conditions of probation were influenced by Peterson's 1996 New York state conviction for incest, Peterson's accessing on his home computers of legal adult pornography web sites both before and after his sentencing, and the probation officer's finding that Peterson 'pose[d] a great risk to the community.'"

2. **In the overbreadth reasoning, at 248 F.3d at 83** — "Although a defendant might use the telephone to commit fraud, this would not justify a condition of probation that includes an absolute bar on the use of telephones. **Nor would defendant's proclivity toward pornography justify a ban on all books, magazines, and newspapers.**"

3. **In footnote 1, at 248 F.3d at 86 n.1** — "We note that another special condition of probation imposed by the District Court in its May 19, 2000 judgment specifically prohibits Peterson from possessing or downloading any pornographic materials. **Peterson has not objected to this condition on appeal.** This condition was not imposed as part of the February 28, 2000 judgment, and therefore Peterson was not violating the terms of his probation when he accessed legal pornography on his computer during the period between February 28 and May 19, 2000."

**Verdict on *Peterson*: correct the client's premise.** Footnote 1 forecloses the argument. *Peterson* had a pornography condition, and the panel expressly recorded that he did not challenge it. There is no *Peterson* holding — none — on the validity of a legal-adult-pornography condition, on First Amendment grounds or any other. Citing *Peterson* for that proposition would be an inflated holding of exactly the kind that gets caught.

**What *is* usable.** The sentence at 248 F.3d at 83 — "Nor would defendant's proclivity toward pornography justify a ban on all books, magazines, and newspapers" — is a real, quotable overbreadth analogy from a binding opinion, and it is unusually well aimed at **SC ¶ 4**, which reaches "narratives." It is dicta, and the Writer should present it as reasoning rather than holding, but it is the Second Circuit's own sentence and it says that a documented interest in pornography does not license a categorical ban on the printed word.

**One caution.** *Eaglin* characterizes *Peterson* as a case in which the defendant "had been charged with violating conditions of supervised release by viewing adult pornography," 913 F.3d at 96 (citing 248 F.3d at 82). That gloss sits awkwardly with *Peterson*'s own footnote 1, which says he was **not** in violation when he accessed legal pornography. Quote *Peterson* from *Peterson*, never from *Eaglin*'s summary of it.

#### 5.1.2 *Loy* — there are two opinions, and only the second decides anything

**Read in full from primary text this session** (law.resource.org full text, cross-checked against the Caselaw Access Project's F.3d text for star pagination).

- ***United States v. Loy*, 191 F.3d 360 (3d Cir. 1999) ("*Loy I*").** The district court had imposed conditions forbidding Loy to possess "pornography of any type," barring unsupervised contact with minors, and requiring alcohol treatment. The Third Circuit affirmed the suppression ruling and the drug-testing condition but **remanded without reaching the merits**, because the court "imposed these special conditions without making any factual findings relating to them or providing any reasons in support of them." 191 F.3d at 369-71. *Loy I* holds nothing about pornography. Do not cite it for anything on this subject.

- ***United States v. Loy*, 237 F.3d 251 (3d Cir. 2001) ("*Loy II*") (Becker, C.J.).** This is the merits opinion. On remand the district court had reimposed the condition, now phrased as a prohibition on possessing "all forms of pornography, **including legal adult pornography**," justified as necessary "to protect the children that are victimized in the production of child pornography" and to "deter defendant from engaging in additional criminal conduct."

**The holding, verbatim, at 237 F.3d at 267:**

> "Thus, in Loy's case, to the extent that the condition might apply to a wide swath of work ranging from serious art to ubiquitous advertising, the condition is overly broad and violates the First Amendment. To the extent that its breadth is unclear, it is unconstitutionally vague."

**Disposition, at 237 F.3d at 270:** "[W]e will affirm the condition restricting Loy's contact with minors, but will vacate the condition prohibiting Loy from possessing pornography, and remand to the District Court for further proceedings consistent with this opinion."

**The limit, in the very next sentences, same page:**

> "That said, there is no question that the District Court could, perfectly consonant with the Constitution, restrict Loy's access to sexually oriented materials, so long as that restriction was set forth with sufficient clarity and with a nexus to the goals of supervised release. **Further, the Constitution would not forbid a more tightly defined restriction on legal, adult pornography**, perhaps one that clarified whether it extended non-visual materials, or that borrowed applicable language from the federal statutory definition of child pornography located at 18 U.S.C. § 2256(8)."

And at 237 F.3d at 266: "We in no way mean to imply that courts may not impose restrictions on the consumption of sexually explicit materials by persons convicted of sex crimes."

**Verdict on *Loy*: the client's premise needs one word changed.** *Loy* did vacate a condition that reached legal adult pornography, and did so partly on First Amendment grounds. But it did **not** hold that legal adult material is beyond a sentencing court's reach. The constitutional defect was **breadth and imprecision**, not subject matter. A motion that says "the Third Circuit held that a condition restricting legal adult pornography violates the First Amendment" will be met with the sentence quoted above, from the same paragraph. Frame it as *Loy* framed it.

#### 5.1.3 The three parts of *Loy II* that are worth more to this motion than its headline

*Loy II* contains three passages that fit SC ¶ 8 better than its First Amendment holding does.

**(a) An enforcer's discretion cannot cure a vague term — 237 F.3d at 266.** The government argued that "pornography" was cabined because Loy could ask his probation officer. The Third Circuit rejected it:

> "A condition with no core meaning beyond 'whatever is necessary for Loy's rehabilitation' cannot be cured by allowing the probation officer an unfettered power of interpretation, as this would create one of the very problems against which the vagueness doctrine is meant to protect, i.e., the delegation of 'basic policy matters to policemen ... for resolution on an ad hoc and subjective basis.'"

and

> "[W]ithout a more definitive standard to guide the probation officer's discretion, there is a real danger that the prohibition on pornography may ultimately translate to a prohibition on whatever the officer personally finds titillating."

SC ¶ 8 does not merely leave the definition to the enforcer by implication. It **says so in terms**: "as defined by my treatment staff." *Loy* rejected as a cure the very thing this paragraph adopts as its operative mechanism. That is the single best use of *Loy* in this motion, and it is a vagueness/delegation use, not a First Amendment use.

**(b) A scienter gloss will not save it — 237 F.3d at 265.** "Even if a scienter requirement were to be read into the condition, however, this construction would not save it. ... Indeed, a contrary rule would rob the vagueness doctrine of all of its meaning, for legislatures would simply repair otherwise vague statutes by inserting the word 'knowingly.'" Relevant to Issue 2 as well — see § 5.2.4.

**(c) § 2256 cannot supply the definition where the term reaches past child pornography — 237 F.3d at 270 n.4.** "Although federal law contains a definition of child pornography, see 18 U.S.C. § 2256, the release condition imposed here cannot be presumed to track that statute, as the condition explicitly prohibits 'legal adult pornography.' Further, the condition does not limit its reach to visual works, as does federal law."

**This footnote is the answer to the government's best argument**, and it is not just persuasive — the Second Circuit has adopted its reasoning. See § 5.1.7.

**(d) The pre-enforcement posture — 237 F.3d at 258.** *Loy II* devotes its first section to holding the challenge justiciable before enforcement, reasoning that the government's contrary position "would have Loy discover the meaning of his supervised release condition only under continual threat of reimprisonment, in sequential hearings before the court," and that "[s]uch an exercise is not necessary, nor will it clarify the issues." This is a direct analogue to the Rule 32.1 advisory-committee point Part IV already makes, from a court applying it to a pornography condition specifically.

#### 5.1.6 The real answer: *Eaglin* is binding, in-circuit, and directly on point

**Read in full from primary text this session** — first from the court's slip opinion PDF, then re-read against the Caselaw Access Project's F.3d text to confirm every pincite below.

*United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d 88 (2d Cir. 2019), decided a challenge to two conditions: a near-total internet ban and "a total ban on viewing or possessing adult pornography." The Second Circuit vacated **both**.

**The condition, at 913 F.3d at 101 n.6:** Eaglin "must not view, possess, own, subscribe to or purchase any material, including pictures, videotapes, films, magazines, books, telephone services, electronic media, computer programs, or computer services that depict sexually explicit conduct, **as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 2256(2)**." "It identifies no possible exceptions."

**This matters enormously for our purposes.** Eaglin's condition was **tethered to the federal statutory definition** — precisely the cure *Loy* recommended and *Simmons* urged. It was therefore **not vague**. And the Second Circuit struck it anyway. That means the *Eaglin* theory is **independent of vagueness**, and survives even if the government persuades the Court that "sexually stimulating material(s)" is clear enough. SC ¶ 8 is both vaguer than Eaglin's condition and less supported. Bernecky's case is *a fortiori*.

**The framework, at 913 F.3d at 94-95:** the Court "will 'carefully scrutinize unusual and severe conditions,'" and "[t]he conditions imposed here — prohibiting access to the Internet and to legal adult pornography — **are** such unusual and severe conditions; they merit our close examination."

**The First Amendment premise, at 913 F.3d at 99** (quoting *Farrell*, 449 F.3d at 497):

> "We tolerate such conditions only in limited circumstances: 'Pornographic materials ... receive full First Amendment protection when in the possession of ordinary adults, but may be regulated in the hands of parolees to a much greater extent.'"

**The rule, at 913 F.3d at 99:**

> "Such bans must be supported on the record by detailed factual findings establishing that the proposed ban is reasonably related to the sentencing factors set forth in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) and that it is reasonably necessary to accomplish their objectives. **A general reference to 'the public interest' ordinarily will not suffice.**"

**The error identified, at 913 F.3d at 100:**

> "Here, the District Court erroneously treated the possession of adult pornography as reprehensible in itself, rather than as an otherwise lawful action that violated Eaglin's conditions of supervised release."

**The holding, at 913 F.3d at 100:**

> "In line with the decisions of many other courts, however, we conclude that a blanket ban on possessing or accessing adult pornography cannot be imposed unless such a ban on adult pornography is reasonably related to the sentencing factors and reasonably necessary to accomplish the goals of sentencing."

**The disposition, at 913 F.3d at 101:** "Accordingly, the condition must be struck."

**The "gateway drug" answer the client asked for — 913 F.3d at 100.** *Eaglin* names the two circumstances in which such a ban might be justified:

> "Imposing a wholesale ban on accessing adult pornography might be justified where the offense of conviction involved the creation of adult pornography with unconsenting victims, see, e.g., *Simmons*, 343 F.3d at 82, **or where a mental health professional testified that viewing pornography would be detrimental to the defendant's rehabilitation**, see, e.g., *Springer*, 684 F. App'x at 40."

This is the client's own point in the Second Circuit's mouth, and it is worth stating exactly as *Eaglin* states it. The clinical theory that adult pornography is a gateway is not categorically irrelevant — but the way it enters a supervised-release condition is **through a mental health professional's testimony, considered by a court, which then makes findings and imposes a condition**. It does not enter through a provider's form contract, unreviewed, enforced by threat of discharge and violation. R.K.K.S. may hold whatever clinical view it likes. What it may not do is convert that view into a term of Mr. Bernecky's supervision without a judge ever passing on it. The client's framing in item 14 — that "the provider cannot rely on the Court to enforce that clinical theory on the patient through the threat of revocation, absent a particularized condition actually authorizing it" — is exactly right, and *Eaglin* is the citation for it.

**The out-of-circuit chorus *Eaglin* assembles, at 913 F.3d at 100-01**, is quotable and includes one sentence that could have been written about SC ¶ 8:

> *United States v. Salazar*, 743 F.3d 445, 452 (5th Cir. 2014) — "It is hard to imagine how preventing Salazar from accessing **sexually stimulating materials** would prevent future criminal conduct when there is no indication in the record that Salazar has an unhealthy relationship with such materials or that such materials contributed to his underlying crimes or other violations."

Also collected: *United States v. Taylor*, 796 F.3d 788, 793 (7th Cir. 2015); *United States v. Perazza-Mercado*, 553 F.3d 65, 76 (1st Cir. 2009); and *United States v. Voelker*, 489 F.3d 139, 152-53 (3d Cir. 2007).

#### 5.1.7 The rest of the Second Circuit line — and the adverse authority in it

**Read in full from primary text this session:** *Reeves*, *Farrell*, and the relevant portions of *Simmons* (as quoted within *Farrell* and *Reeves*, and confirmed against *Simmons*'s own F.3d text).

***Simmons*, 343 F.3d 72 (2d Cir. 2003).** At 81:

> "For purposes of evaluating artistic or cultural merit, the term 'pornography' is **notoriously elusive**. In that context, determining whether material deserves the label of pornography is a subjective, standardless process, heavily influenced by the individual, social and cultural experience of the person making the determination."

At 82, after upholding the condition because the defendant's statute of conviction supplied § 2256's definition:

> "While it is unnecessary for district courts imposing such a prohibition to explicitly reference this statutory definition of pornography, **we urge them to do so in future cases, particularly since in other contexts the term is inherently vague**."

***Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77 (2d Cir. 2010).** *Reeves* supplies the **test** that decides SC ¶ 8, at 81:

> "This does not end our inquiry, however, since **a subjective element in a release condition may be sufficiently specific if tethered to objective criteria**."

Loy*, 237 F.3d 251, 263 n.4 (3d Cir. 2001) ...."
- At 489, the Second Circuit's own parade of horribles, which is better than any list we could invent and includes two items already in the draft: the State's proposed definition "would apparently also include virtually all television programs in which attractive characters are shown romantically entangled, **many fashion magazines and lingerie catalogs**, and a high percentage of the music videos on MTV."

That is not a technicality; it is the axis on which *Farrell* turns.

**The three pieces of adverse authority the Writer must handle:**

SC ¶ 8 bans "sexually stimulating material(s) of **any kind**"; SC ¶ 4 reaches "**narratives**"; § 2256 is confined to visual depictions of minors. Nothing about § 2256 tells Mr. Bernecky whether a lingerie catalogue is contraband. 2. ***Simmons*, 343 F.3d at 82, cites approvingly two cases upholding the exact phrase in SC ¶ 8.** This is the sharpest adverse point in the file and I want it stated plainly: *Simmons* cites *United States v. Phipps*, 319 F.3d 177, 193 (5th Cir. 2003) ("upholding prohibition on 'sexually oriented or **sexually stimulating** materials' on review for plain error"), and *United States v. Bee*, 162 F.3d 1232, 1235 (9th Cir. 1998) ("prohibition on possessing '**sexually stimulating**' material was not an abuse of discretion"). **Answers:** both were court-imposed conditions reviewed under deferential standards (plain error; abuse of discretion), not pre-enforcement challenges; neither delegated the definition of the term to a private party; both predate *Eaglin*, which tightened the standard in 2019 and requires "detailed factual findings"; and *Phipps* is described by *Farrell*, 449 F.3d at 488 & n.4, as turning on a companion condition that **narrowed** the first — a narrowing SC ¶ 8 does not have. **[VERIFY — I have not read *Phipps* or *Bee*. Do not write Part II as though Mr. Bernecky holds an unqualified First Amendment right here. He does not. The argument is that a *diminished* right still requires the findings *Eaglin* demands, and that **no findings exist at all** because no judge has seen this term.

#### 5.1.8 Verdict on Issue 1

**Yes — there is real, citable authority, and it is binding.** Build the argument on ***Eaglin***, not on *Peterson* and not on *Loy*'s First Amendment holding.

The sequence that works:

1. **§ 3583(d)(2) / *Eaglin*** — a ban on lawful adult material is an "unusual and severe" condition that requires "detailed factual findings," 913 F.3d at 94-95, 99. Here there are none, and there could be none, because no judge has ever reviewed SC ¶ 8. *Eaglin* struck a **statutorily-tethered** ban entered by an Article III judge who at least gave a reason. SC ¶ 8 is an untethered ban entered by a private practice that gave none.
2. **Vagueness / *Reeves* + *Simmons* + *Loy*** — a subjective term survives only if "tethered to objective criteria," *Reeves*, 591 F.3d at 81; "sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind as defined by my treatment staff" is tethered to the enforcer's own judgment, which is the cure *Loy* rejected at 237 F.3d at 266.
3. **Delegation / *Matta*, *Carlineo*, *Jimenez*, *Loy*** — already in the draft; *Loy* at 266 adds a circuit court saying that this specific cure fails for this specific category of condition.
4. **The clinical-theory answer / *Eaglin* at 100** — a provider's belief about gateway effects enters a sentence through testimony and judicial findings, not through a form.

*Loy* is usable and should be used, in positions 2 and 3. *Peterson* should be used only for the single overbreadth sentence at 248 F.3d at 83, presented as reasoning.

### 5.2 Issue 2 — the saving-construction distinction

#### 5.2.1 The client's proposition, and the problem with it as stated

Item 19's first bullet asks the Court to withhold from a provider's form the narrowing construction that *Kunz* applies to conditions — on the theory that the doctrine "exists to save a court's own order from an absurd literal reading, not to rescue a private provider's overbroad form."

**The premise is factually wrong about *Kunz*, and the error is one the government will find immediately.** I re-read the *Kunz* opinion in full from `KUNZ-OPINION-FULLTEXT.md`. *Kunz* did **not** apply its narrowing construction to the district court's judgment. It applied it **to the CIMP Agreement** — a Probation Office form that is not a judicial order:

> "**Importantly, the CIMP terms themselves suggest that they should be read that way.** All of the challenged provisions are phrased in terms of what 'I' (Kunz, the supervisee) will or will not actively do."

> "In sum, even a stubbornly literal reading of the challenged CIMP terms does not require the interpretation offered by Kunz's expert. **We thus construe the requirements** to prohibit Kunz from initiating without prior permission any of the above processes or changes ...."

> "**We think it would be absurd** to interpret Probation as purporting to require Kunz to ask permission every time he attempts to access a website whose URL happens to begin with the characters 'https' ...."

A motion that argues "the *Kunz* saving construction applies only to judicial orders" is arguing against the case it cites throughout Parts I and II. Do not make that argument in that form.

#### 5.2.2 The version of the point that is supported — *Browder*'s asymmetry

**Read in full from primary text this session** (Caselaw Access Project F.3d text with star pagination).

*United States v. Browder*, 866 F.3d 504 (2d Cir. 2017), does the exact thing the client wants, **within one opinion**, and the motion already relies on it heavily.

**The court's own condition — construed charitably.** At 866 F.3d at 510:

> "In light of the foregoing, **we construe the computer monitoring condition as Browder himself does** — that is, as having sentenced him to whatever computer monitoring policy was used by the Western District's Probation Office at his release — and consider whether that sentence, as effectuated in the Probation Office policy, was unreasonable or an 'abuse of discretion.' We conclude it was not."

**The private treatment agreement — not construed, but held unenforceable.** At 866 F.3d at 513-14, the Second Circuit did not ask whether the agreement's minor-contact term could be read narrowly enough to coexist with Browder's sentence. It asked whether it conflicted, found that it did, and stopped:

> "The gravamen of Browder's complaint is simply that the proposed treatment agreement conflicted with his actual sentence."

> "Because such a requirement as to Browder's children **would contradict, rather than execute, Browder's sentence, the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose it**, as Browder correctly argued."

**And the functional equivalent of a saving construction was expressly rejected.** The district court had reasoned that Browder suffered no harm because permission to see his daughters would certainly have been granted — the practical analogue of "read the term charitably and no problem arises." At 866 F.3d at 514:

> "Even if that were the case, however, **approvals from the Probation Office are insufficient to remedy the issue raised by Browder**. First, the treatment agreement actually required the approval not only of the Probation Office, but also of 'the treatment team' — something the District Court did not address and over which it had no direct oversight. Second, and more fundamentally, the District Court misapprehended the relationship between a defendant's sentence (by a judge) and its execution (by a probation officer). **The key question is not whether permission, if sought, would be granted; it is whether Browder can be required, by the Probation Office or its designee, to ask in the first place.**"

**This is the argument.** Two instruments, one opinion, two treatments. The judicial order got a construction. The provider's agreement got a holding that the offending term could not be imposed. The Writer does not need a categorical rule; the Writer needs this contrast, stated in one paragraph.

#### 5.2.3 *Kunz*'s own limiting principle points the same way

*Kunz* did not construe the CIMP terms charitably across the board. It did so **where the terms' own text supplied the narrowing** — "the CIMP terms themselves suggest that they should be read that way" — and **where Probation's evident purpose supplied a standard** — "[t]he clear intention of the prohibition is to prevent Kunz from hiding behind the veil of encryption ...."

Where a CIMP term could **not** be reconciled with the judgment, *Kunz* did not save it:

> "But that still leaves the language in the CIMP terms that purports to reserve for Probation the right to restrict Kunz to a single device. **We conclude that this term exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court, and therefore may not be enforced as written.**"

So *Kunz* supplies the rule in both directions, and the rule is not about who wrote the paper. It is about **two conditions**: (i) does the instrument's own text admit the narrower reading, and (ii) does the narrower reading execute the judgment rather than contradict it?

**Apply that to SC ¶¶ 8, 9, and 2.** Each fails both prongs.

- **SC ¶ 8** cannot be narrowed by its own text, because its text affirmatively **assigns the definition elsewhere** — "as defined by my treatment staff." There is no narrower reading to adopt; the paragraph's whole content is a delegation. And a narrowed version would still add a prohibition the Judgment does not contain.
- **SC ¶ 9** cannot be narrowed to match Special Condition (c), because the two are **textual opposites**: (c) reaches "deliberate contact," ¶ 9 reaches "any accidental, incidental, or intentional contact." No construction reconciles "accidental" with "deliberate"; one has to give way. And ¶ 9's other three categories — "potential or former victims, codefendants, vulnerable populations (as defined by treatment staff)" — have no counterpart in the Judgment at all, so there is nothing to narrow them toward.
- **SC ¶ 2** requires no risk determination by anyone, where Standard Condition 12 requires one; and it demands "disclosure of my offense history in full" plus documentary proof, where the Judgment demands notice of a risk. Again, contradiction rather than execution.

**That is the client's point, made in *Kunz*'s own vocabulary rather than against it** — and it is stronger, because it does not require the Court to adopt a novel rule about private instruments.

#### 5.2.4 Two further supports, and one honest limit

**(a) The narrowing construction's premise is a judicial author's intent.** *Loy II* is explicit about this. When the Third Circuit narrowed the minor-contact condition to exclude Loy's own future children, it grounded the move entirely in what the sentencing judge meant, at 237 F.3d at 270:

> "But it is unnecessary to decide this question, because **we believe it unlikely that the District Court intended its condition to extend so far**. Given the severe intrusion on Loy's family life that would otherwise result, we believe that, **absent a clearer sign from the District Court**, the condition should be construed to apply only to other people's children, and not to Loy's own."

A saving construction infers a narrower intent from an author whose purposes the court can reconstruct. **There is nothing to reconstruct here.** The Practice was given a blank for terms particular to this patient and wrote "No special conditions designated." SC ¶ 1 addresses a defendant on pretrial diversion, which Mr. Bernecky has not been for eight years. A document that does not know who its reader is has no particularized intent for a court to charitably infer. That is an argument from *Loy*'s reasoning, and it is available without asserting a categorical rule.

**(b) Refusals to save a vague term.** *Loy II* twice declined to rescue a condition by construction: the scienter gloss "would not save it," 237 F.3d at 265, and the probation officer's interpretive power "cannot cure" it, *id.* at 266. And *Farrell*, 449 F.3d at 493, describes a condition that "provides no standards whatsoever" — an instrument with no standards offers a court nothing to narrow.

**(c) The honest limit — and one argument *not* to make.** Ordinary contract doctrine runs the other way from a saving construction: an ambiguity in an adhesion instrument is construed **against** its drafter, and *Von Behren* describes a materially identical document as "a non-negotiable treatment agreement," 822 F.3d at 1142. That asymmetry is intuitively appealing and it is worth at most **one sentence** of framing. **I found no case applying contra proferentem to a sex-offense treatment contract in the supervised-release context, and no case holding that a private instrument is categorically ineligible for a saving construction.** The only sources I located for the contract principle were law-firm marketing pages and general reference material, which under this file's standing practice are not sources at all. **Do not cite anything for this proposition.** State it as reasoning or leave it out.

**(d) Adverse fact the Writer should know.** In *Von Behren*, the district court held "that because compliance with the terms of the RSA agreement was required for participation in and successful completion of the RSA program, **the requirements of the RSA agreement were, in effect, conditions of Mr. Von Behren's supervised release**." 822 F.3d at 1142-43. The Tenth Circuit recited this without disapproval and proceeded on that footing. This is **excellent** for Part II's opening premise — it is a court holding that a provider's contract terms operate as conditions of supervision. But it cuts against Issue 2's framing: if the contract's terms *are* conditions, the government will say they get whatever construction rules conditions get. The *Browder*/*Kunz* answer above survives that objection; a categorical private/public rule does not.

#### 5.2.5 Verdict on Issue 2

**The client's instinct is right; his formulation is not, and using his formulation would hand the government *Kunz*.**

- **No** authority holds that a private instrument is categorically ineligible for a saving construction.
- ***Kunz* itself gave one to a non-judicial instrument** (the Probation Office's CIMP form).
- **But *Browder* declined to give one to a private treatment agreement in the same opinion in which it gave one to the court's own condition** — and expressly rejected the practical equivalent ("approvals ... are insufficient to remedy the issue"), 866 F.3d at 510, 513-14.
- **And *Kunz* supplies the limiting principle**: a term that "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court ... may not be enforced as written."
- **Recommended framing:** a saving construction is available where the instrument's own text admits the narrower reading and the narrower reading *executes* the judgment. Where the term *contradicts* the judgment, no construction saves it — *Browder* — and the remedy is that it may not be enforced — *Kunz*. Then show that ¶¶ 8, 9, and 2 each contradict rather than execute.

### 5.3 Issue 3 — *Von Behren*, uncharged conduct, and SC ¶ 9

**Read in full from primary text this session** (Google Scholar PDF in the client's Drive, file ID `1OoaNhk6T5S1MuaZj2DiNfcq5tOUZPi3F`).

#### 5.3.1 The correction: Rules 413 and 414, not Rule 404(b)

The client's recollection is "*Von Behren* discussed [that] even [Rule] 404 information is subject to Fifth Amendment scrutiny."

**The substance is confirmed. The rule number is wrong, and the correct number is better for us.** *Von Behren* does not cite Rule 404(b) anywhere. The operative passage is at **822 F.3d at 1146**:

> "Furthermore, an affirmative answer could potentially be used against Mr. Von Behren if he were ever charged with a sex crime. For instance, if Mr. Von Behren were to answer yes to the underage sex question or the physical force question, **those answers could be used against him to show he has a propensity to commit such bad acts**. *See* Fed. R. Evid. 413, 414 (Rule 413(a) allows the introduction of character evidence in sexual assault cases to show a propensity toward committing such crimes, and Rule 414(a) allows the same thing in child molestation cases.)."

The Rule 404 principle **is** in the opinion, but as the background premise that Rules 413/414 displace, quoted from *United States v. Nance*, 767 F.3d 1037, 1041 (10th Cir. 2014), at 822 F.3d at 1146-47:

> "[a]lthough '[t]he rules of evidence generally prohibit the admission of evidence for the purpose of showing a defendant's propensity to commit bad acts,' Rule 414(a) 'provides an exception to this general rule' by allowing the jury in a prosecution for child molestation to consider the fact that the defendant has committed other acts of child molestation as evidence that the defendant committed the charged offense."

**Why the correction helps.** Rule 404(b) is a rule of **exclusion** with enumerated exceptions; an argument built on it would have to explain why a disclosure would come in at all. Rules 413 and 414 are rules of **admission** — in prosecutions for sexual assault and child molestation, prior acts come in *for propensity*, the very use 404(b) forbids elsewhere. For a defendant with Mr. Bernecky's conviction, that is the harsher regime and the stronger argument. Write 413/414. Do not write 404.

#### 5.3.2 Uncharged conduct is the whole case

There is no ambiguity about whether the privilege reaches conduct outside the offense of conviction. That is the only thing *Von Behren* is about:

- At 822 F.3d at 1141: the polygraph required him "to answer four questions regarding **whether he had committed sexual crimes for which he was never charged**."
- At 1146: "Here, Mr. Von Behren is being asked to admit that **he committed uncharged sexual crimes**."
- At 1147: the answers "**could focus an investigation — otherwise ignorant of his past sex crimes — on Mr. Von Behren**."

And the threshold is low, at 822 F.3d at 1144 (quoting *Maness v. Meyers*, 419 U.S. 449, 461 (1975)): "[t]he protection does not merely encompass evidence which may lead to criminal conviction, but includes information which would furnish a **link in the chain of evidence** that could lead to prosecution, as well as evidence which an individual reasonably believes could be used against him in a criminal prosecution." Also at 1144, quoting *Rivas-Macias*, 537 F.3d at 1278: "'[n]ot much is required ... to show an individual faces some authentic danger of self-incrimination, [ ] as the privilege "extends to admissions that may only tend to incriminate."'"

#### 5.3.3 Two government answers *Von Behren* forecloses in advance

**(a) "Rule 403 would keep it out anyway."** At 822 F.3d at 1146: "[W]hile the government argues that a trial court could exclude such evidence under Fed. R. Evid. 403 ..., **we do not think the Fifth Amendment privilege should be submitted to an evidentiary balancing test**."

**(b) "This clinician has never called the police."** The district court had relied on the fact that Von Behren's polygrapher had conducted 47,000 examinations without ever being contacted by law enforcement. At 822 F.3d at 1146:

> "This may be true, but '[o]nce the court determines that the answers requested would tend to incriminate the witness, it should not attempt to speculate whether the witness will in fact be prosecuted.' *United States v. Jones*, 703 F.2d 473, 478 (10th Cir. 1983). **The district court mistakenly assumed that an assurance from the government was a substitute for immunity.**"

Both answers matter here, because the government's response to Part II.C and to any ¶ 9 argument will be some version of one or the other.

#### 5.3.4 The reporting clause — the closest factual parallel in the file

This is the most useful thing I found for the ¶ 9 argument, and it was hiding in a case the motion already cites.

***Von Behren*'s own treatment contract contained a police-reporting clause using the phrase "potential victims."** At 822 F.3d at 1142, quoted in full by the Tenth Circuit:

> "I hereby instruct RSA, Inc. to report to any appropriate authority or authorities any occurrence **or potential occurrence** of any sexual offense on my part regardless of how RSA, Inc. gains knowledge of such occurrence or potential occurrence. 'Appropriate authority or authorities' as used in this and subsequent revisions may include, but is not limited to, County Human Services Departments, law enforcement agencies, probation or parole personnel, **victims or potential victims**, parents, spouses, school personnel, and employers."

**And the Tenth Circuit gave that clause independent constitutional weight**, at 822 F.3d at 1147:

> "**Notably**, there is a provision in Mr. Von Behren's contract with RSA that instructs RSA 'to report to any appropriate authority or authorities any occurrence or potential occurrence of any sexual offense.' ... **This provision, which specifically authorizes his examiner to report his admissions to the police, undoubtedly adds to Mr. Von Behren's apprehension in regard to answering** the four questions."

**The R.K.K.S. contract has the same architecture, in three paragraphs instead of one:**

| *Von Behren* / RSA | R.K.K.S. |
|---|---|
| Provider instructed to report "any occurrence or potential occurrence of any sexual offense" | Code of Conduct ¶ 6 (report "criminal behaviors [outside of or in addition to Instant Offense or pending charges]"); Code of Conduct ¶ 5 (24-hour notice of "any relapse in problematic behavior") |
| Recipients include "law enforcement agencies" | Consequences ¶ 1 — "law enforcement may be contacted if it becomes necessary to maintain the safety of **potential victims** and/or the community" |
| Recipients include "probation or parole personnel" | Confidentiality ¶ 2 — reports to "United States Probation and other individuals and agencies involved in my treatment" |
| Recipients include "victims or **potential victims**" | SC ¶ 9 — 24-hour reporting of contact with "**potential** or former victims ... vulnerable populations (as defined by treatment staff)" |

**So the client's theory is confirmed, and the chain can be stated concretely.** SC ¶ 9 requires Mr. Bernecky to report, within 24 hours, contact with a person the Practice would classify as a "potential victim." That report goes to a Treatment Team on which his probation officer and her supervisor sit (Consequences ¶ 3(a)), and Consequences ¶ 1 permits routing it to the police. There is no immunity anywhere in the document, and no privilege carve-out. Under *Von Behren*, that combination is not a remote hazard — it is what "undoubtedly adds to" the apprehension the privilege exists to protect, and the disclosure itself is a "link in the chain of evidence."

**A further point worth making.** The very act of characterizing another person as a "potential victim" is an admission about the reporter — it asserts that his contact with that person carries a risk of a sexual offense. There is no innocent version of that report. **[This is my own reasoning, not a holding from any case. Argue it; do not cite it.]**

#### 5.3.5 The relief precedent hiding in *Von Behren*'s background section

At 822 F.3d at 1143, describing the district court's first order:

> "Without knowing the exact questions Mr. Von Behren would be asked, the court **modified Mr. Von Behren's release conditions to exclude any requirement that he admit to a criminal offense other than his offense of conviction**."

That is, almost word for word, the relief Part II currently requests in item (3) — confirmation that neither Special Condition (b) nor (h) requires Mr. Bernecky to disclose uncharged criminal conduct. **A district court has actually entered this order**, in a case the motion already cites, and the Tenth Circuit recited it without a hint of disapproval before going on to hold that the compulsion was unconstitutional.

**Two caveats, both of which are themselves useful.** First, this was the **district court's order**, recited in the appellate court's background section — it is not a Tenth Circuit holding, and should be described as what it is: a modification a district court entered on materially identical facts. Second, and more instructive: **the provider ignored it.** At 822 F.3d at 1143: "A few months later, **despite the district court's pronouncement**, RSA informed Mr. Von Behren that he would need to submit to a sexual history polygraph or leave the program."

That is a direct argument for the shape of Part II's relief. An order addressed to the provider is worth nothing, because this Court has no power over R.K.K.S. — *McDonald*, slip op. at 12 n.2 — and because *Von Behren* shows what happens when a provider decides a court order does not apply to it. What has to happen instead is what Part II already asks for: relief running against **Probation** and against **this Court's own order**, plus the backstop in relief item (7) that no violation report may be filed and no violation found if the Practice terminates him after a timely written objection.

#### 5.3.6 The limit — *Von Behren* footnote 9, and where the privilege argument must **not** go

At 822 F.3d at 1150 n.9:

> "The outcome would be different, and thus the government's argument would be valid, **if Mr. Von Behren had refused to answer a question relevant only to his supervised release status (such as whether he had looked at pornographic material)**, or if he had refused to answer a question that did not involve the risk of self-incrimination, or if he were given immunity and still refused to answer any questions."

Two consequences for Part II, and the second one is an instruction.

**(a) Pitch the ¶ 9 argument at the disclosures carrying criminal exposure.** Not every reportable event under ¶ 9 is privileged. Incidental contact with a minor at a grocery store is a **supervision-status** fact, not a confession. The privilege argument belongs to the categories that carry criminal exposure — contact with a "potential victim," "criminal behaviors" under Code of Conduct ¶ 6, "relapse in problematic behavior" under Code of Conduct ¶ 5. Framing the whole of ¶ 9 as privileged invites footnote 9 as the answer.

**(b) Do not run the Fifth Amendment argument on SC ¶ 8.** Footnote 9's own illustration of a **non-privileged** question is "whether he had looked at pornographic material." Pending-edits item 20 asks for a Fifth Amendment treatment of the Requirements/Code of Conduct "high-risk situations" and "relapse" language, which is correct. But if that argument bleeds onto ¶ 8's viewing prohibition, *Von Behren* supplies the government's rebuttal in a parenthetical. **Allocate the arguments:** ¶ 8 and ¶ 4 get First Amendment, vagueness, and delegation (§ 5.1); ¶ 9, Code of Conduct ¶¶ 5-6, and Requirements ¶ 1 get the Fifth Amendment (§ 5.3).

#### 5.3.7 Verdict on Issue 3

**Confirmed with one correction.** The privilege covers uncharged, other-acts conduct — that is *Von Behren*'s entire subject — and the mechanism is **Fed. R. Evid. 413 and 414**, 822 F.3d at 1146, not Rule 404(b). The reasoning supports treating SC ¶ 9's "potential victim" disclosure as implicating the privilege, and the support is unusually direct because *Von Behren*'s own contract contained a police-reporting clause naming "potential victims" that the Tenth Circuit treated as aggravating the constitutional problem, *id.* at 1142, 1147. Two government answers are foreclosed in advance (*id.* at 1146). One limit must be respected: footnote 9 excludes questions "relevant only to ... supervised release status," and names pornography-viewing as its example (*id.* at 1150 n.9).

## 6. CONCLUSION — which side has the better argument, and how confident I am

**Issue 1 — strong, with confidence.** With *Eaglin* leading and *Loy*/*Reeves*/*Simmons*/*Farrell* supporting, the challenge to SC ¶ 8 and ¶ 4 is the strongest single argument in Part II. *Eaglin* struck a **statutorily defined** ban imposed by an Article III judge who gave a reason on the record. SC ¶ 8 is an **undefined** ban, imposed by a private practice, that expressly makes the enforcer the definer, with no findings of any kind. The government's best cards — *Cabot*/*Simmons* notice, *Phipps*/*Bee*, and *Farrell*'s loss — each have a clean answer, and the pre-enforcement posture is the answer to the biggest one. **The one thing that would weaken this materially** is if the government produces a clinician's written opinion that access to adult material is contraindicated for Mr. Bernecky specifically. *Eaglin* at 100 leaves that door open. It is worth knowing in advance whether such a document exists.

**Issue 2 — moderate, and only in the reframed version.** As the client stated it, the argument loses to *Kunz*. As reframed through *Browder*'s asymmetry and *Kunz*'s own "exceeds the authority actually delegated" limit, it is sound and requires the Court to adopt no new rule. I am confident in the reframed version and would not file the original.

**Issue 3 — strong on the law, with one drafting risk.** *Von Behren* supports everything the client wants it to support. The risk is not the authority; it is the **rule number** and the **allocation**. ## 8. FOR THE WRITER — plain-English punch list

*Recommendations only. I have not edited `MOTION-v5.md`.*

**1. Rebuild the SC ¶ 8 / ¶ 4 argument around *Eaglin*, not around a First Amendment theory borrowed from *Loy*.**
Lead with this: the Second Circuit struck a ban on legal adult pornography in 2019 — a ban that was **precisely defined** by reference to 18 U.S.C. § 2256(2) and **entered by a federal judge who stated a reason on the record**. It struck it because "[s]uch bans must be supported on the record by detailed factual findings" and because the district court "erroneously treated the possession of adult pornography as reprehensible in itself, rather than as an otherwise lawful action." *Eaglin*, 913 F.3d at 99, 100. Then the turn: SC ¶ 8 is not defined at all, was not entered by a judge, and rests on no findings, because no judge has ever seen it. *Eaglin* is already in the motion for the internet point; it is doing a fraction of the work it could do.

**2. Do not say *Peterson* addressed legal adult pornography as a legal question.**
It did not. Footnote 1, at 248 F.3d at 86 n.1, expressly records that Peterson "has not objected to this condition on appeal." Use *Peterson* for one thing here: "Nor would defendant's proclivity toward pornography justify a ban on all books, magazines, and newspapers." 248 F.3d at 83. That sentence is aimed at SC ¶ 4's "narratives" and it is the Second Circuit's own.

**3. *Loy* is cleared for use — but for vagueness and delegation, not for the headline.**
The part29 instruction was a "nobody has read this yet" flag, not a bad-law flag, and it has now been satisfied. Use *Loy*, 237 F.3d at 266: "there is a real danger that the prohibition on pornography may ultimately translate to a prohibition on whatever the officer personally finds titillating," and "[a] condition with no core meaning beyond 'whatever is necessary for [the defendant's] rehabilitation' cannot be cured by allowing the probation officer an unfettered power of interpretation." SC ¶ 8 makes that cure its operative mechanism — "as defined by my treatment staff." **Do not write that *Loy* held a legal-adult-pornography ban unconstitutional.** Same paragraph, 237 F.3d at 267: "the Constitution would not forbid a more tightly defined restriction on legal, adult pornography."

**4. *Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77 (2d Cir. 2010), decides SC ¶ 2. Put it in Part II.B ahead of *Boles*.**
This is the biggest find of the dispatch and it was sitting unread in the file. The Second Circuit **vacated** a condition requiring a supervisee to "notify the Probation Department when he establishes a significant romantic relationship and ... inform the other party of his prior criminal history concerning his sex offenses." 591 F.3d at 80. Four holdings, all directly usable:
   - **Vagueness**, at 81: "We easily conclude that people of common intelligence (or, for that matter, of high intelligence) would find it impossible to agree on the proper application of a release condition triggered by entry into a 'significant romantic relationship.'"
   - **The test**, at 81: "a subjective element in a release condition may be sufficiently specific if tethered to objective criteria" — and here there was "no objective baseline." Quote this for ¶ 8 as well; it is the cleanest statement of the standard in the circuit.
   - **Who decides**, at 82: "We have no doubt that in the appropriate circumstance **a court, on the recommendation of the Probation Department**, could require a defendant to notify third-parties of risks arising from the defendant's criminal record, personal history, or characteristics." A court, on a recommendation. Not a private practice, on nobody's recommendation, as to an entire class of people, triggered by nothing.
   - **Social isolation**, at 82 — and this answers item 21's second bullet without any clinical literature at all: "Nor is it at all apparent that such a notification requirement will promote his rehabilitation. To the contrary, the requirement would almost certainly adversely affect, and could very well prematurely end, any intimate relationship he might develop, **placing him at a greater risk of social isolation and thus impair, rather than enhance, his rehabilitation**."
   - And the standard for conditions burdening intimate association, at 82-83: "a deprivation of that liberty is 'reasonably necessary' only if the deprivation is narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest."

The draft reads: "'A condition is too vague if it requires a reasonable person to speculate as to its meaning.' *United States v. Carlineo*, 998 F.3d 533, 536 (2d Cir. 2021) (citing *United States v. **12. Own the three adverse authorities in Mr. Bernecky's own voice.** (See § 5.1.7 for the full answers.)
