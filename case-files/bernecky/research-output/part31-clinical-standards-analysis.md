## 0. WHAT THIS IS, AND WHAT IT IS NOT

This is a professional-literature memorandum, not a legal one. It does not repeat, and does not depend on, the delegation/vagueness/Fifth Amendment analysis in `part29-treatment-contract-analysis.md` or MOTION-v5 Part II. It asks a different question: **measured against the published standards of the clinical profession that produced this document, does the R.K.K.S. Treatment Contract look like sex-offense-specific treatment as that profession defines it?**

The client's framing was that the contract "violates some of the most basic tenants of treatment." My conclusion, stated up front so the rest can be read against it: **on four of the five points, yes, and demonstrably so from published, citable, professional-association sources. On one point — social isolation as a risk factor — the underlying empirical literature is genuinely more mixed than the client's framing assumes, and the motion must be careful there or it will be corrected.**

**A structural finding worth stating first.** The single most useful source I found is not a journal article. It is the **ATSA Best Practice Guidelines**, the practice-standard document of the professional association for this exact specialty. I read both the current 6th edition (2025) and the prior 2014 edition in full. Several of the R.K.K.S. contract's most objectionable terms are not merely unsupported by those guidelines — they are the specific practices the guidelines tell members **not** to engage in, in language close enough to be quoted directly against the document.

## 2. ISSUE ONE — "ACCEPTABLE PROOF OF INFORMED CONSENT" FROM A SEXUAL PARTNER

**Contract term at issue.** Special Conditions ¶ 2: "I understand that I am required to disclose all my intimate partners to treatment staff. I understand that I need to provide **acceptable proof of informed consent** to treatment staff which includes **disclosure of my offense history in full** prior to engaging in a sexual relationship."

### 2.1 The professional-standard proposition

**Partner and support-person disclosure is a recognized practice in this field. But in every published formulation I found, the clinician does the work and the clinician bears the responsibility. The client is never made the guarantor of a third party's state of mind, and no published standard uses the phrase "proof of informed consent" as something the client must produce.**

This is not a matter of inference. The ATSA Guidelines address third-party disclosure in four separate places, and in each one the obligation runs to the *member*, not the client:

**(a) Support persons.** ATSA Adult Practice Guidelines (2014) 20.05: "**Members educate clients and identified support persons** regarding the roles, responsibilities, expectations, and risks and benefits associated with serving as part of a collaborative support network, **and elicit informed consent accordingly**." The clinician educates the third party. The clinician elicits the consent.

**(b) Chaperones** — the closest structural analogue in the literature to a "disclosure to a person at risk" requirement. 2014 Guidelines 21.01–21.03 make the *member* responsible for recommending only chaperones who "[a]ccept and understand the client's history of sexually abusive behavior," for ensuring "that clients educate potential chaperones candidly about the clients' sexually abusive behaviors, antecedent and ongoing risk factors, and treatment and/or supervision conditions," and for ensuring "that chaperones fully understand the safety plan." Even here, where the client does the telling, the *clinician* verifies the understanding — the client is not asked to prove it.

**(c) Contact with a child.** 2014 Guidelines 22.07: "**Members obtain informed consent** from a child's non-offending parent or legal guardian before approving a client's contact with that child." Again: the member obtains it.

**(d) Family safety planning.** 6th ed. (2025) 31.01: where contact with children is contemplated, "a comprehensive family safety and wellness plan should be established with the client and their intimate partner **facilitated by professionals with expertise in risk, risk management, risk reduction, and child protection** and considered by a multidisciplinary team."

**The affirmative posture toward intimate partners in the current guidelines is inclusion, not gatekeeping.** 6th ed. 14.26: members should "[c]onsider whether it would be appropriate and possible to **include** individuals in the client's relationship network in their treatment to further support the development of relationship skills, support relationship health and stability, maximize treatment gains and enhance prosocial lifestyles." 14.28: "encourage the client to consider **inviting** family members and significant others to actively participate in the treatment process." The 2014 edition said the same thing more bluntly at 11.12: "Members aim, when possible and appropriate, to include adult romantic partners in treatment in order to maximize treatment gains and enhance prosocial lifestyles."

Note the verbs. *Consider. Include. Invite. Encourage. Facilitate.* Not "require the client to produce proof."

**The Colorado SOMB Standards confirm the same architecture from a regulator's side.** Colorado treats a partner's awareness of the offense as a **criterion of the client's treatment progress**, assessed by the team, with the provider supplying the education: an offender at the relevant stage "is engaged in relationships which are supportive of treatment, and the people engaged in relationships with the offender demonstrate awareness of the sex offender's criminal history including the current offense," and "those who are in either in intimate relationships with the offender or are co-habiting with the offender are willing to participate in treatment and sex offense-specific education **as needed**." Colorado SOMB Standards, Progress/Completion criteria, Environment Management. That is a clinical judgment about relationship quality made by a treatment team over time. It is not a documentary precondition to sex.

### 2.2 The "informed consent" term itself — the client's point, and it is a good one

The client's instinct — that "informed consent" is being used as a term of art that a layperson cannot satisfy — is correct, and it is sharper than he framed it, because **this profession has its own settled clinical meaning for "informed consent" in a sexual context, and it is not the meaning the contract assigns.**

The Colorado SOMB Standards contain a client-facing curriculum module titled, precisely, "Informed Consent," teaching sex-offense-specific treatment clients what informed consent to sexual activity means. It states three rules:

> "**Rule #1:** Both parties must be able to say 'Yes' or 'No' without fear of penalty or harm."
> "**Rule #2:** Both parties must know what they are consenting to and both parties must understand the outcomes and consequences of that decision."
> "**Rule #3:** The two parties must have equal power."

And in the accompanying healthy-sexuality guidance for providers: "Consent involves communication in advance of what will take place (sexual activity) between two individuals. It involves mutuality and reciprocity. ... [T]he needs and desires of both parties are negotiable and negotiated; an agreement is reached prior to the activity ensuing."

**Informed consent, in the clinical vocabulary of this specialty, is consent to the sexual act — its nature, its consequences, and freedom from coercion. It has nothing to do with the partner's knowledge of the client's criminal record.** The R.K.K.S. contract borrows the field's own term and silently substitutes a different content: disclosure of "my offense history in full," reduced to documentary "proof," graded for acceptability by staff.

The APA Ethics Code supplies the parallel professional meaning on the treatment side and confirms whose obligation informed consent is: it is something a **psychologist obtains from a person receiving services**, using "language that is reasonably understandable to that person," APA Ethics Code Std. 3.10(a) — not something a patient procures from a third party and files with the clinic.

### 2.3 How the contract compares — and the isolation consequence

Four departures, each independently identifiable:

1. **Role inversion.** Every published protocol assigns the disclosure/education/consent function to the clinician. ¶ 2 assigns it to the patient and reserves to staff only the power to grade the result.
2. **Evidentiary form.** No source I found — ATSA (either edition), the ATSA Code of Ethics, the APA Code, or the Colorado SOMB Standards — requires or contemplates *documentary proof* of a third party's consent as a precondition to a patient's private conduct. I searched for one specifically. It does not appear.
3. **Terminological substitution.** ¶ 2 uses the field's term for consent-to-sex to mean disclosure-of-record, without saying so.
4. **No standard, no reviewer, no record.** "Acceptable" is undefined; the grader is the same entity that can discharge him under Consequences ¶ 2(vi).

**And the practical consequence is the one the client identified.** The current ATSA guidelines describe forming a support network as *already* difficult for this population and instruct members to make it easier, not harder: 6th ed. 14.31 — "Recognize that developing a support network may be challenging and gradual for clients based on their past sexually abusive behaviors, criminal histories, periods of institutionalization as well as their own level of defensiveness and mistrust. As a result, members should support clients to make small and gradual changes, ... **acknowledge the challenges of establishing a new social support network** and integrating into a prosocial lifestyle, and encourage and support clients to establish and preserve a healthy lifestyle."

A term that makes a documented, staff-graded disclosure a precondition to any sexual relationship does the opposite of 14.31. It converts the single relationship category the guidelines most want built (14.24, 14.26, and 2014 ed. 11.11–11.12) into the one requiring the most paperwork and the most exposure. **That is not an inference about human nature; it is the direct opposite of the operational instruction the professional association gives its own members.**

**Confidence: high.** This is the best-supported of the five points. The sources are the specialty's own practice standards, they are unanimous in the direction they run, and the contract's departure from them is structural rather than a matter of degree.

## 3. ISSUE TWO — SOCIAL ISOLATION AS RISK FACTOR OR TREATMENT GOAL

**Contract terms at issue.** Cumulatively: SC ¶ 2 (partner disclosure and proof), SC ¶ 5 (staff pre-approval before using dating sites, social media, email, text apps, chat rooms), SC ¶ 9 (24-hour reporting of "any accidental, incidental, or intentional contact" with, *inter alia*, "vulnerable populations (as defined by treatment staff)"), Confidentiality ¶ 3 (no contact with other clients outside group without staff approval), and Code of Conduct ¶ 6 (report changes in "relationship status, social networks," "means of communication").

### 3.1 The proposition, in the form the literature actually supports

**Supported, and strongly:** *Building prosocial relationships and social support is an affirmative treatment objective of sex-offense-specific treatment, and reducing social isolation is understood as supporting desistance.*

- **ATSA 6th ed. (2025) 14.30:** "Recognize that in addition to having prosocial community support people, it is critical to assist and support clients to develop and maintain prosocial community supports such as stable and appropriate housing, health care, financial security, education, employment, and recreational and leisure activities, **all of which support desistance from sexually abusive behaviors and are vital to achieve and maintain a prosocial lifestyle**."
- **ATSA 6th ed. 14.24:** members should "[a]ssist clients in developing an understanding of intimacy in sexual and non-sexual relationships (e.g., family, platonic friendships, romantic, and sexual), ... and to develop skills to facilitate participation in intimate relationships."
- **ATSA 2014 ed. 10.06:** risk-management plans "include specific strategies for avoiding or limiting access to potential victims, recognizing and coping with risk factors, and **building social support systems**."
- **ATSA 2014 ed. 11.15:** members "assist clients who are transitioning to the community or are already in the community to develop and maintain **stable, prosocial lifestyles**, which are characterized by stable and appropriate housing, employment, and leisure activities."
- **Clarke, Brown & Völlm (2017), 29 Sexual Abuse at 447:** "successful reintegration is integral to reducing the likelihood of reoffending. For example, **isolation and loneliness are potential risk factors in sexual offending** (Edwards & Hensley, 2001; Marshall, 2010). Thus, communities that fail to reintegrate offenders may, **counterintuitively, also fail to quell important risk factors**." And at 471: "**By helping to reduce social isolation, Circles support desistance from sexual offending.** This facilitation of community inclusion, together with nonjudgmental social support, is consistent with the GLM."
- **de Vries Robbé, Mann, Maruna & Thornton (2015)**, discussing Farmer, Beech & Ward (2012): desisters "seemed to have found a place within a social group or network. ... **In contrast, the 'active' or at-risk group all described themselves as socially alienated or isolated from others.**" The same paper identifies "**Constructive social and professional support network**" as one of eight proposed protective domains, and describes "lack of emotional intimacy with others" as "a strongly evidenced risk factor for sexual offending (Mann et al., 2010)."
- **Good Lives Model, as described in de Vries Robbé et al.:** "as the establishment of a romantic intimate relationship may increase the opportunity to achieve sexual Goods through consensual and reciprocal sexual activity, this will reduce the likelihood of attempting to find sexual gratification through inappropriate strategies." And the paper's summary line, which is the most quotable sentence I found on this issue: "**Protective-oriented programs aim to build resources rather than strip away risk factors.**"
- **de Vries Robbé et al.** also anticipates the exact structural point this motion makes: "In the case of sexual offending in particular, **restrictive external circumstances are frequently imposed upon the individual against his preference, such as incarceration, residency restrictions, social isolation, and restricted employment opportunities. If these external circumstances are guided by empirical evidence, they can be an important part of risk management processes** to create more protective environments." The conditional is the point: the authors treat externally imposed isolation as legitimate **only** when empirically guided.

### 3.2 Where the literature is genuinely mixed — read this before drafting

**The proposition "social isolation causes sexual reoffense" is NOT what the meta-analytic literature says, and a motion that asserts it flatly can be embarrassed.**

Hanson & Morton-Bourgon's 2004 meta-analysis — the field's foundational predictor study — reports (per the Public Safety Canada page summary; **§ 1.2 caveat applies**):

- **Loneliness did not predict sexual recidivism**: "There was no evidence that sexual recidivism was predicted by ... loneliness (d = .03)."
- **Social skills deficits did not predict**: "sexual recidivism was unrelated to social skills deficits (d = −.07)."
- **But conflicts in intimate relationships did**: d = .36.
- **And emotional identification with children did**: d = .42.
- The authors' own summary: "**Some measures of intimacy deficits predicted sexual recidivism, whereas others did not.**"

The 2014 ATSA Guidelines reflect exactly this nuance when they list the empirically supported risk categories: "Intimacy and relationship difficulties (e.g., unstable relationships, **conflictual** intimate relationships, deficits in social support, restricted social interaction and involvement)." 2014 ed. 6.02. Note that the category is *relationship difficulty*, not *aloneness*; the same list survives into the 6th edition.

Clarke et al. use the deliberately hedged phrase "**potential** risk factors." And Craun & Bierie (2014), 78 Fed. Probation 28 — a **U.S. Courts** publication — is a methodological critique arguing that the collateral-consequences literature may overstate registry-specific harm because it lacks appropriate comparison groups. A government responder who reads around this area will find it.

**So the honest, defensible framing is:**

> Sex-offense-specific treatment treats the formation and maintenance of prosocial and intimate relationships as an **affirmative objective** of treatment, and treats intimacy and relationship deficits as among the empirically supported dynamic risk factors that treatment exists to reduce. A provider term whose predictable effect is to make those relationships harder to form works against the treatment the Court ordered.

**Do NOT write:** "Isolation causes reoffending" / "the literature establishes that social isolation increases recidivism risk." It does not, in that form.

**The cleanest single-sentence support** for the framing above is ATSA 6th ed. 14.30 (relationships and community supports "support desistance from sexually abusive behaviors and are vital to achieve and maintain a prosocial lifestyle"), paired with de Vries Robbé et al.'s "[p]rotective-oriented programs aim to build resources rather than strip away risk factors."

### 3.3 How the contract compares

The contract's cumulative structure inverts the guidelines' relationship architecture at every level of relationship the guidelines identify:

| ATSA 6th ed. target | Contract term |
|---|---|
| 14.24, 14.26 — build intimate relationships; consider including partners in treatment | SC ¶ 2 — disclose all partners; produce graded documentary proof before sex |
| 14.27–14.29 — identify and invite prosocial support people | Requirements ¶ 3 — treatment content discussed with "other significant people in my life"; Confidentiality ¶ 3 — no contact with fellow clients outside group without staff approval |
| 14.30 — housing, employment, financial security, leisure as supports | Code of Conduct ¶ 6 — report income/debt, mobility, "means of communication," "social networks" to the clinic |
| 14.31 — support gradual network-building; acknowledge the difficulty | SC ¶ 5 — pre-approval before using dating sites, social media, text apps, email; SC ¶ 9 — 24-hour report of *accidental* contact with an undefined "vulnerable population" |

**A specific, non-obvious point worth one sentence in the motion.** ¶ 5's pre-approval requirement expressly reaches **dating sites**. Colorado's SOMB curriculum treats dating-site and social-media use as ordinary subject matter for *therapeutic discussion* of a client's consensual relationship history — it appears in the Consensual Sexual Activity module ("please include information regarding the use of dating sites, chat rooms and other forms of social media"), as material to be *talked about*, not as conduct requiring a permission slip.

**Confidence: moderate-to-high on the framing in § 3.2; low on any framing that asserts isolation causes recidivism.**

## 4. ISSUE THREE — UNDEFINED, STAFF-DISCRETION PROHIBITIONS

**Contract terms at issue.** SC ¶ 8 ("sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind **as defined by my treatment staff**"); Consequences ¶ 2(vi) ("other problem behavior not outlined in this Treatment Contract, **as determined in the sole discretion of the staff of the Practice**"); Code of Conduct ¶ 5 ("any **relapse in problematic behavior**"); SC ¶ 9 ("vulnerable populations (**as defined by treatment staff**)"); Code of Conduct ¶ 6 ("any other changes in relevant risk factors ... **etc.**").

### 4.1 The proposition: clarity of rules and consequences is a stated practice standard

**The best single source in this memo is ATSA Adult Practice Guidelines (2014) 14.01** — and the Writer should note the § 1.4 caveat that this text is from the superseded edition:

> "**14.01 Members ensure clarity and agreement between the provider and clients. Such agreement addresses, at a minimum, the following:**
> • The nature, goals, and objectives of treatment;
> • The expected frequency and duration of treatment;
> • **Rules and expectations of treatment program participants;**
> • Rewards and incentives for participation and progress;
> • **Consequences of non-compliance with program rules and expectations;** and
> • **Criteria used for assessing progress and determining program completion.**"

Read against Consequences ¶ 2(vi), that guideline is dispositive on its own terms. A provision authorizing immediate discharge for "other problem behavior **not outlined in this Treatment Contract**" is a written acknowledgment that the rules and the consequences of non-compliance have **not** been made clear — the contract says as much in the operative clause.

The current edition carries the same principle in the engagement register rather than as a checklist:

- **6th ed. 12.07:** "Recognize that client engagement may increase, and resistance may decrease, when the treatment provider and client are in relative agreement about treatment goals and objectives. As such, to the extent possible, members should **involve clients in the development of their treatment plans and in the identification of realistic goals and objectives**." (2014 ed. 13.03 is materially identical.)
- **6th ed. 12.08:** "Explore and clarify, at the onset of treatment, the client's understanding of why they have been referred to treatment ... Clients should be **clearly informed** that the primary treatment targets ... include ... offense precursors and risk factors; sexual interests, arousal, and behaviors; attitudes; intimacy deficits; coping skills; and risk management and risk reduction strategies."
- **6th ed. 10.02:** the individualized written treatment plan must outline "Treatment goals, **time-limited objectives**, and targets for therapeutic intervention." (2014 ed. 9.03: "**clear and specific** treatment goals and objectives.")

### 4.2 The prohibition-specific proposition: treatment targets must be criminogenic, and blanket material bans are disfavored

Two sources speak directly to SC ¶ 8's ban on "sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind."

**(a) ATSA 2014 ed. 11.10** — a guideline whose whole point is that a clinician's disapproval is not a treatment target:

> "Members are aware that clients may hold attitudes, beliefs, and values that are **unconventional but unrelated to their risk** for sexually abusive or criminal behaviors; these unconventional attitudes, beliefs, and values are **not deemed appropriate primary treatment targets**."

The parallel 6th-edition provision is Guideline 15, which permits non-criminogenic targets only where they "enhance therapeutic alliance, treatment engagement, and treatment responsiveness" — a permission for things like trauma history, not a licence for open-ended prohibition.

**(b) Colorado SOMB Standards, healthy-sexuality guidance to providers** — the most directly on-point sentence I found anywhere:

> "Masturbation (when not compulsive and done privately) is a natural and healthy practice ... As people do masturbate, stimuli for masturbation need to be based on healthy themes, such as closeness, intimacy, mutuality, reciprocity, and safety. This does not rule out visual stimuli which are ubiquitous. **Prohibiting stimulating materials is problematic and impossible.** Instead **it is a task of treatment to determine which materials are 'inappropriate,'** by not reinforcing the values and principles stated herein (e.g., mutuality, reciprocity, safety, etc.). On the other hand, stimuli that reinforce these values are not problematic. **It is not the goal of treatment to eliminate sexuality or sexual expression, rather to direct it to appropriate themes.**"

That is a state sex-offender-management board telling its own approved providers that the exact prohibition SC ¶ 8 imposes is "problematic and impossible." It is not binding on R.K.K.S., and the Writer must say so. It is nonetheless the single most quotable clinical statement available against ¶ 8.

**(c) And the current ATSA guidelines say, in terms, that supervision conditions of this kind are not all created equal.** 6th ed. Guideline 25.01: "Members should also be aware that **appropriate risk management strategies will vary depending on the client and case dynamics** and should be able to determine which risk management approaches are relevant for different clients. **Risk management protocols and conditions often include both empirically grounded and empirically unsupported strategies.**" Guideline 24.01: "While some policies and practices complement treatment and support risk reduction and prevention strategies, **others have not received empirical support for being relevant to risk management, risk reduction, or crime prevention**." Guideline 25.01's own enumerated list of common risk-management protocols includes "Technology and internet restrictions" and "Abstain conditions" — i.e., ATSA classifies exactly the categories at issue as *risk-management* impositions to be individually justified, not as treatment.

### 4.3 Discretion-based enforcement and the therapeutic alliance

The literature on why vague, discretion-based enforcement is *clinically* costly (as distinct from legally infirm) runs through the therapeutic-alliance research.

- **Levenson (2013/2014), *J. Sexual Aggression*, at 9–10** (read in full): "**Sex offender treatment programmes have historically utilised confrontational strategies. Ironically, however, such approaches may serve to recreate traumatic experiences in the clinical setting and reinforce dysfunctional coping strategies** once used to adapt to a traumatogenic environment." And: "clinicians should model respectful and collaborative communication **lest they reproduce and reinforce the very types of disempowering relational patterns they seek to correct**." And on outcome magnitude: "it is clear in the general psychotherapy research literature that **the therapeutic alliance and a client's experience of the counselling relationship account for significantly more variance in outcomes than theoretical framework or specific counselling techniques**."
- **Levenson**, on group process specifically — directly relevant to a contract that pairs a candor mandate with punitive uncertainty: "persistent, harsh or adversarial confrontation by group members or therapists can **inhibit clients from being forthcoming in treatment**. ... When members see honesty and disclosure being rewarded with support and encouragement, anxiety and threat will be reduced, thereby decreasing the need for defensive posturing."
- **Colorado SOMB Standard 3.130** states the same finding as a regulatory standard, with the field's citations attached: "Research has shown that therapists who demonstrated an **empathic, warm, rewarding, and directive** approach resulted in the greatest positive changes in clients who have sexually offended. Research has shown that a **challenging and supportive approach, rather than a harsh confrontational style, produced increased treatment benefits**." (SOMB's own footnotes 72–73 cite Glaser (2009); Marshall (2005); Marshall, Serran, Moulden, Mulloy, Fernandez, Mann & Thornton (2002); and Marshall, Serran, Fernandez, Mulloy, Mann & Thornton (2003). **I have not read any of those underlying studies** — cite them, if at all, as cited in SOMB 3.130.)
- **SMART/SOMAPI Ch. 7** (§ 1.2 caveat): "The way in which treatment is delivered accounts for far more of the observed treatment benefits than does the application of appropriate procedures ... The manner in which a therapist delivers a program protocol and **the therapeutic alliance with the offender are important moderators of treatment success**."
- **ATSA 6th ed. 10.02** treats this as a planning requirement, not an aspiration: the written treatment plan must specify "**Therapeutic styles that can optimize motivation, engagement, and therapeutic alliance**."

### 4.4 How the contract compares

- ¶ 2(vi) is an express admission that the rules are not written down (2014 ed. 14.01, third and fifth bullets).
- ¶ 8's contraband category is fixed by a definition that has never been reduced to writing and can change without notice — the opposite of "clear and specific treatment goals" (2014 ed. 9.03) and, per SOMB, a prohibition the field regards as "problematic and impossible."
- Nothing in the contract reflects the client involvement 12.07 calls for; the document is a signature block on a form (see § 6).
- The one place the contract records a *therapeutic* rationale for anything is SC ¶ 7's list of discussion topics. No term identifies a criminogenic need it is addressing — which is what 11.10/Guideline 15 and 25.01 require before a non-criminogenic restriction is imposed.

**Confidence: high**, with the version caveat on 14.01 and the jurisdictional caveat on SOMB.

## 5. ISSUE FOUR — THE TREATMENT / SUPERVISION / LAW-ENFORCEMENT BOUNDARY

**Contract terms at issue.** Consequences ¶ 3(a) ("The Treatment Team is comprised of all therapists in the Practice, **the Probation Officer, and the Probation Supervisor**"); Confidentiality ¶ 2 (reports to Probation "and **other individuals and agencies** involved in my treatment"); Consequences ¶ 1 ("**law enforcement may be contacted**"); Code of Conduct ¶ 6 (report "criminal behaviors [outside of or in addition to Instant Offense or pending charges]"); Requirements ¶ 1 ("full acknowledgement of my actions").

**This is the point on which the professional literature is most directly and most usefully hostile to the document.**

### 5.1 The role-boundary standard — ATSA says this in terms

**ATSA Best Practice Guidelines for Men (6th ed. 2025)**, in the Risk Management section, states four rules. Three of them are violated on the face of Consequences ¶ 3(a):

> "• **Treatment providers limit their role to that of clinicians and do not attempt to assume the roles of supervision officers or law enforcement agents or represent themselves as such.**"
> "• Probation and parole officers do not represent themselves as specialized treatment providers to clients unless they possess the requisite education, training, supervision, licensure, and continuing education ..."
> "• **Probation and parole officers should not assume specialized clinical responsibilities within treatment programs for men who have committed sexually abusive behaviors (e.g., individual or group therapy) with clients for whom they have supervision responsibility.** If this is unavoidable, probation and parole officers should seek support to manage their dual roles and engage in peer consultation or supervision to help them do this."

The 2014 edition states the same rules at Guideline 19.02 in nearly identical words, under the heading: "**Members recognize the distinct but potentially complementary roles and responsibilities of treatment providers and supervision officers, clarify these roles and responsibilities to clients and other professionals, and actively strive to maintain these professional boundaries.**"

**And ATSA treats even the weakest form of the arrangement — an officer merely *observing* a session — as something requiring caution and consent.** 6th ed.:

> "Treatment providers exercise caution if institutions require that probation and parole officers be present in clinical treatment sessions or programs for clients. In this regard, members recognize:
> — **The presence of probation and parole officers in treatment events can influence client confidentiality, inhibit client participation and disclosure, influence the therapeutic alliance, disrupt continuity of the treatment process, and blur clients' perceptions of officers' and therapist roles.**
> — **The presence of nonclinical professionals in therapy groups is inconsistent with best practices** and can compromise group therapy design and dynamics as well as the experience of group members."

And if it happens anyway, members should "[o]btain appropriate informed and voluntary consent from clients when applicable" and "[e]nsure that probation and parole officers clarify their roles and responsibilities as supervision officers to the clients." (2014 ed. 19.03–19.06 is the same, with 19.05 reading: "Members recognize that such observations can also impact the client's confidentiality, **inhibiting client participation and disclosure**; disrupt continuity of the treatment process; and blur clients' perceptions of officers' roles.")

**The comparison writes itself.** ATSA says an officer *sitting in a room* is a confidentiality and alliance problem requiring caution and consent. Consequences ¶ 3(a) makes the officer and her supervisor **voting members of the body that decides discharge** — a "specialized clinical responsibilit[y] within [a] treatment program[] ... with clients for whom they have supervision responsibility," in the guidelines' exact phrase.

### 5.2 What the reporting relationship is supposed to look like

ATSA does not merely say Probation should stay outside the clinical role. It specifies the **content** of the information-sharing relationship, and the list is remarkably close to the relief MOTION-v5 Part II already requests at item (4).

**2014 ed. 18.04:** members "establish and clarify the appropriate parameters (e.g., timing, type of content) and mechanisms ... for reciprocal information-sharing with the probation/parole officer.... **Information to be shared minimally includes the following:** • Attendance in treatment; • Overall participation in treatment; • Specific changes in dynamic risk factors and protective factors; • Progress toward specific goals in treatment; • Engagement and compliance with supervision; • Referrals to and/or participation in additional programs and services; and • Adjustments to level of supervision or supervision strategies."

**6th ed. 19.01** states the same content for progress/completion/discharge reporting: "The nature of the treatment provided or completed ...; Attendance; Level of engagement and participation; Progress made on specific treatment goals; and Recommendations for future treatment ...."

**Neither list includes session notes, raw testing data, internal case records, or disclosures of uncharged conduct.** That is a meaningful omission in a document that is otherwise exhaustive about what should be shared. It supports Part II's relief item (4) directly and from the profession's own side.

### 5.3 Confidentiality limits must be specific, in writing, and must name the recipients

- **ATSA 6th ed. 4.01** requires that the client be told the "**Specific limits on confidentiality. This includes identifying what information will be included in the report, who will and who might (with their consent) receive the report and have access to the information contained in it, and the circumstances under which information may otherwise be released**," and separately the "**Mandatory reporting requirements**."
- **ATSA 2014 ed. 2.05**, same requirement: "Specifying limits on confidentiality, such as **persons or entities to whom the findings will be provided** and the circumstances under which information may otherwise be released."
- **ATSA Professional Code of Ethics (2001) § 9(a)**: members are responsible for ensuring clients "fully understand issues related to confidentiality," including "(i) informing Clients of the limits of confidentiality; (ii) informing Clients of any circumstances that may cause an exception to the agreed upon confidentiality; (iii) **specifically informing Clients about mandatory reporting requirements**; and (iv) clarifying issues of confidentiality where multiple parties are involved." § 9(c) adds that where services are provided to several related persons, the member must "clarify at the outset how confidentiality will apply among participants and **to any external party (e.g., criminal justice agency)**."
- **APA Ethics Code Std. 4.02(a)**: psychologists discuss "(1) the relevant limits of confidentiality and (2) the **foreseeable uses of the information** generated through their psychological activities," and (b) "the discussion of confidentiality occurs at the outset of the relationship."
- **APA Std. 3.10(c)**: "When psychological services are **court ordered or otherwise mandated**, psychologists inform the individual of the nature of the anticipated services, **including whether the services are court ordered or mandated and any limits of confidentiality, before proceeding**."
- **APA Std. 3.07** (third-party requests): the psychologist must clarify at the outset "the role of the psychologist ..., **an identification of who is the client**, the probable uses of the services provided or the information obtained, and the fact that there may be limits to confidentiality."
- **APA Std. 3.11(a)** (services delivered to or through organizations): clients must be told, among other things, "(3) which of the individuals are clients, (4) the relationship the psychologist will have with each person and the organization, (5) the probable uses of services provided and information obtained, **(6) who will have access to the information**, and (7) limits of confidentiality."

**Confidentiality ¶ 2's phrase "other individuals and agencies involved in my treatment" fails every one of those provisions in the same way: it declines to identify the recipients.** Std. 3.11(a)(6) and 2014 ed. 2.05 are the two cleanest hits — both require naming *who will have access*, which is the one thing ¶ 2 refuses to do.

### 5.4 Compelled confession — the guideline that says not to do it

**ATSA 6th ed. (2025) 12.05** — the strongest single sentence in this memo against Requirements ¶ 1, SC ¶ 7, and Code of Conduct ¶ 6:

> "Support the client to be forthright in discussing their personal history, offending behaviors, and offense-specific contributing factors, **but recognize it is not the role of treatment providers to attempt to determine or verify a client's legal guilt or innocence or to secure confessions of unreported or undetected sexually abusive behaviors**. ... Attempting to have clients provide an account of their sexually abusive behavior that is completely consistent with the allegations or legal findings reported or identified in case background information **is however not typically a required or recommended treatment goal**."

The 2014 edition, 13.07, is if anything sharper on the verb: "it is not the role of treatment providers to attempt to determine or verify a client's legal guilt or innocence, **or to coerce confessions** of unreported or undetected sexually abusive behaviors."

The 6th edition adds, at 12.06/2014 ed. 13.06, that "the influence of denial and minimization in sexual recidivism risk **has not yet been clearly established** and may vary among client groups" — i.e., the premise of Requirements ¶ 1 ("successful treatment depends upon full acknowledgement of my actions") is stated by the profession as an open empirical question, not an established one.

**And ATSA has already identified the exact dilemma the R.K.K.S. contract creates, and prescribed a remedy the contract does not adopt.** 2014 ed., Appendix B (Polygraphy):

> "Members must ensure that limits of confidentiality are fully disclosed to clients prior to polygraph testing, and that clients are afforded the opportunity for informed consent, specifically with respect to the ways in which the findings will be used and to whom the findings will be provided. **Client disclosures of potentially incriminating information to mandated reporters can, lead to future prosecution. Members inform clients, in writing, of this potential dilemma and how it is addressed in their jurisdiction and program.**"

That is the professional association telling providers, in writing, to warn clients in writing that mandated disclosure may lead to prosecution, and to explain how the program handles it. **The R.K.K.S. contract does neither.** It commands total candor about uncharged criminal conduct (Code of Conduct ¶ 6), routes it to Probation and permits routing it to police (Confidentiality ¶ 2, Consequences ¶ 1), and never once acknowledges the dilemma. This is the strongest clinical complement available to MOTION-v5 Part IV.

### 5.5 The honest counterweight

There is a real scholarly position that this tension is *inherent* in sex-offense treatment and cannot be designed away. **Glaser (2009)** argues that clinicians in these programs are "forced to breach traditional mental health ethical principles in order to successfully achieve their outcomes, including placing the community's interest ahead of those of the offender, restricting confidentiality, and the imposition of involuntary treatment," such that the programs "take on the characteristics of punishment, rather than treatment." **I read only the abstract.** **Prescott & Levenson**, *Sex offender treatment is not punishment*, 16 J. Sexual Aggression 275 (2010), respond that treatment "can and should be conducted in a fashion consistent with professional codes of ethics" (abstract only, read from a PsycINFO-format compilation PDF).

**Two things follow for the Writer.** First, do **not** argue that reporting to Probation is itself unethical — ATSA 2014 ed. 18.05 affirmatively requires members to "report, in a timely manner, any violations of their clients' conditions of supervision," and Code of Ethics § 9(d) says nothing in the code releases members from mandated reporting. The argument is about **who sits inside the clinical role**, **whether the limits are specified**, and **whether confessions are coerced** — not about reporting as such. Second, if the government cites Glaser for "this is just how these programs work," the answer is that Glaser's critique is a reason to define the boundary carefully, and ATSA's guidelines are what defining it carefully looks like.

**Confidence: high.** This is the second-best-supported point after Issue One, and it has the advantage that the sources are the *current* edition of the specialty's own practice standards.

## 6. ISSUE FIVE — INDIVIDUALIZATION VS. THE BLANKET FORM

**Contract fact at issue.** SC ¶ 10: "I agree to abide by the following special conditions: **No special conditions designated**." Plus SC ¶ 1, addressed to a defendant on pretrial supervision or diversion, which Mr. Bernecky is not.

### 6.1 The client's phrase is correct: this *is* a recognized basic tenet

The client asked whether individualized treatment planning is actually "a most basic tenet of treatment," rather than something we would be asserting. **It is, and it is stated as a numbered guideline in both editions of the ATSA standards, as a Colorado regulatory standard, and as the responsivity principle of the dominant treatment model.**

**ATSA Best Practice Guidelines for Men (6th ed. 2025), Guideline 10** — a top-level numbered guideline, not a sub-item:

> "**Members recognize the importance of individualized treatment plans to optimize delivering effective and efficient treatment interventions.**"
> "**10.01** Ensure that, prior to initiating treatment, a treatment plan is identified and developed based on assessing and identifying individual risk, need, strengths, and responsivity issues."
> "**10.02 Develop and implement an individualized, written treatment plan for each client**, outlining: • Recommended treatment modalities and intensity of treatment; • Treatment goals, time-limited objectives, and targets for therapeutic intervention; • Therapeutic styles that can optimize motivation, engagement, and therapeutic alliance; • Research-supported assessment methods and instruments that can assist in identifying criminogenic risk factors present for a given client."
> "**10.03** Routinely review and update treatment plans ...."

**ATSA 2014 ed., Guideline 9**, to the same effect: "**Members recognize the importance of individualized, assessment-driven treatment services and deliver treatment accordingly**," and 9.03: "Members develop and implement an **individualized, written treatment plan for each client**, outlining **clear and specific** treatment goals and objectives that are consistent with the results of a current psychosexual evaluation." Guideline 9.01 requires that a current, comprehensive psychosexual evaluation exist **before** treatment begins.

**Both editions tie individualization to treatment completion itself.** 2014 ed., Guideline 14: "'successful completion' of a sexual abuser-specific treatment program/regimen indicates that a client has demonstrated sufficient progress in meeting the goals and objectives of **an individualized treatment plan**." 6th ed. 19.05 says the same. **Under the profession's own definition, a program with no individualized plan has no coherent standard for completion at all** — which is directly relevant to Requirements ¶ 2's assertion that "the length of time I spend in treatment is dependent on my readiness to change."

**Colorado SOMB Standard 3.160(A)(6)** states it as a regulatory command:

> "A treatment plan, with measurable goals, outcomes, and timeframes, shall be implemented after the completion of the intake evaluation process. The process shall be guided by the treatment provider and **developed through collaboration with the client**. **The individualized plan** shall promote victim and community safety. In addition, the plan shall identify the behaviors mandating offense-specific treatment and specifically address all clinical issues outlined in the intake evaluation and via validated risk assessment. **The treatment goals shall be consistent with the client's treatment needs, competency and ability. It shall include identified protective and risk factors. Treatment plans shall be written in a way that is understandable to the client** ...."

**And the RNR responsivity principle**, which both ATSA editions adopt as their organizing framework, is the theoretical statement of the same idea. Per SMART/SOMAPI Ch. 7 (§ 1.2 caveat): "Successful programs are responsive to the motivation, cognitive ability and other characteristics of the offender," and "**Treatment is apt to be most effective when it is tailored to the risks, needs and offense dynamics of individual sex offenders**."

### 6.2 The Colorado treatment-contract standard — what a compliant version of this document looks like

**This is the most useful comparator in the memo for Part II**, because it addresses the exact instrument. Colorado SOMB Standard **3.400 / 3.410**:

> "**3.410** The provider shall develop and utilize a written contract with each client prior to the commencement of treatment. The contract shall define the **specific responsibilities of both the provider and the client**. ...
> **B. The contract shall explain the responsibility of the provider to:**
> 1. List the costs of assessment, evaluation, and treatment, including all medical and psychological tests, physiological tests and consultations;
> 2. Describe the waivers of confidentiality and limits of confidentiality ...;
> 3. **Describe the right of the client to refuse treatment and to refuse to waive confidentiality**, as a result of which the provider will be unable to provide services. The contract shall also describe the potential outcomes of that decision;
> 4. **Describe the necessary procedures the client must follow in order to revoke a waiver of confidentiality**;
> 5. ... notify all clients in writing of the limits of confidentiality imposed on therapists by the mandatory reporting law ...;
> 6. Provide instructions and describe limitations regarding the client's contact with victims, secondary victims, and minor children ...; and
> 7. Establish expectations for the client to provide for the protection of past and potential victims ...."

Two features are decisive.

**First, the standard contract is bilateral.** It "define[s] the specific responsibilities of **both the provider and the client**," and seven of the enumerated items are *the provider's* obligations. **The R.K.K.S. contract contains no provider obligations at all.** Every one of its numbered paragraphs is a duty running from Mr. Bernecky to the Practice or a power running from the Practice to Mr. Bernecky. Read side by side with 3.410(B), that is conspicuous.

**Second — and this is the sentence that ties Issue Five to Issue Three — Colorado expressly contemplates the *category* of restriction SC ¶ 8 and ¶ 5 impose, and expressly conditions it on individualization.** SOMB 3.410, Discussion:

> "In addition, the provider **may** incorporate additional limits and expectations **based upon the client's identified risks, needs and patterns of behavior**. For example, limits may be placed regarding the use of **pornography/sexually stimulating material**, substance use, or **internet use**, **as appropriate**."

So: pornography and internet limits in a treatment contract are legitimate — **when tied to that client's identified risks, needs and patterns of behavior.** The R.K.K.S. contract imposes both as blanket form terms and then writes "**No special conditions designated**" in the space reserved for what this client actually needs.

**That juxtaposition is the whole of Issue Five in two sentences, and it is the most efficient clinical argument in this memo.**

### 6.3 Discharge criteria, individualized

Colorado also shows what a professionally structured discharge provision looks like. SOMB 3.200(B) enumerates a **closed list** of five grounds for pre-completion discharge — Administrative Transfer, Therapeutic Transfer, Medical Discharge, Incompetency Discharge, and "**Non-Compliance Discharge – The client's behavior is contradictory to the treatment and/or supervision conditions and the treatment provider, in consultation with the other CST members, determines that the client is no longer an appropriate candidate for the treatment program**." And 3.210 requires a written discharge summary identifying, if unsuccessful, "**specific violations of the treatment contract**," the goals completed and in process, and current risk and protective factors.

Compare Consequences ¶ 2(vi): immediate discharge for "other problem behavior **not outlined in this Treatment Contract**, as determined in the **sole discretion** of the staff." A discharge standard that expressly rests on unwritten conduct cannot produce the written discharge summary the profession expects, because there is nothing specific to write.

**Confidence: high.** Individualized written treatment planning is a numbered top-level guideline in the current ATSA standards, a regulatory command in Colorado, and the responsivity leg of the RNR model. The client's phrase — "one of the most basic tenets of treatment" — is accurate.

## 7. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Was the client right?** Substantially, yes — and on more grounds than he identified.

Measured against the published standards of its own profession, the R.K.K.S. Treatment Contract departs from recognized practice in at least five identifiable ways, each supported by a professional-association practice standard rather than by argument:

1. It has **no individualized treatment plan** (ATSA 6th ed. 10, 10.02; 2014 ed. 9, 9.03) and says so on its own face.
2. It is **unilateral**, containing none of the provider-side obligations a professionally structured treatment contract is expected to contain (Colo. SOMB 3.410(B)).
3. It **seats supervision officers inside the clinical decision-making body**, which the current ATSA guidelines specifically instruct against, and does so in a setting where ATSA counsels caution and consent even for mere observation.
4. It **commands disclosure of uncharged criminal conduct** to a mandated reporter that may contact police, without the written warning ATSA's own guidelines require when incriminating disclosure is foreseeable — and it does so in service of a "full acknowledgement" premise the profession describes as empirically unsettled, in the teeth of a guideline saying it is "not the role of treatment providers ... to secure confessions of unreported or undetected sexually abusive behaviors."
5. It **imposes open-ended, staff-defined prohibitions** without identifying the criminogenic need each addresses, in a field whose guidelines require clarity of rules and consequences and disclaim non-criminogenic targets.

**Where the client's framing needs correction:** the isolation argument. The empirical literature does **not** establish that social isolation causes sexual reoffense, and the leading meta-analysis found loneliness and social-skills deficits unrelated to sexual recidivism. What the literature does establish — clearly and in the profession's own operational instructions to its members — is that building relationships and social supports is an **affirmative objective** of this treatment. That is enough for the motion, and it is what the motion should say.

**One thing I could not find, and the Writer should know it.** I found no published professional source that criticizes treatment-contract terms of this kind *as such* — no article titled, in effect, "what is wrong with sex-offense treatment contracts." The argument in this memo is built by holding the contract up against affirmative practice standards and showing the mismatch, not by citing a source that has already made the criticism. That is a legitimate and, I think, more persuasive method — it lets the profession's own rules do the work — but it means the Writer should not tell the Court that "the clinical literature has criticized contracts like this one." It has not, so far as I could find. It has published standards that this one does not meet.

### 8.2 Specific additions, tied to existing subparts

**(1) Part II.A — after the "sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind" paragraph (currently ¶ 180).**
Add two or three sentences. The Judgment ordered sex-offense-specific treatment; the association that publishes the practice standards for that specialty instructs its members that treatment plans must be individualized and written with "clear and specific treatment goals and objectives" (2014 ed. 9.03 / 6th ed. 10.02), and that a client's "unconventional but unrelated" attitudes and interests "are not deemed appropriate primary treatment targets" (2014 ed. 11.10). **(2) Part II.B — after the *Boles* paragraph (currently ¶ 198), on SC ¶ 2.**
This is the highest-value insertion in the memo, and it answers the client's dispatch directly. Three moves:

- *Effect:* the current ATSA guidelines direct members to build intimate and prosocial relationships (6th ed. 14.24, 14.26, 14.30) and to recognize that "developing a support network may be challenging and gradual ... based on ... past sexually abusive behaviors, criminal histories, periods of institutionalization" (14.31). A term making the formation of any sexual relationship contingent on a documented, staff-graded disclosure works against the treatment the Judgment ordered.
**Keep the isolation claim in the register of § 3.2 above. Do not claim isolation causes reoffense.**

**(3) Part II.E — the Treatment-Team subpart (currently ¶¶ 226–231).**
This is where the clinical material is strongest and where the motion already has the right instinct — the "parent who brings a child to therapy" analogy at ¶ 232 is doing exactly the right work. **Replace or reinforce it with the profession's own rule**, which is better than an analogy because it is a rule:

**And the payoff for relief item (4):** ATSA's own enumeration of what a provider should report to a supervising officer is attendance, participation, changes in dynamic and protective factors, progress toward goals, engagement and compliance, referrals, and supervision adjustments (2014 ed. 18.04; 6th ed. 19.01). **It does not include session notes, raw testing data, or internal case records.** Part II's relief item (4) asks for precisely that line. Saying that the profession draws the same line is worth two sentences.

**(4) Part IV (Fifth Amendment) — one paragraph, not in Part II.**
The single best clinical sentence for Part IV: ATSA's own guidelines instruct that "**[c]lient disclosures of potentially incriminating information to mandated reporters can[] lead to future prosecution**," and that members must "**inform clients, in writing, of this potential dilemma and how it is addressed in their jurisdiction and program**" (2014 ed., App. B). The form reserved a place for this patient's individualized conditions and the provider wrote nothing in it.

Nothing more.
2. **Do not cite Hanson & Morton-Bourgon (2004), Hanson et al. (2007), or the SMART/SOMAPI chapter without reading them.** I read landing-page and web summaries only. 6. **Do not say the clinical literature has criticized treatment contracts like this one.** So far as I could find, it has not. The argument is a mismatch against affirmative standards, and it should be stated that way.
7. **Do not lean on the collateral-consequences/registry literature.** Craun & Bierie (2014), 78 Fed. Probation 28 — a **U.S. Courts** publication a federal judge may well know — is a methodological critique arguing that studies in that line may overstate registry-specific harm for want of comparison groups. That whole literature is contested terrain and the motion does not need it.
8. **Do not argue that provider reporting to Probation is unethical.** ATSA 2014 ed. 18.05 affirmatively requires members to report supervision violations, and the ATSA Code of Ethics (2001) § 9(d) disclaims any release from mandated reporting. The clinical objection is to *membership in the clinical body*, *unnamed recipients*, and *coerced confession* — not to reporting.
