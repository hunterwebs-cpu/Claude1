## 1. BOTTOM LINE

The memo is accurate in most respects. Every case it relies on exists and is cited to the right court and year. I found **no fabricated case and no fabricated quotation**. Every quotation I could test appears in the source. All but one appear at the page the memo gives.

The memo does need corrections before the Writer relies on it. In order of importance:

1. **The memo missed six published Second Circuit decisions on its own issues, five of them from 2020 to 2026.** The most important is ***United States v. Stuart*, No. 25-599 (2d Cir. Sept. 3, 2026)**, a published opinion on third-party risk notification. The memo says it searched for 2023–2026 authority on risk notification, but it does not mention *Stuart*. *Stuart* helps Jeff on the merits and on ripeness, but the government will cite it. The memo also missed *Rasheed*, *Birkedahl*, and *Bryant* (all 2020, all following *Traficante*), *Salvador* (2026, adverse on the "follow the rules" and Standard Condition 13 points), and *Harris* (2026, confirming *Boles*). See Part 4.
2. **A factual error about where Jeff's conditions came from (memo Part 3.1, Observation 1).** The memo says Jeff's CIMP condition "tracks" the Browder remand condition "except that Jeff's version lacks the words 'and based on reasonable suspicion.'" That is wrong. There are at least four other differences, including that Browder's surveillance clause reads "owned or **used**" where Jeff's reads "owned or **operated**." Jeff's CIMP condition matches the condition quoted in *Kunz* word for word, not Browder's. The memo's further claim that the Browder remand order "is the source of these conditions" has no support in the record. See Part 3, item F-1.
3. **One wrong pincite.** *Kunz*'s "contradict the express language … exceed the authority actually delegated" passage is at **68 F.4th at 766**, not 767–68. The one-device holding is correctly cited at 768.
4. **Several holdings are stated more broadly than the cases support, or adverse context is left out.** The main ones:
   - *Bee* (9th Cir.) upheld a court-imposed ban on sexually stimulating material "as deemed inappropriate by [his] probation officer and/or treatment staff." That undercuts the memo's distinction based on the phrase "as defined by my treatment staff."
   - *Eaglin* itself says expert testimony from a mental-health professional can justify an adult-pornography ban.
   - *Farrell* ruled against the parolee. The language the memo quotes is dicta.
   - *Simmons* upheld the pornography ban before it.
   - *Reeves* turned on facts that are more favorable than Jeff's.
   - The six-month retention period was approved only in the Browder remand order, not by the Second Circuit.
   - *Kunz* called *Browder* "instructive" and said it was "not bound" by it. It did not treat *Browder* as controlling.
   - *Carlineo* expressly contrasts the restorative-justice program before it with Probation-approved treatment programs.
   - *Parisi*'s no-changed-circumstances rule came from a modification that **enlarged** the defendant's conditions. It cuts both ways.
   See Part 3.
5. **One client fact is treated as established in Part 5.4.** The memo's Part 3.2 correctly says the PO's statement that she "noticed" the game chat through monitoring is unconfirmed. Part 5.4 then argues from it as if proven. Jeff's 9/19 email says only that he is "assuming via keylogger etc." See item F-6.
6. **Many NOT VERIFIED items can now be closed.** I read *Villafane-Lozada*, *Birkedahl*, *Arciniega*, *Asherman*, *Phipps*, *Bee*, *Packingham* (existence and cite), *Jaffee*, and *United States v. Johnson*, 529 U.S. 53, in primary form. I also found a district-court order on the counsel question. See Part 5.

- **High** for quotations, pincites, and document facts. Each was tested against primary text.
- **Moderate** for "still good law." There was no KeyCite or Shepard's run. Later treatment was checked through CourtListener searches and the later opinions themselves (Part 6).

## 4. AUTHORITY THE MEMO MISSED

These are all published Second Circuit decisions unless noted. Each was read in full from the slip opinion unless noted.

1. ***United States v. Stuart*, No. 25-599 (2d Cir. Sept. 3, 2026) (Jacobs, J.).** Probation's argument would be that once a court has made the risk finding, Probation can choose whom to notify. *Stuart* supports that, but only after a court finding. Jeff has none. *Stuart* affirmed a condition, imposed at a violation sentencing, authorizing Probation "to notify third parties, to whom [Stuart] may present a risk including employers and potential employers, of [her] criminal history and past criminal conduct." The holding rested on the fact that **the district court itself** had "sufficiently defined the risks posed by Stuart and found it necessary to notify third parties of them," leaving Probation only "to identify instances where that risk materializes on the ground."
   - *How it helps Jeff:* (a) It reaffirms *Peterson*, *MacMillen*, and *Boles*: the court, not Probation, decides whether notification is warranted, and the question is whether Probation "is equipped with a standard of decision that constrains its discretion" (quoting *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 766). No court has made any such determination for Jeff. (b) On ripeness, it distinguishes *Traficante* where the delegation "has already occurred and is not contingent on future judicial action" (quoting *Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d at 151).
   - *How it hurts:* it shows the Second Circuit will uphold broad, category-based notification once a court has made findings. The government's response to the motion may be to ask for exactly that. The memo already flags this risk at 5.1(c).
   - *Stuart* also notes, but does not decide, the occupational-restriction question, citing *United States v. Brown*, 173 F.4th 45, 51 (2d Cir. 2026). Not read; known only through *Stuart*.
2. ***United States v. Rasheed*, 981 F.3d 187 (2d Cir. Nov. 25, 2020) (W.D.N.Y. appeal).** Affirmed the judgment "as modified by" the W.D.N.Y. standing order. It held a challenge to the amended risk condition "foreclosed" as unripe because "any allegedly improper delegation is conditioned on the district court finding, during [Rasheed's] term of supervised release, that he poses a risk." It collects *Bryant*, 976 F.3d 165, 182 (2d Cir. 2020); *Birkedahl*, 973 F.3d at 57; and five 2020 summary orders. **Use:** *Traficante*'s reading of the standing order is settled law in at least four published decisions, not a single panel's view.
3. ***United States v. Birkedahl*, 973 F.3d 49 (2d Cir. 2020) (W.D.N.Y. appeal).** Same holding on the revised risk condition (slip op. 17). It also dismissed a challenge to computerized voice stress analysis as unripe. Close NOT VERIFIED item 3 as to *Birkedahl*.
4. ***United States v. Bryant*, 976 F.3d 165, 182 (2d Cir. 2020).** Known through *Rasheed*. Not read.
5. ***United States v. Salvador*, 180 F.4th 470 (2d Cir. July 7, 2026) (Nathan, J.)** (reporter cite as given in *Stuart*). **ADVERSE** to the argument that a "follow the rules and regulations of that program" clause is vague or an impermissible delegation. On plain-error review, it upheld a condition to "cooperate with and abide by all instructions of immigration authorities," analogizing to Standard Condition 13. **Distinguish it:** it was plain-error review; immigration authorities are government officials; and *Salvador* stressed that the condition did not hand any "particular sentencing decision" to the outside body. The treatment contract, by contrast, hands the provider decisions about sexual relationships, lawful material, and platform use. *Salvador* also reaffirms that vagueness and delegation challenges to the "words on the page" are ripe, discussing *Reeves*.

8. ***United States v. Young*, 910 F.3d 665, 669, 671 & n.26 (2d Cir. 2018).** Known through *Villafane-Lozada* and *Stuart*. Not read. It read a treatment authorization to exclude inpatient treatment in light of *Matta*, and it quoted the Rule 32.1 advisory note on recourse to the sentencing court. Worth reading. It supports narrowly construing treatment-program delegations.
