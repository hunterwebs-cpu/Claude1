### 1.3 Roadmap of conclusions

Sixteen terms of this contract are objectionable. Four are seriously vulnerable and would, standing alone, justify a new Part of the motion:

- **SC ¶ 8** (a ban on "sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind as defined by my treatment staff") imposes a liberty restriction the Judgment does not contain anywhere, defined by a private party's own unbounded say-so.
- **SC ¶ 5** (pre-approval by treatment staff before using "any platforms," expressly including **email**) hands a private PLLC a gatekeeping power over Mr. Bernecky's internet use that this Court never gave even to the Probation Office.
- **SC ¶ 2** (compelled disclosure of all intimate partners, plus "acceptable proof of informed consent ... which includes disclosure of my offense history in full prior to engaging in a sexual relationship") is a third-party risk-notification requirement imposed by a private provider with no judicial risk finding of any kind — the *Peterson*/*Boles* problem in private-contract form.
- **Consequences ¶ 2(vi)** (immediate discharge for "other problem behavior not outlined in this Treatment Contract, as determined in the sole discretion of the staff of the Practice") makes Mr. Bernecky's continued compliance with Special Conditions (b) and (h) — and therefore his liberty — turn on a private party's unwritten, unreviewable judgment.

A fifth finding is structural and, in my view, the most important thing in this memo: **Consequences ¶ 3(a) states that "The Treatment Team is comprised of all therapists in the Practice, the Probation Officer, and the Probation Supervisor."** By the contract's own terms, the body that decides whether Mr. Bernecky is discharged includes his supervising probation officer and that officer's supervisor. That collapses the distinction the government will otherwise want to draw between "a private provider's rules, which the court cannot control" and "Probation's rules, which the court plainly can." Every *Kunz*/*Browder* limit on what Probation may add to a sentence applies to this document directly, because Probation sits on the body that writes and enforces it.

A sixth finding cuts **in Mr. Bernecky's favor on an existing Part** of the motion: the contract's own alcohol term (Code of Conduct ¶ 3) asks only that he "refrain from excessive use of alcohol," while Special Condition (h) commands total abstinence while in treatment. The treatment provider — the very entity whose treatment triggers the abstinence clause — does not itself require abstinence. That is documentary corroboration for MOTION-v5 Part V's assertion that nobody is administering the clause as written.

**One genuine adverse development the Writer must know about:** *United States v. Jimenez*, decided by the Second Circuit in February 2026 and amended in May 2026, upheld a mental health treatment condition against both vagueness and delegation challenges, expressly distinguishing *Carlineo* and noting that *Carlineo* itself "expressly distinguished" the condition it vacated from "special conditions such as substance abuse and mental health treatment." *Jimenez* also supplies the analytical frame that makes this motion winnable anyway — the **"additive, not subtractive"** test. See § 5.2 and § 6.

## 2. ISSUES PRESENTED AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1.** Where a Judgment orders a defendant to "participate in a sex offense-specific treatment program and **follow the rules and regulations of that program**," does that clause authorize the provider to impose restrictions on the defendant's liberty that appear nowhere in the Judgment?

> **No.** *Browder* holds it does not, on a materially identical clause. Browder's own sentencing condition required him "to comply with the mandates of the treatment program," 866 F.3d at 507 n.4, and the Second Circuit still held that the provider's agreement term exceeding the sentence "would contradict, rather than execute, Browder's sentence," so that "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose it," *id.* at 514. A "follow the rules" clause incorporates a provider's rules only insofar as those rules execute, rather than enlarge, the sentence.

**Issue 2.** Does the contract's minor-contact term (SC ¶ 9) exceed Special Condition (c)?

> **Yes, in three independent respects** — though *not* in the specific respect *Browder* turned on, because Mr. Bernecky has no biological or adopted children. (a) The Judgment forbids "**deliberate** contact"; the contract requires 24-hour reporting of "any **accidental, incidental, or intentional** contact." (b) The Judgment vests approval in "the probation officer or ... the Court"; the contract vests "review[] for appropriateness" in "treatment staff" — the precise defect *Browder* identified at 866 F.3d at 514 ("the approval not only of the Probation Office, but also of 'the treatment team' — something the District Court did not address and over which it had no direct oversight"). (c) The contract adds three categories of persons the Judgment never mentions: "potential or former victims, codefendants, vulnerable populations (as defined by treatment staff)."

**Issue 3.** Does the contract's ban on "sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind as defined by my treatment staff" (SC ¶ 8) exceed the Judgment?

> **Yes, completely.** The Judgment contains **no** restriction on lawful adult sexual material. Its only material-possession condition bars child pornography. Special Condition (j) ("The defendant shall not possess or have in his control any child pornography."). The contract creates an entirely new prohibition whose scope is set by a private party's own definition. It fails *Carlineo*'s vagueness test ("too vague if it requires a reasonable person to speculate as to its meaning," 998 F.3d at 536) and *Carlineo*'s delegation rule ("the Probation Office may supervise and execute a sentence but may not fashion a sentence's terms," *id.* at 538) *a fortiori*, since the delegate here is not even the Probation Office.

**Issue 4.** Does the contract's platform pre-approval term (SC ¶ 5) exceed Special Condition (a)?

> **Yes.** Special Condition (a) requires *advance notification* of devices and authorizes *monitoring* by the U.S. Probation Office. It creates no approval gate for internet services, and it names no private party as gatekeeper. SC ¶ 5 requires prior approval by treatment staff before using "any platforms including but not limited to (social media, dating sites, **email**, blog/discussion forums, gaming services, text apps, photo sharing sites, chat rooms, etc.)." This is the *Kunz* "exceeds the authority actually delegated" problem transplanted into a private contract, plus a First Amendment overlay (*Packingham*) that a monitoring condition would not carry.

**Issue 5.** Do the contract's compelled-disclosure terms conflict with the Fifth Amendment analysis already established in this matter for Special Condition (g)?

> **Yes, and more sharply than the polygraph condition does.** SC ¶ 7 ("necessary [to] discuss my offense conduct, fantasies, sexual behaviors, sexual thoughts, masturbation patterns, sexual history"), Requirements ¶ 1 ("full acknowledgement of my actions"), Code of Conduct ¶ 5 (24-hour "relapse" reporting), and Code of Conduct ¶ 6 (reporting "criminal behaviors [outside of or in addition to Instant Offense or pending charges]") together compel disclosure of uncharged criminal conduct — to a party that Confidentiality ¶ 2 obliges to report to Probation and that Consequences ¶ 1 permits to contact "law enforcement" — with no immunity and on pain of discharge, which under Special Condition (b) is a supervision violation. That is the exact structure the Tenth Circuit held unconstitutional in *Von Behren*, and it is the structure the Ninth Circuit described in the sentence *Von Behren* quotes: "When the government conditions continued supervised release on compliance with a treatment program requiring full disclosure of past sexual misconduct, with no provision of immunity for disclosed conduct, it unconstitutionally compels self-incrimination." *United States v. Bahr*, 730 F.3d 963, 966 (9th Cir. 2013), *quoted in Von Behren*, 822 F.3d at 1151.

**Issue 6.** Does the contract's financial-reporting term (Code of Conduct ¶ 6) exceed Special Conditions (d) and (i)?

> **Yes.** Both financial conditions run to "the U.S. Probation Office" and nobody else, and Special Condition (d) states its own purpose — "[i]n order to monitor the defendant's compliance with not buying or subscribing to online services that provide child pornography." The contract requires Mr. Bernecky to report "significant changes in income/debt" to a **private counseling practice**, untethered to any purpose the Judgment states.

**Issue 7.** Does the contract's other-client contact term (Confidentiality ¶ 3) exceed Standard Condition 8?

> **Yes, as to the decisionmaker.** Standard Condition 8 conditions contact with known felons on "the permission of the probation officer." Confidentiality ¶ 3 requires "prior approval of treatment staff" for contact with other clients. It also creates an internal contradiction with Requirements ¶ 4, which requires him to "actively encourage the significant people in my life to participate in treatment."

**Issue 8.** Does the contract's frequency-uncapped assessment term (SC ¶ 6) exceed Special Condition (g)?

> **Probably yes, on frequency; arguably yes, on instrument.** Special Condition (g) caps testing at "not to exceed twice in a calendar year, and an additional two re-tests per year, as needed." SC ¶ 6 states, without any cap, that "ongoing assessment of my progress through psychological and physiological evaluation (including but not limited to computer based sexual interest assessments and polygraph) will be part of treatment." Whether "computer based sexual interest assessments" fall within the Judgment's phrase "polygraph, computerized voice stress analyzer or other such testing" is genuinely arguable; the surrounding language ("or other similar device") suggests the Judgment had deception-detection instruments in mind, not visual-reaction-time sexual-interest instruments. **[VERIFY — I did not research the case law on visual reaction time / Abel-type assessments as supervised-release conditions; that is an open research task.]**

**Issue 9.** Is the contract's alcohol term consistent with Special Condition (h)?

> **No — and the inconsistency helps Mr. Bernecky.** Code of Conduct ¶ 3 asks only that he "refrain from excessive use of alcohol." Special Condition (h) commands that "[w]hile in treatment or taking psychotropic medication, the defendant shall abstain from the use of alcohol." Two documents Mr. Bernecky is obliged to obey say different things. That is a fair-notice defect on its face, and it is affirmative evidence for Part V.

**Issue 10.** Does the discharge structure (Consequences ¶¶ 1-3) make Mr. Bernecky's liberty contingent on a private party's discretion?

> **Yes.** Consequences ¶ 2(vi) authorizes IMMEDIATE DISCHARGE for "other problem behavior not outlined in this Treatment Contract, as determined in the sole discretion of the staff of the Practice." Discharge from treatment is, under Special Condition (b) and (h), a failure to complete court-ordered treatment. *Matta*'s rule — "may not delegate ... decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion," 777 F.3d at 122, *quoted in Browder*, 866 F.3d at 510 n.20 — applies with greater, not lesser, force to a delegate that is not a federal officer at all.

### 3.2 A finding that changes how *Browder* should be argued

**Mr. Bernecky's Judgment is a near-verbatim copy of the condition set Judge Vilardo wrote on remand in *Browder*, three months earlier.**

On March 14, 2018, on remand from the Second Circuit, Judge Vilardo re-stated Browder's conditions "as revised to reflect guidance from the Second Circuit since those conditions were first imposed in 2011 by Judge Arcara." Judge Larimer entered Mr. Bernecky's Judgment on June 12, 2018. Compare:

**Browder remand (Mar. 14, 2018):** "The defendant must participate in a sex offense-specific treatment program and follow the rules and regulations of that program. The probation officer will supervise the details of the defendant's participation in the program, including the selection of a provider and schedule. If inpatient treatment is recommended, however, court approval must be obtained unless the defendant consents. The defendant is not to leave treatment until complete or as ordered by the Court. The defendant is required to contribute to the cost of services rendered."

**Bernecky Judgment (June 12, 2018), Special Condition (b):** identical, less the inpatient sentence.

The same near-identity holds for the CIMP condition, the minor-contact condition (word for word, including the "excluding his biological or adopted children" carve-out and the "pick up his children from school" sentence), the financial condition (word for word), the SORA/PSR condition, the search condition, and the mental health/alcohol condition (word for word).

**Two consequences follow, and both are significant for the Writer.**

*First*, *Browder* is not merely persuasive authority about somebody else's treatment agreement. It is the **direct textual ancestor** of the very conditions Mr. Bernecky is serving. The court that composed this language did so in an opinion whose central holding was: "**As the Second Circuit's decision on the treatment condition teaches, Browder cannot be forced to sign or abide by agreements that reach beyond the Court's ordered conditions.**" *Browder*, No. 10-CR-263, slip op. (W.D.N.Y. Mar. 14, 2018) (Vilardo, J.). Whatever a W.D.N.Y. judge meant by "follow the rules and regulations of that program" in this template, he meant it against the express backdrop of a holding that provider agreements may not reach beyond the court's conditions.

*Second* — and the Writer should decide whether to use this, because it is outside the treatment-contract issue and belongs to Part I — **the Browder remand condition contains a clause Bernecky's Judgment omits.** Judge Vilardo wrote: "As triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity, **and based on reasonable suspicion**, the defendant shall consent to and cooperate with unannounced examinations..." Mr. Bernecky's Special Condition (a) reads: "As triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity, the defendant shall consent to and cooperate with unannounced examinations..." The reasonable-suspicion clause — which Judge Vilardo added *specifically* to answer Browder's objection, saying "to remove any doubt, this Court will graft those words on to the monitoring provision" — is absent from Mr. Bernecky's Judgment. That is a live, unexploited argument for Part I.B/I.C.

### 3.3 What *Browder* actually holds

*Browder* is a mixed decision. The computer-monitoring half went **against** the defendant; the treatment half went **for** him. Quote the second half; do not overstate the first.

**The holding for us**, verbatim, 866 F.3d at 513-14:

> "The gravamen of Browder's complaint is simply that the proposed treatment agreement conflicted with his actual sentence."

> "We agree with Browder that the treatment agreement would have subjected him to a more punitive condition governing contact with minors than did the condition to which Browder was actually sentenced by the District Court. Browder was therefore well within his rights to object to that term of the agreement."

> "First, the treatment agreement actually required the approval not only of the Probation Office, but also of 'the treatment team' — something the District Court did not address and over which it had no direct oversight."

> "Second, and more fundamentally, the District Court misapprehended the relationship between a defendant's sentence (by a judge) and its execution (by a probation officer). **The key question is not whether permission, if sought, would be granted; it is whether Browder can be required, by the Probation Office or its designee, to ask in the first place.** Because such a requirement as to Browder's children would contradict, rather than execute, Browder's sentence, the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose it, as Browder correctly argued."

**Three additional features of *Browder* that MOTION-v5 does not currently use and should:**

1. **Browder's own sentence contained a "comply with the mandates of the treatment program" clause — and it did not save the provider term.** 866 F.3d at 507 n.4: "The defendant is to enroll, attend, and participate in mental health intervention specifically designed for the treatment of sexual offenders as approved by the U.S. Probation Office. **The defendant is to comply with the mandates of the treatment program** and is not to leave such treatment until discharge is agreed to by the U.S. Probation Office and treating agency." The panel's footnote 16 expressly noted that "Contrary to the requirements of his special conditions, Browder ... refused to 'comply with the mandates of the treatment program.'" And it *still* reversed the violation finding. **This is the direct answer to the government's best argument**, which will be that Special Condition (b) says "follow the rules and regulations of that program" and so the provider's rules are the Court's rules. They were not in *Browder*, and they are not here.
2. **The Second Circuit treated the provider agreement's terms as functionally conditions of supervision.** 866 F.3d at 514 n.38: "Notably, the treatment agreement even refers to its terms as 'conditions.'" The R.K.K.S. contract does the same thing: its final paragraph reads, "I ... affirmatively acknowledge that I have read and understand that I am required to follow all the **conditions** listed above regarding my treatment and behavior," and its penultimate section is titled "**SPECIAL CONDITIONS**" — using the Judgment's own vocabulary.
3. **The remedy on remand is a template.** Judge Vilardo ordered: "Browder does not need permission to have contact with his own children, and he need not sign any treatment agreement requiring him to obtain such permission. Therefore, while the condition that Browder participate in mental health treatment remains, **such treatment may not be contingent on his signing any agreement that forces him to obtain permission** to have contact with his own children." And separately: "he may **amend and initial** the agreement language noted above consistent with this decision." That is a cheap, concrete, already-used-in-this-District remedy the Writer should request by name.

**What in *Browder* cuts against us:**

- **The children carve-out was the hinge, and Mr. Bernecky has no children.** Browder had two daughters, and the sentence's express exclusion of them created a head-on textual conflict with the provider agreement. Per client-directives.md, the Judgment's "excluding his biological or adopted children" carve-out is a **null set** as to Mr. Bernecky. A Writer who frames the SC ¶ 9 argument as "just like Browder's children" will be met with an easy distinction. The argument must be built on the *other three* divergences identified in Issue 2, which do not depend on the carve-out at all.
- **Browder made a documented good-faith effort.** He attended at least two sessions, objected in writing, and had the objection recorded in the facility's records. 866 F.3d at 513. The panel relied on all of it. Whatever Mr. Bernecky does about this contract, it must follow that pattern: attend, object in writing, keep proof.
- **Browder was decided on abuse-of-discretion review of a violation finding, not on a § 3583(e)(2) modification motion.** The posture is different. That difference favors us on the merits (this Court's own § 3583(e)(2) authority is broader than appellate review of a violation), but the Writer should not describe *Browder* as having "vacated a condition." It reversed a violation finding.

Sections, in order: REQUIREMENTS (¶¶ 1-4), CONFIDENTIALITY (¶¶ 1-4), ACCESS TO CARE (¶ 1), CODE OF CONDUCT (¶¶ 1-6), CONSEQUENCES AND DISCHARGE CRITERIA (¶¶ 1-3, with sub-items), and SPECIAL CONDITIONS (ten unnumbered paragraphs, cited here as SC ¶¶ 1-10 in document order). ### 4.2 It is a blanket form, not an individualized instrument — and it says so twice

- **SC ¶ 1 does not apply to Mr. Bernecky at all:** "I understand that while under pretrial supervision or pretrial diversion I am not permitted to talk about the offense for which I am currently being investigated." Mr. Bernecky is not on pretrial supervision or diversion. He is eight years past a guilty plea and on post-conviction supervised release. The paragraph is boilerplate for a different population.
- **SC ¶ 10 — the individualization slot — is empty:** "I agree to abide by the following special conditions: **No special conditions designated**."

This is the same objection MOTION-v5 ¶ 84 makes about the CIMP agreement ("an agreement built for blanket use cannot enlarge conditions that were supposed to be individualized"), and here the document proves the point on its own face: the provider was given a place to write down what this particular defendant needs, and wrote nothing.

### 4.3 Probation is a member of the body that enforces it

Consequences ¶ 3(a): "**The Treatment Team is comprised of all therapists in the Practice, the Probation Officer, and the Probation Supervisor.**"

### 4.4 Unknown and unfavorable facts

- **The two incorporated authorizations have not been read.** (§ 1.2.)
- **Whether R.K.K.S. is the (b) provider, the (h) provider, or both, is not established.** The name and Confidentiality ¶ 1 ("required to obtain Mental Health Counseling") point toward (h); the SPECIAL CONDITIONS section is unmistakably sex-offense-specific and points toward (b). This matters — see § 3.1, correction 1.
- **Nothing in the record shows Mr. Bernecky has been asked to do anything under the objectionable terms yet.** No polygraph has been scheduled under SC ¶ 6, no platform approval has been sought or refused under SC ¶ 5, no partner disclosure has been demanded under SC ¶ 2. That is a ripeness exposure. The counter is *Balon*, already in the motion, plus the Rule 32.1 advisory committee note already in the motion, plus the concrete fact in MOTION-v5 ¶ 210 that his treatment statements **are already being used against him** in the Monroe County SORA proceeding.
- **R.K.K.S. is presumably an approved provider.** *Carlineo* rested partly on the fact that the Partners program "has not been evaluated by the Probation Office, and there are no apparent licensing, certification, or objective standards that govern and define the Program." 998 F.3d at 537. That prong does not transfer. ## 5. DISCUSSION

### 5.1 The threshold question: are these contract terms "conditions of supervised release" at all?

**Rule.** A treatment provider's agreement terms become functionally enforceable as conditions where compliance with the agreement is a prerequisite to completing the court-ordered treatment. In *Von Behren*, the district court "held that because successful completion of sex offender treatment was a new condition of Mr. Von Behren's supervised release, and because compliance with the terms of the RSA agreement was required for participation in and successful completion of the RSA program, the requirements of the RSA agreement were, in effect, conditions of Mr. Von Behren's supervised release." 822 F.3d at 1142 (the Tenth Circuit's description of the district court's first order; the panel did not disturb that framing and proceeded on it). *Browder* reached the same place by a different route: the panel treated the provider's terms as things the Probation Office was "without power to impose," 866 F.3d at 514, which presupposes that imposing them would have been an exercise of supervisory power.

**Application.** Special Condition (b) requires Mr. Bernecky to "participate in a sex offense-specific treatment program and follow the rules and regulations of that program," and adds that he "is not to leave treatment until complete or as ordered by the Court." Special Condition (h) likewise: "not to leave such treatment until completion." Discharge under Consequences ¶ 2 is therefore not merely a private contractual consequence — it is a failure to complete court-ordered treatment, reportable by a Treatment Team that includes his own probation officer. Every term of this contract is backed, at one remove, by the threat of a violation report.

**Conclusion.** The contract's terms operate as conditions of supervision. That is what makes the *Browder*/*Kunz*/*Matta* line apply to a document signed with a private PLLC.

### 5.2 The delegation framework, and the *Jimenez* problem

**Rule.** "The power to impose special conditions of supervised release ... is vested exclusively in the district court." *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122, *quoted in Browder*, 866 F.3d at 510 n.20. A court "may delegate to a probation officer decisionmaking authority over certain minor details of supervised release — for example, the selection of a therapy provider or treatment schedule," but "may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *Id.* The Second Circuit restated the principle in *Carlineo*: "the district court, not the Probation Office, retains exclusive authority to set conditions of supervised release," 998 F.3d at 537, and "the Probation Office may supervise and execute a sentence but may not fashion a sentence's terms," *id.* at 538.

**The adverse authority the Writer must confront.** In *United States v. Jimenez*, No. 24-1661 (2d Cir. Feb. 25, 2026, amended May 12, 2026), the Second Circuit rejected both a vagueness and a delegation challenge to an outpatient mental health counseling condition. On vagueness, the panel held that a condition "merely requir[ing] participation in a counseling program, including regular therapy sessions, **the details of which will be determined by service providers and probation officers**," poses "no notice issue," and observed that in *Carlineo* "we expressly distinguished the condition at issue ... from 'special conditions such as substance abuse and mental health treatment.'" Slip op. at 33. It further held that a requirement of cognitive behavioral therapy "to the extent practicable and as determined to be appropriate by the therapy provider" was not vague. *Id.* at 33 & n.7.

**Why *Jimenez* does not sink this argument — and in fact supplies its frame.** *Jimenez* draws the line in two places, and Mr. Bernecky's contract falls on the wrong side of both:

1. **The additive/subtractive test.** "The discretion afforded to the probation office is **additive, not subtractive**, to Jimenez's liberty, and therefore, does not constitute an improper delegation." Slip op. at 27. The delegation upheld in *Jimenez* was the power to *excuse* the defendant from an obligation. Every objectionable term in the R.K.K.S. contract is subtractive: SC ¶ 8 takes away lawful adult material; SC ¶ 5 takes away email and every other platform absent permission; SC ¶ 2 takes away private sexual association absent disclosure and proof; SC ¶ 9 takes away accidental contact without a report; Consequences ¶ 2(vi) takes away treatment status itself.
2. **The "additional burdens" line.** The *Jimenez* condition "does not entitle probation officers to impose additional burdens on Jimenez, such as giving them the authority to decide whether Jimenez participates in treatment at all. **Contra** *United States v. Harris*, 164 F.4th 181, 197 (2d Cir. 2026)." Slip op. at 34. Imposing additional burdens is precisely what this contract does. And the *contra* citation to *Harris* signals that the Second Circuit has, in 2026, found an impermissible delegation on the other side of that line. **[VERIFY — I obtained the *Harris* citation from the *Jimenez* text and have not read *Harris*. It is a 2026 F.4th decision on treatment-condition delegation that MOTION-v5 does not cite. Reading it is a priority research task.]**

**Conclusion on the framework.** *Jimenez* forecloses a challenge to Special Conditions (b) and (h) *as written*. It does not touch a challenge to what a private provider has written *underneath* them. The motion should not attack the treatment conditions; it should attack the contract, and should cite *Jimenez* itself for the line it crosses.

#### TC-1. SC ¶ 8 — the ban on "sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind as defined by my treatment staff"

**The language.** "I will not purchase, possess, view, produce, or use sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind **as defined by my treatment staff**. I understand that I am required to notify treatment staff within 24 hours of any incidents." Treatment Contract, SPECIAL CONDITIONS ¶ 8 (p. 3).

**The problem: exceeds Judgment authorization + vagueness + unfettered-discretion delegation (all three).**

**Compare the Judgment.** The Judgment contains no restriction on lawful sexual material of any kind. Its sole material-possession condition is Special Condition (j): "The defendant shall not possess or have in his control any child pornography." A Judgment that enumerated ten special conditions, and that specified *child* pornography when it addressed prohibited material, did not silently also prohibit lawful adult material. This is the same *expressio unius* point MOTION-v5 ¶ 66 makes about employment, and it is stronger here, because the Judgment did not merely omit the subject — it addressed the subject and drew a narrower line.

**Compare *Browder*.** This is *Browder*'s core holding applied to a different clause. The Mid-Erie term in *Browder* was more onerous than the sentence because it dropped a carve-out; SC ¶ 8 is more onerous because it adds a prohibition that has no counterpart in the sentence at all. "Because such a requirement ... would contradict, rather than execute, Browder's sentence, the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose it." 866 F.3d at 514. And *Browder*'s "key question" formulation transfers exactly: the question is not whether treatment staff would in fact define some particular magazine as prohibited; it is whether Mr. Bernecky can be required, by a provider, to submit the question at all.

**Vagueness.** "A condition is too vague if it requires a reasonable person to speculate as to its meaning." *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 536 (citing *United States v. Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77, 80-81 (2d Cir. 2010)). "[A] probationer has a due process right to conditions of supervised release that are sufficiently clear to inform him of what conduct will result in his being returned to prison." *United States v. Simmons*, 343 F.3d 72, 81 (2d Cir. 2003), *quoted in Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 537. "Sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind" is worse than vague — it is defined by reference to a definition that has never been written down and can change without notice. A condition whose content is "whatever the provider says it is" does not put anyone on notice; it makes notice structurally impossible.

**Delegation.** *Carlineo*: the Probation Office "may not fashion a sentence's terms." 998 F.3d at 538. Here the fashioning is done by a private PLLC. *Jimenez*: this is subtractive discretion, and it imposes an "additional burden," slip op. at 27, 34.

**Contrast with *Jimenez*.** *Jimenez* tolerated "as determined to be appropriate by the therapy provider" — but the deferred question there was the *therapeutic modality* (whether CBT was practicable), not the scope of a liberty-restricting prohibition. Deferring clinical method to a clinician is what *Matta* calls a "minor detail." Deferring the definition of contraband is not.

#### TC-2. SC ¶ 5 — treatment-staff pre-approval before using "any platforms," including email

**The language.** "I understand that the use of any platforms including but not limited to (social media, dating sites, email, blog/discussion forums, gaming services, text apps, photo sharing sites, chat rooms, etc.) **should be approved by treatment staff prior to use**." SC ¶ 5 (p. 3).

**Compare the Judgment.** Special Condition (a) does two things and only two things about Mr. Bernecky's internet life: it requires "advance notification of any computer(s), automated service(s), or connected device(s) that will be used," and it authorizes the **U.S. Probation Office** to "install any application as necessary to surveil all activity" and to conduct triggered examinations. **Notification and monitoring. SC ¶ 5 does the same conversion and then hands the permission power to somebody outside the federal government entirely.

Note also that the Judgment's own gatekeeping clause names the permissible gatekeepers: a device may be used "unless authorized by **the Court or the U.S. Probation Office**." Special Condition (a). The Judgment identified who may authorize. A treatment provider is not on the list.

**Compare *Browder*.** Directly on point through *Browder*'s footnote about the Mid-Erie agreement: the agreement "actually required the approval not only of the Probation Office, but also of 'the treatment team' — something the District Court did not address and over which it had no direct oversight." 866 F.3d at 514. That sentence describes SC ¶ 5 precisely. And *Browder*'s "key question" formulation is again the heart of it: not whether approval would be given for Gmail, but whether he can be made to ask.

**"Email" makes this concrete rather than abstract.** A supervisee cannot function — cannot receive a court notice, a medical result, a pay stub, an employer's message — without email. MOTION-v5 ¶ 112 already develops this register ("A term demanding a telephone call before an ordinary transaction can be completed is not a monitoring measure. It is a permission slip for daily life"). SC ¶ 5 is that sentence's strongest illustration.

**Vagueness.** The operative verb is "**should** be approved," in a document whose breach is grounds for discharge under Consequences ¶ 2(vi) and ¶ 3(i). A rule that cannot tell its reader whether it is mandatory does not "inform him of what conduct will result in his being returned to prison." *Simmons*, 343 F.3d at 81, *quoted in Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 537. The trailing "etc." compounds it: the enumerated list is expressly non-exhaustive, so the prohibited set is unbounded.

**First Amendment overlay.** *Browder* itself noted, in footnote 26, that *Packingham v. North Carolina*, 137 S. Ct. 1730 (2017), struck a social-media ban for sex offenders on First Amendment grounds, and distinguished it because *Browder* "involved an internet ban — not internet or computer monitoring — and that ban extended beyond the completion of a sentence." **The first half of that distinction runs the other way here.** SC ¶ 5 is an access gate, not monitoring. The second half still cuts against us (Mr. Bernecky is under sentence), and *Browder* also noted the argument was waived there. So *Packingham* is a supporting note, not a load-bearing beam. The load-bearing in-circuit point remains *United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d 88, 96 (2d Cir. 2019), already in the motion via *Kunz* for the proposition that internet access is "nearly essential" to modern life. **[VERIFY — I did not read *Packingham* or *Eaglin* in primary source this session; both are cited here as they appear inside *Browder* and inside MOTION-v5 respectively.]**

#### TC-3. SC ¶ 2 — compelled disclosure of intimate partners and compelled offense-history disclosure to those partners

**The language.** "I understand that I am required to **disclose all my intimate partners** to treatment staff. I understand that I need to provide **acceptable proof of informed consent** to treatment staff which includes **disclosure of my offense history in full** prior to engaging in a sexual relationship." SC ¶ 2 (p. 3).

**The problem: exceeds Judgment authorization + third-party notification without any judicial risk finding + vagueness ("acceptable proof") + delegation.**

**Compare the Judgment.** Nothing in the Judgment addresses adult intimate relationships, requires disclosure of partners to anyone, or requires Mr. Bernecky to disclose his offense history to any private person. The Judgment's only third-party-notification provision is **Standard Condition 12**, and it requires that "the probation officer determines that you pose a risk to another person" before any notification obligation attaches. SC ¶ 2 imposes a categorical notification requirement as to an entire class of persons, triggered by nothing, determined by nobody.

**Compare *Browder*.** This is the *Browder* problem in its most aggravated form. In *Browder* the provider added an approval requirement to a subject the sentence had already addressed. Here the provider has legislated on a subject the sentence never touched, and has assigned itself the role of judging whether the "proof of informed consent" is "acceptable."

**The *Peterson*/*Boles* problem.** The Second Circuit vacated a third-party-notification condition in *United States v. Peterson*, 248 F.3d 79, 85-86 (2d Cir. 2001) (per curiam), because it left the notification obligation to a probation officer's unfettered discretion. *Browder* describes *Peterson* as "holding that two special conditions — delegating to the probation officer the decision whether to require sex offender counseling and the decision whether to require third-party notifications — were impermissible delegations of judicial authority." 866 F.3d at 510 n.20. *United States v. Boles*, 914 F.3d 95, 111-12 (2d Cir. 2019), applied the same reasoning to the standard risk-notification condition, and this District's March 22, 2019 standing order replaced that condition with one requiring **the court itself** to make the risk determination. SC ¶ 2 requires a notification the Second Circuit would not let a *probation officer* require on his own say-so, imposed by a private counseling practice on no say-so at all.

**Vagueness.** "Acceptable proof of informed consent" is undefined. Acceptable to whom, in what form, proving what? *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 537 ("does not put Carlineo on notice as to what conduct could trigger a charge of violating the condition").

**Liberty interest.** Adult private sexual association is a recognized liberty interest, *Lawrence v. Texas*, 539 U.S. 558 (2003), and the Second Circuit requires that a condition "affecting a cognizable liberty interest" be "supported by particularized findings that it does not constitute a greater deprivation of liberty than reasonably necessary." *Jimenez*, slip op. at 9-10 (quoting *United States v. Sims*, 92 F.4th 115, 125 (2d Cir. 2024), quoting *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122). No findings of any kind support SC ¶ 2, because no judge has ever seen it. **[VERIFY — *Lawrence* and *Sims* are cited here from memory and from *Jimenez*'s text respectively; neither read in primary source this session.]**

**Delegation.** *Matta*: no delegation of "decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." 777 F.3d at 122. *Carlineo*: no delegation of "decision-making authority which would make a defendant's liberty contingent on a probation officer's exercise of open-ended discretion." 998 F.3d at 538. *Jimenez*: a condition may not "entitle probation officers to impose additional burdens on [the defendant], such as giving them the authority to decide whether [he] participates in treatment **at all**." **The genuine counter-argument, and the answer.** MOTION-v5 ¶ 70 already quotes *United States v. McDonald*, No. 1:11-CR-01075-MV-1, slip op. at 12 n.2 (D.N.M. Jan. 6, 2026), for the proposition that a court "lacks authority to tell a private treatment provider how to enforce, or not enforce, their rules and regulations." That is correct and the motion should not pretend otherwise. **The relief must therefore be shaped to run against Probation and the Court's own order, never against R.K.K.S.** MOTION-v5's Part II relief is already correctly shaped this way — it asks that a termination following a timely written objection not itself be treated as a violation, and that Probation make other treatment arrangements. #### TC-6 through TC-9. The compelled-disclosure cluster (self-incrimination)

**The language, four terms operating as one machine:**

- **Requirements ¶ 1** (p. 1): "I agree to be honest and assume full responsibility for my problematic behavior. I understand that successful treatment depends upon **full acknowledgement of my actions and thinking patterns**."
- **SC ¶ 7** (p. 3): "To be successful in treatment I understand that it is necessary [to] discuss my **offense conduct, fantasies, sexual behaviors, sexual thoughts, masturbation patterns, sexual history**, and any other relevant topics."
- **Code of Conduct ¶ 5** (p. 2): "I agree to avoid situations and behaviors that will place me at high risk of engaging in problematic behavior. I understand that I am required to **notify treatment staff within 24 hours of any relapse in problematic behavior**."
- **Code of Conduct ¶ 6** (p. 2): "I will notify treatment staff of ... any other changes in relevant risk factors (including, but not limited to, ... **criminal behaviors [outside of or in addition to Instant Offense or pending charges]** ...)."

**And the two terms that convert disclosure into exposure:**

- **Confidentiality ¶ 2** (p. 1): "I understand that treatment staff will provide **verbal and written reports to United States Probation and other individuals and agencies involved in my treatment**."
- **Consequences ¶ 1** (p. 2): "I understand that my supervising officer will be notified of any violation of this Treatment Contract. I also understand that **law enforcement may be contacted** if it becomes necessary to maintain the safety of potential victims and/or the community."

**The problem: compelled self-incrimination without immunity, enforced by the penalty of discharge and consequent violation.**

**Rule.** "[I]f the state, either expressly or by implication, asserts that invocation of the privilege would lead to revocation of probation, it would have created the classic penalty situation." *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 435 (1984), *quoted in Von Behren*, 822 F.3d at 1149. "A witness is compelled under the Fifth Amendment **as soon as the government threatens him with a substantial penalty** — it makes no difference whether he proceeds with answering or stands on his right." *Von Behren*, 822 F.3d at 1151. And most directly: "When the government conditions continued supervised release on compliance with a treatment program requiring full disclosure of past sexual misconduct, with no provision of immunity for disclosed conduct, it unconstitutionally compels self-incrimination." *United States v. Bahr*, 730 F.3d 963, 966 (9th Cir. 2013), *quoted in Von Behren*, 822 F.3d at 1151.

**Why *Von Behren* is a closer fit to this contract than to the polygraph condition.** *Von Behren* was, structurally, a **treatment contract case**. Its facts are these: a supervisee was assigned to a certified provider; the provider "presented Mr. Von Behren with a **non-negotiable treatment agreement**"; the agreement required a sexual-history polygraph; "[f]ailure to complete the sexual history polygraph would result in removal from the program"; and the agreement contained an instruction to "report to any appropriate authority or authorities any occurrence or potential occurrence of any sexual offense on my part regardless of how RSA, Inc. gains knowledge of such occurrence." 822 F.3d at 1142. On that last provision the Tenth Circuit said: "This provision, which specifically authorizes his examiner to report his admissions to the police, **undoubtedly adds to Mr. Von Behren's apprehension** in regard to answering." *Id.* at 1147.

The R.K.K.S. contract contains the same three elements: a non-negotiable form, a compelled-disclosure requirement, and a reporting provision (Confidentiality ¶ 2 and Consequences ¶ 1) that routes disclosures to Probation and permits routing them to law enforcement.

**The government's argument, and *Von Behren*'s answer to it.** The government will say the penalty is for failing to complete treatment, not for invoking the privilege. The Tenth Circuit heard and rejected exactly that: "Mr. Von Behren was not permitted to complete treatment **solely and directly as a result of** invoking his Fifth Amendment privilege and refusing to answer incriminating questions. The government's argument is a **distinction without a difference**." 822 F.3d at 1150. *Accord United States v. York*, 357 F.3d 14, 24-25 (1st Cir. 2004) (recognizing it "would be constitutionally problematic" if a supervised-release provision requiring sex offender treatment "require[d] York to submit to polygraph testing ... so that York's refusal to answer any questions — even on valid Fifth Amendment grounds — could constitute a basis for revocation") (*quoted in Von Behren*, 822 F.3d at 1150).

**Compare the Judgment — and note what it does *not* authorize.** Special Condition (b) requires participation in treatment and compliance with program rules; Special Condition (h) requires participation in mental health treatment. **Neither authorizes any disclosure by the provider to anyone.** The Judgment addressed provider-generated disclosure exactly once, in Special Condition (g), and when it did, it imposed a **limit**: "The results of any polygraph pre-tests and polygraph examinations may be disclosed to the US Probation office and the Court, but **shall not be further disclosed without a court order**." And it authorized one other specific disclosure, in Special Condition (e): release of the presentence report to the New York State Board of Examiners of Sex Offenders and to the county court.

So the Judgment enumerated its disclosures and, in the one instance closest to treatment material, capped further dissemination absent a court order. Confidentiality ¶ 2's authorization to report to "**other individuals and agencies involved in my treatment**," and Consequences ¶ 1's authorization to contact "**law enforcement**," both exceed what the Judgment permits for the nearest analogous category. That is an *expressio unius* argument with a built-in contrast clause, and it is one of the cleaner arguments in this memo.

**Code of Conduct ¶ 6's criminal-behavior clause is the sharpest single term.** It requires Mr. Bernecky to affirmatively notify treatment staff of "criminal behaviors [outside of or in addition to Instant Offense or pending charges]" — that is, of uncharged crimes — with no immunity, to a party obliged to report to Probation and permitted to call the police, on pain of discharge. Where Special Condition (g)'s compelled-answer sentence at least operates only during testing capped at four sessions a year, Code of Conduct ¶ 6 operates continuously.

**Why the SORA proceeding makes this concrete.** MOTION-v5 ¶ 210 establishes that statements Mr. Bernecky made in treatment "are discussed at length in the materials before that court and are being used to argue for a higher risk classification." That is not apprehension; it is documented, present-tense use in another forum by another sovereign. *Von Behren*'s incrimination analysis emphasized Federal Rules of Evidence 413 and 414 propensity use, 822 F.3d at 1146; New York's SORA risk-assessment process performs a functionally similar propensity role in a state proceeding. **[VERIFY — I have not researched whether New York SORA proceedings are "criminal cases" for Fifth Amendment purposes; there is state case law on this and the answer may be no, which would matter. Flagged as a research task. Note that *Murphy v. Waterfront Commission*, 378 U.S. 52 (1964), already cited in MOTION-v5 ¶ 194, supplies the cross-sovereign point independently.]**

**Adverse authority.** *McKune v. Lile*, 536 U.S. 24 (2002), upheld a prison sex-offender program requiring admission of past offenses against a Fifth Amendment challenge. *Von Behren* distinguishes it directly: "While the holding in *McKune* sheds light on what penalties rise to the level of compulsion in the context of prison inmates, Mr. Von Behren is on supervised release, not in prison," 822 F.3d at 1148, and Justice O'Connor's controlling concurrence expressly declined to endorse penalties like "longer incarceration." *Id.* (quoting *McKune*, 536 U.S. at 52 (O'Connor, J., concurring)). Cite *Von Behren* for the distinction rather than briefing *McKune* fresh. Note also that if the client's account in § 4.5 is right and he signed this contract while still in BOP custody, the government may try to pull *McKune* closer; the answer is that the obligations run through his supervised release, not through his custody.

**Related contract term — Requirements ¶ 3.** "I understand that I may be asked to discuss my treatment progress and treatment assignments in group therapy, with my Probation Officer, and **other significant people in my life**." Compelled disclosure of therapeutic content to unnamed private persons has no authorization in the Judgment and no evident therapeutic necessity that could not be met by consent at the time. Combined with **Requirements ¶ 4** ("I will actively encourage the significant people in my life to participate in treatment"), the contract obliges Mr. Bernecky to recruit third parties into his treatment and then discuss his treatment with them. On the privilege side, this is a compelled waiver of the psychotherapist-patient privilege recognized in *Jaffee v. Redmond*, 518 U.S. 1 (1996), as to an undefined class of people. **This is the weakest of the flagged points** — courts routinely permit treatment-to-probation reporting in this posture — but "other significant people in my life" is specific enough to be objectionable on its own.

#### TC-10. Code of Conduct ¶ 6 — financial and lifestyle reporting to a private provider

**The language.** "I will notify treatment staff of any change in my residence or employment status and any other changes in relevant risk factors (including, but not limited to, relationship status, social networks, substance abuse, **significant changes in income/debt**, mobility [vehicle, Driver License], health status, criminal behaviors [outside of or in addition to Instant Offense or pending charges], medication compliance/changes, **means of communication**, **etc.**)." Code of Conduct ¶ 6 (p. 2).

**The problem: exceeds Judgment authorization on multiple axes + vagueness.**

**Financial.** Special Conditions (d) and (i) both run to "**the U.S. Probation Office**" and nobody else. Special Condition (d) states its own purpose: "In order to monitor the defendant's compliance with not buying or subscribing to online services that provide child pornography." Neither condition mentions a treatment provider, and neither purpose is served by telling a counseling practice about a raise or a car loan. MOTION-v5 Part III already argues that Special Condition (d) cannot reach further than the offense gives Probation reason to look (¶ 168, citing *United States v. Jenkins*, 854 F.3d 181, 194 (2d Cir. 2017)). The same argument applies *a fortiori* to a private recipient with no stated purpose at all.

**Communications.** "[M]eans of communication" and "social networks" require Mr. Bernecky to report to a private party the same class of information Special Condition (a) requires him to report to the Probation Office. The Judgment picked the recipient.

**Residence and employment.** Standard Conditions 5 and 7 already require notice of residence and employment changes to the probation officer, on stated timelines (10 days in advance; 72 hours if unanticipated). Code of Conduct ¶ 6 creates a parallel, timeline-less duty to a second recipient, so that a single life event generates two independent noncompliance exposures.

**Vagueness.** "[A]ny other changes in relevant risk factors ... **etc.**" is an open-ended obligation in a document whose breach can produce discharge. *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 537.

**Compare *Browder*.** *Browder* did not involve financial reporting, so the comparison is by reasoning rather than by facts: a provider term requiring disclosures the sentence directed to a different recipient, for purposes the sentence never identified, "contradict[s], rather than execute[s]," the sentence. 866 F.3d at 514.

#### TC-11. Confidentiality ¶ 3 — contact with other clients

**The language.** "I will not disclose any information regarding another client to anyone outside this program. I agree to have **no contact with other clients outside my treatment group without prior approval of treatment staff**. I will keep treatment staff informed of the nature of any contact I have with another client outside treatment sessions." Confidentiality ¶ 3 (p. 1).

**The problem: shifts the Standard Condition 8 decisionmaker to a private party; internal contradiction; association burden.**

**Compare the Judgment.** Standard Condition 8: "If you know someone has been convicted of a felony, you must not knowingly communicate or interact with that person without first getting **the permission of the probation officer**." The Judgment identified the permission-giver. Confidentiality ¶ 3 substitutes "treatment staff" for a defined subset of that population.

**Compare *Browder*.** Same structural defect the panel identified at 866 F.3d at 514 — approval required from a "treatment team" the district court "did not address and over which it had no direct oversight."

**Internal contradiction.** Requirements ¶ 4 requires Mr. Bernecky to "actively encourage the significant people in my life to participate in treatment." If a significant person in his life becomes a client of the Practice, Requirements ¶ 4 requires the recruitment and Confidentiality ¶ 3 forbids the contact without permission.

**Practical burden.** MOTION-v5 ¶ 244 already establishes that Mr. Bernecky "is required to attend a counseling and support group composed of people convicted of felonies." Confidentiality ¶ 3 means that every person the Court's own conditions place in a room with him becomes a person he may not speak to outside that room without a private party's leave. That is a useful, concrete illustration for Part VI's felon-contact argument.

#### TC-12. SC ¶ 6 — uncapped "ongoing assessment," including "computer based sexual interest assessments"

**The language.** "I understand that **ongoing assessment** of my progress through psychological and physiological evaluation (**including but not limited to computer based sexual interest assessments and polygraph**) will be part of treatment." SC ¶ 6 (p. 3).

**The problem: exceeds the Judgment's express frequency cap; probably exceeds its instrument list; no cost limit.**

**Compare the Judgment.** Special Condition (g) opens: "The defendant shall submit to polygraph, computerized voice stress analyzer or other such testing, **not to exceed twice in a calendar year, and an additional two re-tests per year, as needed**." This Court set a number. SC ¶ 6 sets none, and describes assessment as "ongoing."

This is a cleaner version of the *Kunz* problem than the ones already in MOTION-v5 Part I.B, because there is no interpretive question at all: the Judgment states a numeric cap, and the contract omits it. "[T]his term exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court, and therefore may not be enforced as written." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68.

**Instrument scope.** Whether "computer based sexual interest assessments" is within "polygraph, computerized voice stress analyzer or other such testing" is a real question. The Judgment's second sentence — "examinations using a polygraph, computerized voice stress analyzer, or **other similar device**" — suggests the drafter had deception-detection instruments in mind. A visual-reaction-time or viewing-time sexual-interest instrument measures something else entirely. At minimum the point is arguable and worth a request for clarification rather than a demand for striking. **[VERIFY — no research done on case law governing sexual-interest assessments as supervised-release conditions, or on their evidentiary status. Open task.]**

**Cost.** Special Condition (g) says "The defendant is required to contribute to the cost of services rendered," as do (b) and (h). Code of Conduct ¶ 2 requires him to "pay my assigned copay at the time of each session unless I have made other arrangements with the Practice **and my Probation Officer**." The Judgment authorizes cost contribution but sets no amount and states no standard; the amount is set by the Practice, with Probation's concurrence. This overlaps existing research (research-output/part26-fee-amount-delegation.md; part12-fee-figures.md) and is cross-referenced rather than re-analyzed here.

#### TC-13. Code of Conduct ¶ 1 — attendance, and its collision with Standard Condition 7

**The language.** "I will attend all treatment sessions and be on time. I understand that **the only acceptable excuse for absence or lateness is a verifiable medical or other emergency**. ... I understand that after three unexcused absences my case will be reviewed for discharge." Code of Conduct ¶ 1 (p. 2).

**The problem: conflicting court-imposed obligations, with no priority rule.**

Standard Condition 7 requires Mr. Bernecky to "work full time (at least 30 hours per week) at a lawful type of employment." Mandatory Condition 6 and Special Condition (e) require SORA compliance, and MOTION-v5 ¶ 210 establishes he is a respondent in an active Monroe County Court proceeding. Neither a work shift nor a court appearance is "a verifiable medical or other emergency." Under Code of Conduct ¶ 1 read literally, appearing in the state court proceeding that his federal Judgment set in motion is an unexcused absence, and three of them trigger discharge review.

This is a modest point and should be a sentence, not a section — but it is a good sentence, because it shows the document was drafted without reference to the Judgment it purports to implement.

#### TC-14. Confidentiality ¶ 4 — automatic consequences for expressing ideation

**The language.** "I understand that the expression of suicidal or homicidal ideation **will result in one or more the following**: notification to my Probation Officer; a referral to mobile crisis; or a mental hygiene arrest." Confidentiality ¶ 4 (p. 1).

**Assessment: mostly defensible, one flag.** A duty to warn and a duty to act on imminent risk are ordinary and largely required of New York mental health practitioners. What is objectionable is the automaticity as written: "the **expression** of suicidal or homicidal ideation **will result in**" one of three listed outcomes, one of which is arrest — with no imminence or dangerousness screen of the kind New York's own emergency-admission statutes require. **[VERIFY — N.Y. Mental Hygiene Law §§ 9.41, 9.45 and the "likely to result in serious harm" standard are stated here from general knowledge; I did not read the statutes this session. Do not put a statutory citation in a filing without reading the text.]**

The practical significance is not the arrest risk. It is that a document announcing automatic law-enforcement consequences for expressing a thought sits badly beside a document (Requirements ¶ 1, SC ¶ 7) demanding total candor about thoughts. **The contract demands complete disclosure and simultaneously announces penalties for the content of what is disclosed.** That is a fair point to make in one sentence, and it reinforces the compelled-disclosure argument in TC-6 through TC-9.

#### TC-15. Requirements ¶¶ 1-2 — the Practice controls the duration of court-ordered obligations

**The language.** Requirements ¶ 2: "I will actively participate in treatment. I understand that **the length of time I spend in treatment is dependent on my readiness to change**, my willingness to comply with treatment tasks, fulfill treatment goals, and demonstrate sustained change in my thinking and behaviors."

**The problem: a delegation chain that reaches a liberty restriction the Judgment made time-limited.**

Special Conditions (b) and (h) both require Mr. Bernecky to remain in treatment "until complete[ion] or as ordered by the Court." Requirements ¶ 2 makes completion a function of the Practice's assessment of his "readiness to change" — an internal, unreviewable, unstandardized judgment.

**And it has a downstream consequence the Writer will want.** Special Condition (h)'s alcohol prohibition is triggered by being "in treatment." If the Practice controls when treatment ends, the Practice controls how long the alcohol prohibition lasts. That is a delegation of the *duration* of a liberty restriction to a private party — squarely within *Matta*'s prohibition and squarely on the "subtractive" side of *Jimenez*'s line. It is also a direct reinforcement of MOTION-v5 Part V.

#### TC-16. Code of Conduct ¶ 3 — the alcohol term (favorable)

Analyzed at § 4.6 above. This one is not an objection; it is evidence. The provider whose treatment triggers Special Condition (h)'s abstinence requirement asks only that Mr. Bernecky "refrain from excessive use of alcohol."

Two uses:
1. **Documentary corroboration for Part V.** It converts "nobody has told him he may not drink" into "the treatment provider handed him a signed document requiring something less than abstinence."
2. **A fair-notice defect in its own right.** Mr. Bernecky holds two documents he must obey, saying different things about the same conduct, with no rule for which controls. *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 536 ("too vague if it requires a reasonable person to speculate as to its meaning").

**Caution.** Do not argue that the contract *supersedes* Special Condition (h). It does not, and a provider cannot dispense with a court-ordered condition any more than it can add one — the principle cuts both ways and the motion's credibility depends on saying so.

## 6. CONCLUSION — my own read, and how confident I am

**Strong (I would file these):**

- **TC-1 (sexually stimulating material), TC-2 (platform pre-approval), TC-3 (intimate partners), TC-4 (sole-discretion discharge).** Each is a liberty restriction with no counterpart anywhere in the Judgment, defined or administered by a private party. *Browder*'s holding, *Carlineo*'s vagueness and delegation holdings, and *Jimenez*'s own additive/subtractive line all point the same direction. **High confidence.**
- **TC-12 (frequency cap).** The Judgment states a number; the contract omits it. There is no interpretive question. **High confidence**, though the relief is narrow.
- **The Consequences ¶ 3(a) structural finding** (Probation sits on the Treatment Team). **High confidence** that this is factually correct and legally significant; it is the fact that makes *Kunz* apply to a private document.
- **TC-16 (alcohol).** Favorable, documentary, and already supports an existing Part. **High confidence.**

**Solid but secondary:**

- **TC-5 (minor contact).** The three divergences are real, but *Browder*'s facts do not transfer because of the null-set carve-out. **Moderate-to-high confidence** on the reasoning; **low confidence** in any framing that leans on the children parallel.
- **TC-6 through TC-9 (compelled disclosure).** The legal rule is strong and *Von Behren* is closely on point structurally. The weaknesses are (i) ripeness — no disclosure has yet been demanded and refused; (ii) *McKune*, distinguishable but real; (iii) the Second Circuit has not adopted *Von Behren*, and *United States v. Johnson*, 446 F.3d 272 (2d Cir. 2006), sits in the way (MOTION-v5 ¶ 202 already addresses this). **Moderate confidence**, and this material belongs folded into Part IV rather than standing alone.
- **TC-10 (financial reporting to provider).** Clean textual point; low stakes. **High confidence, low value.**
- **TC-11 (other-client contact).** Clean textual point; strongest as an illustration inside Part VI. **High confidence, moderate value.**

**Weak — include only as supporting sentences, if at all:**

- **TC-13 (attendance conflict)**, **TC-14 (ideation)**, **TC-15 (duration)**, **Requirements ¶ 3 (disclosure to "significant people")**. Each is genuinely objectionable but individually thin. TC-15 is the best of the four because of its link to Part V.

**The single biggest risk to this whole line of argument** is *Jimenez*. A government lawyer who finds it will argue that the Second Circuit approved, three months ago, exactly this arrangement — a treatment condition whose details are "determined by service providers and probation officers." The motion must cite *Jimenez* first, in Mr. Bernecky's own voice, and draw the line *Jimenez* itself draws: additive versus subtractive; details versus additional burdens. A motion that ignores *Jimenez* and gets it back in the government's response is in much worse shape than one that owns it.

**The second biggest risk** is over-reading *Browder*. It is a mixed decision that reversed a violation finding on facts (a children carve-out) Mr. Bernecky does not share. Quote its reasoning; do not describe it as having vacated conditions or as having invalidated treatment agreements generally.

## 8. FOR THE WRITER — plain-English punch list

*Recommendations only. I have not edited MOTION-v5.md.*

**1. Do not create a new Part VIII for the contract. Expand Part II instead.**
Part II is currently a page and a half of pure procedure — it asks for an objection mechanism without ever showing the Court a single provider rule that needs objecting to. That is the motion's biggest structural weakness, and this contract fixes it. Part II should open with the document, walk through four or five specific terms, and *then* ask for the procedure. A judge who has just read "sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind as defined by my treatment staff" will grant a procedure. A judge who has read only an abstract request for a procedure may not see the need.

**2. Lead Part II with the *Browder* genealogy finding, not with *Browder*'s facts.**
The strongest sentence available: the conditions Mr. Bernecky is serving are a near-verbatim copy of the condition set a judge of this District wrote on remand in *Browder*, three months before this Judgment was entered — in an opinion holding that a supervisee "cannot be forced to sign or abide by agreements that reach beyond the Court's ordered conditions." That is a much better opening than reciting Browder's daughters. (§ 3.2.)

**3. Kill the government's best argument in the first paragraph.**
The government will say Special Condition (b) commands him to "follow the rules and regulations of that program," so the rules are the Court's. Answer it immediately with *Browder* footnote 4: Browder's condition said he "is to comply with the mandates of the treatment program," and the Second Circuit reversed his violation anyway. (§ 3.3.)

**4. Make Consequences ¶ 3(a) the hinge of the Part.**
"The Treatment Team is comprised of all therapists in the Practice, the Probation Officer, and the Probation Supervisor." Once that sentence is on the page, the *McDonald* objection ("a court lacks authority to tell a private treatment provider how to enforce their rules") stops being an obstacle: as to anything the Treatment Team does, the decisionmaker includes Probation, and *Kunz* governs Probation.

**5. Pick four terms, not sixteen.**
My recommendation: SC ¶ 8 (sexually stimulating material), SC ¶ 5 (platform approval, and say "email" out loud), SC ¶ 2 (intimate partners and offense-history disclosure), Consequences ¶ 2(vi) (sole discretion). Then a short paragraph gesturing at the rest. Sixteen numbered objections will read as a catalogue and dilute the four that matter.

**6. Use the "No special conditions designated" line.**
The form had a place for individualized conditions and the provider wrote nothing in it, while SC ¶ 1 addresses a defendant on pretrial diversion whom Mr. Bernecky is not. One sentence, and it establishes the blanket-form point without argument.

**7. Cite *Jimenez* before the government does, and use its own test.**
Frame it as: the Second Circuit held this year that a treatment condition is fine when the discretion it confers is "additive, not subtractive" and does not "impose additional burdens." Then list what this contract subtracts. Turning the newest adverse case into the motion's own test is worth more than avoiding it.

**8. Fold the compelled-disclosure material into Part IV, not Part II.**
Part IV already carries the Fifth Amendment argument. Add a short subsection making the point that the polygraph condition is not the only place compulsion operates — Code of Conduct ¶ 6 requires continuous reporting of uncharged "criminal behaviors" to a party that reports to Probation and may call the police, with no cap and no immunity. Use *Von Behren*'s treatment-contract facts (non-negotiable agreement, reporting clause, expulsion) which are much closer to this document than to Special Condition (g). And use the Judgment's own contrast: Special Condition (g) capped further disclosure absent a court order; Confidentiality ¶ 2 and Consequences ¶ 1 do not.

**9. Add the alcohol contract term to Part V — it is the best fact in that Part.**
Two sentences: Special Condition (h) commands abstinence while in treatment; the treatment provider's own contract asks only that he "refrain from excessive use of alcohol." Then the existing argument. Do not argue the contract supersedes the condition.

**10. Add one sentence to Part VI.**
Confidentiality ¶ 3 forbids contact with other clients outside group without treatment-staff approval — so every person the Court's own conditions place in a room with him becomes a person he needs a private party's permission to speak to. That is a better illustration of Standard Condition 8's absurdity than the apartment-hallway hypothetical, because it actually happened.

**11. Shape all relief against Probation and the Court's own order — never against R.K.K.S.**
Part II's existing relief is already correctly shaped. Sharpen it with three additions drawn from the *Browder* remand: (i) an order that Mr. Bernecky's compliance with Special Conditions (b) and (h) may not be made contingent on his agreeing to any provider term that imposes a restriction not found in this Judgment; (ii) leave to **amend and initial** the specific terms identified, which is the remedy Judge Vilardo actually ordered in *Browder*; and (iii) a direction that Probation state in writing which terms of this contract it regards as within the Judgment.

**12. Two small textual corrections while you are in the file.**
(a) Add a footnote noting that the Judgment's Special Conditions are unlettered and that the motion's (a)-(j) lettering is its own convention in document order. (b) Quote Standard Condition 12's second sentence ("The probation officer may contact the person and confirm that you have notified the person about the risk") in Part VII — it strengthens the *Boles* point.

**13. One argument outside this dispatch, flagged because I found it and it should not be lost.**
The *Browder* remand condition reads "As triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity, **and based on reasonable suspicion**, the defendant shall consent to and cooperate with unannounced examinations..." — words Judge Vilardo added expressly "to remove any doubt." Mr. Bernecky's Special Condition (a) does not contain them, though it otherwise copies the Browder remand text. That belongs in Part I.
