## 1. INTRODUCTION

This memorandum tests one narrow, practical premise the client has built into his decision to proceed with the § 722-c funding motion already drafted in `SORA-COUNTY-LAW-722-C-EXPERT-FUNDING-MOTION-DRAFT.md`: per the client-directives log, the client "acknowledges and accepts the risk that a forensic evaluation could find Jeff presents a threat to public safety, and that requesting funds means the fact an evaluation occurred may become known even if the substance is not disclosed." `client-directives.md` at 3-8 (Aug. 15, 2026 entry). The client has decided to proceed regardless, so this memorandum does not revisit that decision. Its job is narrower: is the premise itself — that the *fact* of the evaluation is likely to leak even if the *substance* is withheld — legally accurate, an overstatement, or roughly right, and what should change procedurally to make the protection the client is relying on actually real rather than assumed.

**Scope.** Four sub-questions, addressed in order: (1) how § 722-c's ex parte procedure actually operates and whether anything about SORA specifically undercuts it; (2) whether New York law requires disclosure of a retained-but-unused expert in a SORA proceeding or an analogous civil proceeding; (3) whether NY law protects a non-testifying consulting expert's work the way federal Rule 26(b)(4)(D) does; and (4) a direct practical answer plus concrete recommendations.

## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1:** Does the § 722-c ex parte procedure itself create any built-in mechanism, or realistic risk, by which the Board or DA would learn that an evaluation occurred, and does anything about SORA specifically undercut the confidentiality ordinary indigent-defense practice assumes?

*Conclusion:* The statute's own text supplies the ex parte *finding* procedure but, standing alone, supplies **no sealing or confidentiality mechanism at all** — that has to be separately requested and separately granted under other authority. Done correctly (a standalone motion, not folded into an omnibus filing; heard ex parte if the court has any hesitation; and an affirmative request for a sealing order under Judiciary Law § 2-b(3) and 22 NYCRR 216.1, obtained at the close of the process), the existing indigent-defense practice this motion follows is designed precisely to keep both the fact and the substance from the DA, and nothing about SORA's structure defeats that — Correction Law article 6-C is one of the proceeding types § 722-c names by name, on the same footing as Family Court and criminal practice, and the NYSDA guide's confidentiality guidance is not written as criminal-only. But the protection is not self-executing, and there are real, SORA-specific and record-keeping-specific leak channels the motion as currently drafted does not yet fully close. Section 4, Issue 1 sets these out.

**Issue 2:** Does New York law require disclosure of a retained-but-unused, non-testifying expert's identity or findings in a SORA proceeding or an analogous civil proceeding?

*Conclusion:* No, on two independent grounds. First, CPLR 3101(d)(1)(i) by its own terms reaches only "each person whom the party expects to call as an expert witness at trial" — an expert a party decides *not* to call falls outside the provision altogether, a distinction New York courts have recognized. Second, and more fundamentally for this case, SORA proceedings do not import CPLR Article 31's disclosure regime in the first place; § 168-n(3) is a sui generis, self-contained disclosure mechanism (pre-hearing notice of the Board's own package, plus an adjournment-and-subpoena tool reaching "any state or local facility, hospital, institution, office, agency, department or division") that has no textual hook for compelling a party's own privately retained, unused consultant. Nothing in this file's own prior research, and nothing found in this pass, identifies any mechanism by which either the Board or the DA could compel disclosure of an evaluation Respondent chooses not to use.

**Issue 3:** Is there a NY analog to the federal non-testifying-expert work-product protection, and does it reach a SORA proceeding?

*Conclusion:* Yes in substance, though the leading case for the specific "consulting-only expert" proposition remains unverified by me directly. CPLR 3101(d)(2)'s trial-preparation-materials protection (qualified — substantial need/undue hardship) and CPLR 3101(c)'s absolute attorney-work-product protection combine, per secondary-source consensus and a single named, if unverified, case, to place a purely consulting, non-testifying expert's work essentially beyond ordinary discovery. No case — SORA or otherwise — was found applying this specifically to a SORA proceeding, which is unsurprising given Issue 2's conclusion that SORA does not import CPLR Article 31 discovery to begin with: the protection matters as a backstop, not as the primary reason disclosure isn't required here.

**Issue 4:** Is the client's stated risk premise accurate, an overstatement, or roughly right, and what should change procedurally?

*Conclusion:* An overstatement as a matter of law, but not a paranoid one as a matter of practice — the risk is real only to the extent the motion's own confidentiality request is left as soft as it currently is, or to the extent the fact of an evaluation leaks through a channel other than the § 722-c motion itself (a later adjournment application, a compensation voucher, or something Respondent himself says on the certification questionnaire). Section 6 gives concrete, low-cost fixes for each.

## 4. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### Issue 1: How § 722-c's ex parte procedure actually works, and what could still expose it

**A. The statute itself.** County Law § 722-c's operative clause, as quoted directly in the NYSDA guide's own text (tier (a), read by me directly from the guide's extracted PDF text; corroborated word-for-word by two independent fetch-tool renderings of the current codified text):

> "Upon a finding in an ex parte proceeding that investigative, expert or other services are necessary and that the defendant or other person described in section two hundred forty-nine or section two hundred sixty-two of the family court act, article six-c of the correction law or section four hundred seven of the surrogate's court procedure act, is financially unable to obtain them, the court shall authorize counsel... to obtain the services."

That is the entire confidentiality mechanism the statute itself supplies: the *finding* is made without the adversary present. The statute says nothing about sealing the motion papers, nothing about keeping the order out of the case file, and nothing about the docket. "Ex parte" describes how the *decision* gets made, not what happens to the *paper* afterward. This matters directly for the client's premise: the statute does not, by its own text, guarantee that the *fact* of the application stays hidden once made — that has to come from somewhere else.

**B. Where the actual confidentiality comes from — and that it is not automatic.** The NYSDA guide is explicit that sealing is something counsel must affirmatively obtain, not something that follows automatically from the application being ex parte (tier (a), guide's own text):

> "Do not put § 722-c applications in omnibus motion filings. These motions must be carefully detailed and the District Attorney should not have any input into whether it is appropriate to grant funds to hire a defense expert." NYSDA Guide at 4.

> "Counsel may also wish to ask the court to seal the application and order in the court's files to protect the continuing confidentiality of the defense strategy. Judiciary Law § 2-b(3)." *Id.*

> "Since the application is ex parte, these details can be confidentially revealed to the court; requesting a sealing order at the conclusion of the process ensures that the information remains confidential." *Id.* at 8.

The guide also quotes, in this context, *Marshall v. United States*, 423 F.2d 1315, 1318 (10th Cir. 1970) — federal, non-binding, but descriptive of the underlying rationale — for the proposition that "[t]he manifest purpose of requiring that the inquiry be ex parte is to insure that the defendant will not have to make a premature disclosure of his case." (b), guide's characterization, not independently read.

Two authorities are doing the actual sealing work here, and both need to be named in the motion itself, not left to the court's unguided discretion:

1. **Judiciary Law § 2-b(3)** — the general residual power of a court of record "to devise and make new process and forms of proceedings, necessary to carry into effect the powers and jurisdiction possessed by it." (b)-single-source, fetch-tool rendering of the current text. This is not a targeted sealing statute; it is the general inherent-authority hook courts have used to fashion a sealing order in this specific context, per the guide's citation.
2. **22 NYCRR 216.1** — the actual administrative rule governing how a New York court seals any record: "a court shall not enter an order in any action or proceeding sealing the court records, whether in whole or in part, except upon a written finding of good cause, which shall specify the grounds thereof." (b)-single-source, WebSearch synthesis, not independently corroborated by a second fetch — this should be confirmed against the official rule text before the motion is finalized, but it is consistent with how NY sealing orders are generally described in practice.

The practical consequence: a court can grant § 722-c funding without ever being asked to seal anything, and if no sealing order is requested and entered, the motion and any order granting it are, on their face, ordinary case-file documents — nothing in the statute itself keeps them confidential once the ex parte finding is made. The current motion draft's "if the Court deems it appropriate" phrasing leaves this exact gap open.

**C. Does anything about SORA specifically undercut this, the way it might not in an ordinary criminal case?** The assignment specifically asked me to test this rather than assume the ordinary criminal-defense practice transfers cleanly. Two SORA-specific features are relevant, and neither defeats the practice — but both create narrower leak risks the criminal-defense literature isn't written to address:

- **The SORA proceeding is captioned as, and often docketed under, the same index number as the underlying criminal case** — as this file's own motion draft is, "PEOPLE OF THE STATE OF NEW YORK... -against- JEFFREY BERNECKY... Index No. [___]." Unlike a freestanding Family Court Act § 249/262 application, a SORA § 722-c motion is filed into a case file the DA already has full access to as a party. That makes the *separate-filing-and-sealing* step the guide recommends more important here, not less — in an ordinary criminal case the same dynamic exists, but the SORA docket is unusually compact (essentially one classification proceeding, on a short calendar, with a single hearing date), meaning any new filing on that docket is more conspicuous by its mere appearance than a motion buried in months of pretrial criminal practice would be. This is a reason to be more careful about the separate-filing/sealing request here, not a reason the underlying practice fails to apply.
- **§ 168-n(3)'s own adjournment-and-subpoena mechanism reaches "any state or local facility, hospital, institution, office, agency, department or division"** — already established in `SORA-PSR-RELIABILITY-DISCOVERY-RESEARCH-MEMO.md` §6. A privately retained forensic evaluator, paid through § 722-c funds, is not a "state or local facility, hospital, institution, office, agency, department or division"; nothing in that provision gives the DA a textual hook to reach a defense-retained consultant even if the DA somehow learned one existed. This SORA-specific mechanism does not create additional exposure — it affirmatively does not reach this material.

**D. Realistic non-motion leak channels — the honest answer to what the client's premise gets right.** Even with a properly sealed § 722-c motion, three channels outside the motion itself could reveal that an evaluation occurred, and the client's instinct that "the fact... may become known even if the substance is not" is not baseless as to these:

1. **A later adjournment application.** If Respondent needs more time specifically to complete or await the evaluation, and that reason is stated on the record in a motion the DA sees (as the companion `SORA-ADJOURNMENT-MOTION-DRAFT.md` currently does, grounding its request on the counsel-assignment and disclosure-demand timeline, not on the evaluation), the DA could infer an evaluation is underway. The current adjournment draft does not reference the evaluation, which is correct practice — but if a further adjournment becomes necessary closer to September 11 specifically because of evaluation timing, that request needs to be worded generically ("additional time to prepare Respondent's case" or similar), not tied explicitly to "awaiting a forensic evaluation."
2. **Compensation vouchers.** § 722-c itself requires "a sworn statement specifying the time expended, services rendered, expenses incurred" as a condition of payment. *Koberstein*, 262 A.D.2d 1032, shows this kind of funding history can resurface — there, the same court, on a *later* funding request, referenced a prior expert who had been funded and never called. That is the court's own institutional record, not necessarily DA-visible, but it establishes that the funding/compensation trail does not vanish once the motion itself is sealed. Whether Monroe County's own voucher-processing channel is DA-visible or FOIL-able was not independently confirmed and is flagged in Section 3 as unknown.
3. **Respondent's own hearing submissions.** The client-directives log notes an intention to fold the client's personal read on Bernecky's character into "Jeff's own certification answers (Questionnaire, Questions 8-9)." `client-directives.md` at 12-16. That questionnaire is itself part of the hearing record the DA will see. Care should be taken that nothing in it inadvertently references the fact that an evaluation was obtained, if the decision is ultimately not to use it.

None of these three channels is compelled by law — they are all channels created by what the defense itself chooses to say or file, not by any legal disclosure obligation running the other direction. That distinction is the crux of the answer to Issue 4 below.

### Issue 2: No disclosure obligation for a retained-but-unused expert, in a SORA proceeding or an analogous civil proceeding

**A. CPLR 3101(d)(1)(i) reaches only experts a party expects to call.** The operative text, obtained via fetch-tool rendering of the current codified section, (b)-corroborated across two independent renderings:

> "Upon request, each party shall identify each person whom the party expects to call as an expert witness at trial and shall disclose in reasonable detail the subject matter on which each expert is expected to testify, the substance of the facts and opinions on which each expert is expected to testify, the qualifications of each expert witness and a summary of the grounds for each expert's opinion."

The obligation is triggered by *expecting to call* the person as a witness. An expert Respondent retains, evaluates with, and then decides not to use falls outside the clause's own terms — there is no "person the party expects to call" once that decision is made. This reading is consistent with the single case found addressing the point directly, discussed in Issue 3 below.

**B. SORA does not import CPLR Article 31's disclosure regime to begin with.** This is the more fundamental point, and it was already established in this file before this memo began. `SORA-PSR-RELIABILITY-DISCOVERY-RESEARCH-MEMO.md` read Correction Law § 168-n(3) directly (b-corroborated in that memo) and concluded: "There is no separate CPLR-style discovery regime grafted onto SORA proceedings — no depositions, no interrogatories, no CPLR Article 31 motion practice. The statute's own mandatory pre-hearing disclosure (the recommendation and statement of reasons, 20 days out) plus this adjournment-and-subpoena provision *is* the mechanism." That mechanism, by its own text, reaches materials from "the state board of examiners of sex offenders or any state or local facility, hospital, institution, office, agency, department or division" — not a party's own privately retained consultant. Even if CPLR 3101(d) would otherwise require disclosure of a testifying expert in an ordinary civil action (it would), nothing in § 168-n(3) grafts that regime onto a SORA classification hearing, and no case was found holding otherwise. Correction Law article 6-C is captioned as a "determination proceeding," not an "action," and SORA appeals are routed through CPLR Articles 55-57 (per Correction Law § 168-n(3), already established elsewhere in this file) — the *appellate* civil-practice rules, not Article 31's pretrial disclosure rules, which is a further textual indication that the Legislature imported only the appellate mechanics of the CPLR into SORA, not its discovery apparatus.

**C. The Mental Hygiene Law Article 10 contrast is affirmative evidence, not just an absence.** This file's own § 722-c motion already treats MHL Article 10 as the closest structural civil analog to what SORA's "threat to public safety" element is supposed to represent. Article 10 supplies a useful data point precisely because it shows what the Legislature does when it *does* want a respondent's own funded examiner disclosed to the opposing side. MHL § 10.06(e) provides, in relevant part (b)-single-source, fetch-tool rendering, should be independently confirmed before quotation in a filing):

> "Following the evaluation, such psychiatric examiner shall report his or her findings in writing to the respondent or counsel for the respondent, to the attorney general, and to the court."

That is an explicit, mandatory, three-way disclosure requirement — to the respondent, to the state's own counsel (the Attorney General, Article 10's analog to the DA here), and to the court — regardless of whether the findings are favorable or unfavorable to the respondent. Correction Law article 6-C contains no comparable provision, and County Law § 722-c contains none either. Where the Legislature wanted this outcome, in a proceeding this file's own research already treats as the closest civil analog, it said so in explicit terms. Its absence from SORA's own statutory scheme is not an oversight this memo can fill in by analogy in the other direction — if anything, it cuts against reading any implied disclosure duty into SORA.

### Issue 3: NY's analog to non-testifying-expert work product

**A. The doctrinal structure.** CPLR 3101(c) provides, in full (per fetch-tool rendering, (b)-single-source): "The work product of an attorney shall not be obtainable." CPLR 3101(d)(2) separately provides a qualified protection for "materials... prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial by or for another party, or by or for that other party's representative (including an attorney, consultant, surety, indemnitor, insurer or agent)," discoverable "only upon a showing that the party seeking discovery has substantial need of the materials in the preparation of the case and is unable without undue hardship to obtain the substantial equivalent of the materials by other means," with an absolute carve-out protecting "the mental impressions, conclusions, opinions or legal theories of an attorney or other representative of a party." (b)-corroborated across two independent fetch renderings for the quoted language.

**B. The specific "consulting-only expert" holding.** Secondary-source synthesis, converging consistently across three independent search results, attributes to *Santariga v. McCann*, 161 A.D.2d 320 (1st Dep't 1990), the holding that a radiologist retained "merely as a consultant to evaluate the validity of plaintiff's allegations and to assist in the preparation of a defense, and who will not testify at trial, is a fact witness not subject to CPLR 3101(d)(1) disclosure requirements," and that materials such an expert prepares are protected trial-preparation material under 3101(d)(2). This is (b)-corroborated as to the *existence* and general holding of the case (three independent renderings agree), but I was not able to pull and read the opinion itself — pre-1995 Appellate Division reporters were unreachable through every channel tried in this research pass, the same limitation the prior memos in this file recorded. **This case should not be quoted in any filing until independently confirmed against the actual reported text.**

**C. Application here.** Nothing in the case law found suggests this protection is territorially limited to civil actions in a way that would exclude a SORA proceeding, and nothing SORA-specific overrides it — but the honest answer, consistent with Issue 2's conclusion, is that this protection functions as a *backstop* here, not the primary reason disclosure isn't required: SORA never triggers CPLR Article 31 discovery in the first place, so the question of whether a consulting expert's work would be protected *if* Article 31 applied is somewhat academic on this record. It becomes practically relevant only if the Board or DA attempted, through the § 168-n(3) mechanism or some other avenue, to reach the expert's file directly — and even then, as Issue 1.C notes, a privately retained consultant is not the kind of "state or local facility, hospital, institution, office, agency, department or division" that mechanism reaches at all.

## 5. CONCLUSION

The client's stated premise — that requesting § 722-c funds means "good or bad the result will be known even if it wasn't disclosed" — is **an overstatement as a matter of law**. No provision of County Law § 722-c, Correction Law article 6-C, or CPLR Article 31 creates any obligation running from Respondent to the Board or the DA to disclose that an evaluation occurred, let alone its substance, once Respondent decides not to use it. The ex parte procedure, properly invoked with a specific sealing request rather than the current draft's hedged one, is designed for exactly this situation and has no textual or structural SORA-specific defect that would undercut it here. The Mental Hygiene Law Article 10 contrast affirmatively supports this conclusion rather than merely failing to contradict it.

But the premise is **not paranoid as a matter of practice**, and the client's underlying caution has real value: the protection is not self-executing, is not automatic from the word "ex parte" alone, and can be undercut by choices the defense itself makes — an adjournment request that names the evaluation, a compensation voucher trail, or a certification-questionnaire answer that references it. On the current record, the realistic risk of the fact of the evaluation becoming known runs entirely through what Respondent's own side chooses to put on the record, not through any legal disclosure duty pointing the other way. That is a controllable risk, not an inherent one, and Section 6 below makes it concretely controllable.

## 6. RECOMMENDATIONS

1. **Strengthen the confidentiality request in the § 722-c motion from a hedge to an affirmative request.** Replace "if the Court deems it appropriate, sealed or otherwise handled outside the public docket" with a specific request that the Court (a) hear the application ex parte without the District Attorney's presence or input, consistent with County Law § 722-c and ordinary practice for applications of this kind; and (b) enter a sealing order covering the motion, any supporting papers, and the order itself, citing Judiciary Law § 2-b(3) and 22 NYCRR 216.1, with the written good-cause finding 22 NYCRR 216.1 requires.
2. **File the § 722-c motion as a genuinely separate filing**, not appended to or cross-referenced within the downward-departure memorandum or any other document the DA will see in full — consistent with the NYSDA guide's own instruction not to bury these applications in omnibus filings.
3. **Confirm Monroe County's actual docketing mechanics before filing** — specifically, whether the county's e-filing/index system would generate any DA- or public-visible docket entry for a sealed ex parte motion, and whether the Assigned Counsel Program's compensation-voucher process is itself confidential or FOIL-exposed. This is a local-practice question outside what legal research alone can answer; confirm directly with the clerk's office or the Assigned Counsel Program.
4. **Keep any future adjournment request generic if evaluation timing becomes the actual reason for it.** The current adjournment draft is already correctly framed around the counsel-assignment and disclosure-demand timeline, not the evaluation — preserve that framing, and if a further adjournment is needed closer to September 11 specifically to accommodate the evaluation, word it as a general preparation-time request rather than naming the evaluation as the reason.
5. **Review Respondent's certification-questionnaire answers (Questions 8-9) before filing** to confirm nothing in the client's own narrative inadvertently references the fact that an evaluation was obtained, consistent with the goal of preserving the option not to use it.
6. **Independently confirm *Santariga v. McCann*, 161 A.D.2d 320 (1st Dep't 1990)**, before it is cited or relied upon in any filing or advice to the client — it is currently unverified secondary-source synthesis only, though corroborated across three independent renderings.
7. **Independently confirm the current text of 22 NYCRR 216.1 and Mental Hygiene Law § 10.06(e)** against an official source before either is quoted in a filing — both are currently single-source fetch-tool renderings.
8. **Communicate the corrected risk framing to the client directly**: the law does not require disclosure of an unfavorable, unused evaluation, and the realistic risk is procedural and controllable (Recommendations 1-5), not an inherent feature of requesting the funds. This refines, rather than reverses, the client's own decision to proceed — the decision remains sound, and is if anything on firmer ground than the client's own stated premise assumed.
