## 2. Constitutional-challenge argument — it's an analogy, not a claim of on-point SR authority.

Client, verbatim: "as for the constitutional challenge argument, there has
never been anything to hold that as far as sr is concerned. but [in] many
other areas of law when there is a violation of the constitution itself,
courts have not hesitated to invalidate it. for instance, a sentence in
excess of stat[utory] max can be challenged even with plea waivers, and can
even survive the cause and prejudice test. with regard to plain error
issues, courts of appeal do not use rule 52(b) [with its ordinary strict
standard] when the issue is of constitutional significance. thats what I
was talking about. drawing an analogy." Research now complete (see
`research-output/part28-constitutional-error-analogy.md`):
- Sub-claim (stat-max survives plea waiver): **confirmed and stronger than
  expected** — *Hunter v. United States*, 608 U.S. __ (June 18, 2026),
  names a constitutionally infirm SR condition itself as a paradigm
  "miscarriage of justice" exception. **Client is pulling *Hunter* directly
  — incorporate once provided.** Worth its own look in the polygraph
  section on its own merits, not just as analogy support.
- Sub-claim (cause-and-prejudice): **not confirmed as framed** — runs the
  other way per *Frady*/*Bousley*. Citator trap flagged: *Brown v.
  Caraway*/*Hill v. Masters*/*Wheeler* superseded by *Jones v. Hendrix*
  (2023) — do not cite that line.
- Sub-claim (Rule 52(b) relaxed for constitutional error): **not
  confirmed** — the Second Circuit was reversed in *Marcus* for doing
  exactly this. *Sofsky* remains usable in-circuit authority for excusing
  strict Rule 52(b) compliance on an SR condition, but on a notice/
  sentencing-error rationale, not "constitutional significance loosens the
  rule" — must be described that way if used.
- Structural caution from the researcher: every confirmed instance of a
  procedural bar yielding involves an *appellate* court relaxing its own
  rule. § 3583(e)(2)'s changed-circumstances requirement isn't that kind of
  rule — this analogy reinforces the timing argument, it can't substitute
  for the actual changed circumstance (no exam/question existed at
  sentencing).

## 7. The Matta/delegation passage isn't actually making the right argument.

The passage: "Silence, once it exists, belongs to the Court to fill or
leave empty — not to Probation. 'A district court may not delegate to the
Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a
defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of
discretion.' *United States v. Matta*, 777 F.3d 116, 122 (2d Cir. 2015)..."
Client, verbatim: "this isnt the argument. the argument is, silence does not
equal imposed. its the same idea as expressio unius. silence should always
b read as intentional. the district court imposed the sentence as written.
thats it. and this doesnt mean that you start hedging or anything like
that. the point is, the plain text is what carries the day. you cannot look
back in time and try and justify silence." The core theory needs to be:
silence in the judgment is not an oversight to be filled in later by
anyone — it means exactly what it says, on an expressio-unius logic. The
district court imposed the sentence as written; that's the entire inquiry.
No hedging, no retrospective justification-hunting for why the silence
exists. The Matta delegation point may still be useful as a *supporting*
point (Probation specifically can't fill the silence) but should not be the
lead theory of the section.
