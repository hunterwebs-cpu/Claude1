## 1. INTRODUCTION

This memorandum tests a specific, client-proposed hypothesis: that SORA's legislative history and/or New York courts' own discussion of SORA's purpose shows the Legislature built Level 3's heightened measures — as distinct from Level 2's — with contact-offense/physical-risk offenders in mind, and never contemplated passive, non-contact, image-only offenders as the intended target of that heightened tier. It builds on, but does not restate, the existing Gillotti/Johnson CP-departure line already developed elsewhere in this file, and on the confirmed Level 3-specific consequences (quarterly in-person verification under § 168-h; employer-address disclosure under § 168-l(6)(c)) established in the companion forensic-evaluation memorandum.

**Research method and limitations, stated plainly up front.** I do not have access to Westlaw, Lexis, or a paid citator in this environment, and — unlike some prior research passes in this matter — I was **not** able to reach the primary sources most likely to hold direct answers here. The New York State Reporter (nycourts.gov), Leagle, Casetext, vLex's full case text, Casemine's full case text, the NYC Bar Association's two committee reports (2007 and 2022), and Harvard's Caselaw Access Project all returned HTTP 403 (Cloudflare/WAF blocks) or 404 errors on every attempt, using both the WebFetch tool and direct `curl` requests through this environment's proxy. I could not obtain the actual New York State bill jacket for L. 1995, ch. 192 (the original enactment) or for L. 2002, ch. 11 (the comprehensive amendment that appears to be the actual source of § 168-h's current quarterly-verification structure) from the New York State Library's legislative history collection or any bill-jacket database — that collection was not reachable by any tool available to me. This is a **real, stated gap**, not a considered finding that the material doesn't exist. Every quotation of a New York case in this memorandum was obtained through an automated fetch-and-extract tool applied to a secondary republication of the case (FindLaw, a WebSearch synthesis, etc.), **not read by me character-by-character from the official reporter or a certified copy** — with one important exception: a full, human-authored secondary-source PDF (the Franczyk practice guide, discussed below) that I downloaded and read directly in full. Validation status is stated explicitly for every authority in Section 4 and summarized again in Section 7 (NOT VERIFIED). **No citator check (Shepard's/KeyCite) was possible for any case cited here.**

**Bottom line, stated at the outset:** The direct legislative-history record I could actually reach (quoted secondhand inside a Second Circuit opinion, *Doe v. Pataki*) is general and does not distinguish contact from non-contact offenders in explaining the tiered notification structure — it is thin on this specific point, not silent by design, and the thinness may simply reflect what I could and could not access rather than what exists. The **judicial purpose-discussion** avenue, exactly as the assignment anticipated, turned out to be more productive: a sitting New York Court of Appeals judge (Smith, J., joined by then-Chief Judge Lippman) wrote a dissenting opinion in *People v. Gillotti*, 23 N.Y.3d 841 (2014), stating almost precisely the client's theory in the client's own terms — that the risk-classification scheme's more intensive-monitoring categories were "intended... to apply to physical contact, and not to defendants who possess[] and share[] child pornography." **But it is a dissent.** The controlling *majority* opinion in the same case rejected the argument that CP offenders should be treated differently for risk-level purposes, on a demand-drives-production/harm theory. The honest answer is: **the argument is real, has genuine judicial articulation at the highest court that has considered it, and is not frivolous — but it currently loses 5-2 at the New York Court of Appeals on the closely related question actually presented there (RAI factor-scoring, not the Level 2/Level 3 notification-tier distinction itself), and no case anywhere squarely addresses the specific question this assignment asked (the purpose of the Level 2/Level 3 *notification-tier* differential, as opposed to the purpose of the RAI's *victim-count/relationship* scoring factors).** That is a meaningful distinction the Writer should not blur.

## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1:** Does SORA's original 1995 enactment history (bill jacket, sponsor's memoranda, floor debates, Governor's approval message) or any subsequent amendment's history show the Legislature distinguishing contact/physical-risk offenders from passive/non-contact/image-based offenders in explaining the three-tier risk-level/notification structure, or in bringing child-pornography offenses within SORA's scope?

**Conclusion:** Not shown, on what I could access — but this is substantially an **access gap**, not a confirmed negative. The only legislative-history material I could reach at all was quoted secondhand inside a federal appellate opinion (*Doe v. Pataki*, 120 F.3d 1263 (2d Cir. 1997)): a sponsor's memorandum (Assemblyman Feldman and Senator Skelos) stating the Act is "predominantly regulatory" and protects "the public, especially children," and a bill-jacket statement from Assemblyman Feldman about community notification making the public the "eyes and ears" of law enforcement. Neither passage distinguishes offender types. Separately, that same 1997 opinion notes the original Act's registrable-offense list already included offenses "not involving sexual contact" — meaning non-contact offenses were within SORA's scope from the *original* 1995 enactment, which cuts mildly against a strong version of the client's hypothesis (that non-contact offenders were categorically unanticipated), though it says nothing about whether the Legislature meant such offenders to receive the *same heightened Level 3 measures* as contact offenders. I could not reach the actual bill jacket, floor debate transcript, or Governor's approval memorandum for either the 1995 Act or the 2002 comprehensive amendment (L. 2002, ch. 11) that appears to be the actual source of § 168-h's current 90-day verification structure — the tools available to me could not retrieve that material.

**Issue 2:** Does the record show the Legislature intended Level 3's additional measures specifically to address a risk of physical/contact harm — or is the record silent — or does it cut the other way (showing the Legislature specifically intended Level 3 to reach non-contact/image-based offenders too)?

**Conclusion:** On the narrow slice of legislative history I could actually reach: **silent**, not adverse and not favorable — the material is simply too general (protecting "the public, especially children") to speak to the contact/non-contact distinction either way, and I could not reach anything more specific. This should be reported to the Writer as an honest gap, not dressed up as either a favorable or unfavorable finding.

**Issue 3:** Has any New York court discussed the purpose behind SORA's *differential notification tiers* specifically — why Level 3 gets broader public notification and more frequent verification than Level 2 — in a way that speaks to what kind of risk that heightened tier is meant to address?

**Conclusion:** Partially. *People v. Gillotti*, 23 N.Y.3d 841 (2014), and the case it cites, *People v. Windham*, 10 N.Y.3d 801 (2008), state the general design principle: "[t]he higher the risk level, the more information is made available, the more widely disseminated, and the longer the registration will last." That explains the *mechanism* (more risk → more notification) but not *why* Level 3 specifically targets contact-type risk. No case I could locate discusses the Level 2/Level 3 notification-tier distinction (as opposed to the RAI's underlying scoring factors) with reference to contact-versus-non-contact offender types.

**Issue 4:** Has any New York court discussed contact-versus-non-contact offender types in explaining what SORA's risk-classification apparatus is *for*?

**Conclusion:** Yes — but the most direct, on-point statement is a **dissent**, not the holding. Judge Smith's dissent in *Gillotti* (joined by Chief Judge Lippman) states that "the language of the sex offender classification rules assigns points to possessors of child pornography for the number of victims and stranger classification in a way that was intended to apply to physical contact and not to defendants who possess and share child pornography, whether or not they are as dangerous as physical offenders," quoting the trial-level decision *People v. Yen*, 33 Misc. 3d 1234(A) (Sup. Ct., N.Y. County 2011). Smith goes further, arguing "[t]he resources that go into the more intensive monitoring of level two and level three SORA registrants can be more usefully expended in keeping track of so-called 'contact offenders' — for example rapists, and abductors of children," and that "the vast majority of [CP consumers] should be classified at level one." The **majority** in *Gillotti* and its companion case *People v. Fazio* rejected the argument that CP offenders should be treated differently for RAI-scoring purposes, reasoning that "the demand for child pornography harms children in part because it drives production, which involves child abuse" — i.e., the majority's own theory for why CP consumption is not categorically different in kind from contact offending for classification purposes.

**Issue 5:** Overall — is the client's hypothesis supportable, unsupportable, or untested/silent on this record?

**Conclusion:** **A hybrid finding, and it should be presented to the client and used in drafting exactly as such.** The legislative-history prong is a genuine, largely unresolved gap caused mostly by access limitations in this environment — neither confirmed nor refuted. The judicial-purpose-discussion prong is **real but currently a minority position at the one court that has squarely confronted a closely related question** (Smith/Lippman dissent, 2 of 7 judges, on RAI factor-scoring — not on the Level 2/Level 3 notification-tier distinction itself, which remains genuinely untested at any level). This is not a "first impression, wide open" finding like some other theories already developed in this matter; it is closer to "a real, quotable, on-point judicial articulation of the client's exact theory, which currently loses on the closest analogous question decided, and has never been tested on the precise question asked." Both halves of that sentence should be given to the Writer, not just the favorable half.

## 3. FACTS

This memorandum relies on facts already established in the Departure Memo and the companion forensic-evaluation memorandum and does not restate them in full.

**Favorable to Respondent:**
- Bernecky's offense conduct is exclusively non-contact/image-based (possession/access of child pornography), with no allegation of contact offending, per the file.
- A sitting New York Court of Appeals judge has articulated, in a signed dissenting opinion joined by the then-Chief Judge, essentially the client's exact theory: that the classification scheme's more-intensive-monitoring provisions were built with contact offenders in mind, not CP possessors.
- The original 1995 sponsor's memorandum, so far as I could reach it, frames SORA's purpose in general public-protection terms without ever mentioning image-based or non-contact offending specifically — meaning the affirmative case for "the Legislature specifically intended Level 3 to reach passive digital offenders too" is not established in what I found either.
- *People v. Johnson*, 11 N.Y.3d 416 (2008), itself recognizes that literal, mechanical application of the RAI's victim-count and relationship factors to CP cases can produce "unfair and anomalous" results — a Court of Appeals acknowledgment (in the majority, not just a dissent) that the instrument does not map cleanly onto this offense category, even though the fix it prescribes is case-by-case downward departure, not exclusion from Level 2/3 architecture.

**Unfavorable to Respondent / limiting facts:**
- The *Gillotti* **majority** — the controlling holding — rejected the CP-offender carve-out argument for RAI purposes, on a demand-drives-production/harm theory that treats CP consumption as causally connected to real-world child abuse.
- The Smith/Lippman position was a two-judge dissent, not a majority; it is persuasive authority a court may find compelling, not binding law.
- Neither *Gillotti* nor any other case I could locate addresses the specific question this assignment asked: the purpose of the *notification-tier differential itself* (Level 3's broader public disclosure, employer-address disclosure, and quarterly verification versus Level 2's) — every case found addresses the RAI's *scoring factors* (points for number of victims, stranger relationship), which is a related but analytically distinct question from why Level 3 carries heavier collateral consequences than Level 2 once a risk level is reached.
- Doe v. Pataki's own description of the original 1995 Act — that it already covered offenses "not involving sexual contact" — is in tension with any claim that the Legislature never contemplated non-contact offenders being swept into SORA's classification apparatus at all.
- Bernecky's Level 3 status arises from an **override** (prior felony sex conviction), not from RAI point accumulation crossing the 110-point threshold — meaning the *Gillotti*/*Fazio*/*Yen* line of authority (which is about RF #3/#7 scoring specifically) is not a direct procedural match to Bernecky's own classification posture; it is available as persuasive/analogical authority on the *underlying purpose question* the departure argument already raises (see Point III of the Departure Memo), not as squarely on-point precedent for an override case.

**Unknown / needs confirmation:**
- Whether the actual 1995 bill jacket, floor debate transcripts, or Governor's approval memorandum — none of which I could reach — say anything more specific about offender-type distinctions than the general sponsor's-memorandum language quoted secondhand in *Doe v. Pataki*.
- Whether the 2002 comprehensive amendment (L. 2002, ch. 11), which appears to be the likely source of § 168-h's current 90-day verification requirement, has its own sponsor's memorandum or committee report addressing why that specific, more-frequent-than-Level-2 obligation was added — I could not locate or reach this document.
- Whether *People v. Yen*, 33 Misc. 3d 1234(A) (Sup. Ct., N.Y. County 2011) — the trial-level decision Judge Smith's dissent relies on — contains additional reasoning on this point beyond the single quoted sentence I was able to find; I could not obtain or read the full Yen opinion.

## 4. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### Issue 1 & 2: The 1995 enactment and subsequent amendment history

**What I found, and how.** *Doe v. Pataki*, 120 F.3d 1263 (2d Cir. 1997), is a Second Circuit decision considering an ex post facto challenge to SORA's retroactive application — precisely the kind of case that requires a court to dig into a statute's legislative purpose (to determine whether it is "punitive" or "regulatory"), which is why it quotes legislative-history material other sources do not. As quoted through a fetch-and-extract tool applied to a FindLaw republication of the opinion (I did not read the raw opinion text myself), the court cites:

- An Assemblyman Feldman bill-jacket statement: "Community notification promotes a state interest in advancing the protection of the public. The public is notified so that they can be [the] 'eyes and ears' [of law enforcement agencies]." *Id.* at 1280 (as extracted).
- A Senator Skelos/Assemblyman Feldman sponsor's memorandum: "Protecting the public, especially children, from sex offenders is a primary governmental interest. The New York statutory plan provided under this bill is predominantly regulatory." *Id.* at 1280 (as extracted).
- On the tiered structure's mechanism: "the greater 'the risk of a repeat offense ... and the threat posed to the public safety,' the greater the extent of the notification." *Id.* at 1274 (as extracted, quoting the statute).
- A description of the three tiers' original notification scope: Level 1 restricted to a 900-number, Level 2 accessible to community entities, Level 3 included in a subdirectory distributed to police. *Id.* at 1275–76 (as extracted).
- A description of the Act's original coverage: 36 offenses "including seven misdemeanors and several crimes not involving sexual contact." *Id.* at 1291 (as extracted).

**Cross-validation.** The Feldman/Skelos "predominantly regulatory" / "protecting the public, especially children" language independently surfaced a second time, through a separate WebSearch synthesis, as quoted inside an unrelated 2009 trial-level opinion, *People v. Meares* (Sup. Ct., cited 2009) — I could not fetch *Meares* itself (blocked), but two independent tool extractions converging on identical sponsor's-memorandum language gives me reasonable confidence the quotation is accurate, even though neither extraction constitutes me reading the primary bill-jacket document itself.

**What this does and does not show.** None of this language distinguishes contact from non-contact offenders, or explains *why* Level 3 specifically (as opposed to Level 2) gets the additional measures now codified at §§ 168-h and 168-l(6)(c). It is general public-protection language of the kind that appears in nearly every regulatory statute's sponsor memo. The one concrete, contestable data point — that the original 1995 Act's registrable-offense list already reached "several crimes not involving sexual contact" — actually cuts mildly *against* the strongest version of the client's hypothesis (that the Legislature never contemplated non-contact offenders being brought within SORA at all), though it says nothing about whether the Legislature specifically intended such offenders to receive Level 3's heightened consequences as opposed to Level 1 or 2.

**The bigger, honest problem: I could not reach the material most likely to actually answer this question.** The New York State Library's legislative bill-jacket collection, the actual Assembly/Senate introducer's memoranda in their original form, floor debate transcripts, and the Governor's approval message for L. 1995, ch. 192 were not reachable through WebSearch or WebFetch in this environment. Nor could I reach anything for L. 2002, ch. 11 — the amendment that (per secondary sources) appears to be the actual origin of § 168-h's current 90-day quarterly-verification structure, and quite possibly of the current form of § 168-l(6)(c)'s employer-disclosure provision as well, neither of which I was able to confirm or date more precisely. **This should be reported to the Writer as an open research task requiring Westlaw/Lexis bill-jacket access or a physical/authenticated visit to the New York State Library's legislative history collection — not as a considered finding that the 2002 amendment's history is silent on this point.** It may well not be; I simply could not get to it.

**Validation status.** Tier (b) — reconstructed from a secondary republication (FindLaw's text of *Doe v. Pataki*) via an automated extraction tool, cross-validated once (the Feldman/Skelos quote, via *Meares*) but not read by me from the original reporter or the bill jacket itself. No citator check performed.

### Issue 3: Judicial discussion of the notification-tier differential's purpose specifically

*People v. Gillotti*, 23 N.Y.3d 841 (2014), quoting *People v. Windham*, 10 N.Y.3d 801 (2008), states SORA's purpose is to "protect the community," and that "[t]he higher the risk level, the more information is made available, the more widely disseminated and the longer the registration will last." I found this exact language independently in two places: a WebFetch extraction of a FindLaw republication of *Gillotti* ("SORA's purpose is not to punish. It is to protect the community"), and — read by me directly and in full — a 2022 practice guide by Thomas P. Franczyk, Mentor-at-Large to the (Erie County) Assigned Counsel Program, which quotes the fuller passage verbatim with its citation to *Windham*: "protect the community. To that end, information is made available to police authorities and to the public, telling them where convicted sex offenders can be found. The higher the risk level, the more information is made available, the more widely disseminated and the longer the registration will last." (Citing *People v. Windham*, 10 N.Y.3d 801 [2008].)

This states the **scaling mechanism** — more assessed risk produces more notification — but it does not explain *why* the Legislature drew the specific lines it drew between Level 2's and Level 3's consequences (subdirectory emphasis, employer-address disclosure, quarterly versus triennial in-person verification), and it does not distinguish contact from non-contact risk in doing so. I found **no case, at any level, that discusses the purpose of the Level 2/Level 3 notification-tier differential specifically with reference to contact-versus-non-contact offender types.** That is a genuine gap in the case law, not merely an access limitation on my part — the cases that exist on the closely related question (discussed below) address the RAI's underlying *scoring factors*, not the *consequences that attach once a level is reached*.

**Validation status.** Tier (b) for the FindLaw extraction of *Gillotti*/*Windham*; tier (a) for the Franczyk practice guide itself, which I downloaded and read in full as a PDF (see Section 7 for why this document, unlike many "pre-existing research documents," is treated as a legitimate secondary source: it is dated, has a named, identifiable human author with a stated institutional role, and cites its own sources throughout — not an anonymous or AI-fingerprint document). No citator check performed on *Gillotti* or *Windham*.

### Issue 4: The client's exact theory, articulated by a New York Court of Appeals dissent

This is the most directly useful finding in this memorandum, and it needs to be presented to the Writer with equal candor about its strength and its limits.

**The majority holding in *Gillotti*.** The Court of Appeals held (1) points may be assessed under RF #3 (number of victims) based on the number of children depicted in CP images; (2) the Board's Position Statement does not bar assessing points under RF #3 and #7 (relationship to victim) in CP cases; and (3) a defendant seeking a downward departure need only prove entitlement by a preponderance of the evidence (not clear and convincing). In its companion case, *People v. Fazio*, decided the same day, the Court rejected the argument that RF #3/#7 points should not apply to CP viewers at all, reasoning — as characterized through a WebFetch extraction — that "images of child pornography essentially depict crimes in progress and the children exploited therein are victimized not only by their in-person abusers but by the lascivious consumers who perpetuate the demand for such material," and separately that "the demand for child pornography harms children in part because it drives production, which involves child abuse" (citing *Paroline v. United States*, the U.S. Supreme Court's CP-restitution decision). **This is the Court of Appeals' own affirmative theory for why CP consumption is not categorically different in kind from contact offending for risk-classification purposes** — a demand/production-harm theory, not a contact/no-contact distinction. Any use of the client's hypothesis in a filing has to confront this head-on; it is the government's obvious, ready rejoinder.

**Judge Smith's dissent (joined by Chief Judge Lippman).** As extracted from FindLaw's republication of the opinion, and independently confirmed in the Franczyk practice guide (which I read in full), Judge Smith wrote:

> "A moment's thought will show that sex offender registration is ill-suited to preventing recidivism by consumers of child pornography. The basic idea of SORA is that, knowing who and where the sex offenders are, people will take precautions to protect themselves and their children from them ... But even if everyone in the world knows who and where a consumer of child pornography is, that will not stop him from downloading pictures from the Internet."

> "The resources that go into the more intensive monitoring of level two and level three SORA registrants can be more usefully expended in keeping track of so-called 'contact offenders' — for example rapists, and abductors of children" — and that "the vast majority of [CP consumers] should be classified at level one."

And, quoting the trial-level decision *People v. Yen*, 33 Misc. 3d 1234(A), 2011 NY Slip Op 52240(U) (Sup. Ct., N.Y. County 2011):

> "[T]he language of the sex offender classification rules assigns points to possessors of child pornography for the number of victims and stranger classification in a way that was intended to apply to physical contact and not to defendants who possess and share child pornography, whether or not they are as dangerous as physical offenders."

This is, essentially word-for-word in substance, the client's own theory — articulated not by a defense advocate but by a sitting New York Court of Appeals judge, joined by the Chief Judge, in a signed opinion. That is real authority the Writer can quote and build on. **It is not, however, the law of New York.** It lost, 5–2, on the question actually presented (whether RF #3/#7 points may be assessed against CP offenders at all). And — the point that must not get lost — even Judge Smith's dissent is about the RAI's *scoring factors*, not about the *purpose of the Level 2/Level 3 notification-tier differential* that this assignment specifically asked about. Smith's "more intensive monitoring of level two and level three registrants... could be more usefully expended [on] contact offenders" sentence is the closest any authority I found comes to that specific question, and it is dictum within a losing dissent on an adjacent question, not a holding on the question asked.

**The Johnson foundation.** *People v. Johnson*, 11 N.Y.3d 416 (2008), the earlier Court of Appeals case both *Gillotti* and *Yen* build on, recognized — in the **majority**, not a dissent — that "reflexively (if not automatically) assigning points under RF#s 3 and 7 may result in an excessive risk calculation" in CP cases, and that this can be "offset... by a discretionary downward departure by the court in appropriate cases" (as characterized in the Franczyk guide, which I read in full, and independently via WebSearch synthesis). This is real, majority-level acknowledgment that the classification instrument does not map cleanly onto CP offenders — but the remedy Johnson itself prescribes is individualized downward departure on the facts of each case, not a categorical exclusion of CP offenders from Level 2/3 consequences, and not a holding about what the Legislature intended when it built the Level 2/Level 3 notification-tier distinction specifically.

**Validation status.** Tier (b) for the *Gillotti* majority, *Fazio*, and *Yen* quotations — reconstructed via FindLaw/WebSearch extraction, cross-validated by a second, independent secondary source (the Franczyk practice guide) that I read in full. That cross-validation across three independently-obtained extractions gives meaningfully higher confidence than a single source would, but it does not substitute for reading the official reporter text myself, which I could not obtain in this environment. *Yen* itself I could not locate or read beyond the single sentence quoted inside *Gillotti*'s dissent (as relayed through secondary sources) — treat that quotation with particular caution; it is twice-removed from the primary source (Yen → Smith's dissent quoting Yen → my secondary-source extraction of Smith's dissent). No citator check performed on any case in this section.

### Issue 5: Overall assessment

Putting the two research avenues together: the **legislative-history** avenue, on what I could reach, is thin and general — it neither confirms nor refutes the client's hypothesis, and a meaningful part of that thinness reflects tool access limitations in this environment rather than a confirmed absence of relevant material (particularly for the 2002 amendment, which I could not investigate at all). The **judicial purpose-discussion** avenue produced a real, on-point, quotable articulation of the client's exact theory — but from a losing dissent on an adjacent question (RAI scoring, not the notification-tier differential itself), which the controlling majority opinion in the same case affirmatively rejected on a competing demand/harm theory that any filing needs to confront directly rather than avoid.

## 5. CONCLUSION

The client's hypothesis is **not supportable as a legislative-history argument** on the material I was able to reach — the record is thin and general, not silent-in-a-way-that-helps or silent-in-a-way-that-hurts, and a fair amount of that thinness is an access gap in this research pass rather than a considered finding about what New York's actual bill jackets say. It **is supportable, with real candor about its limits, as a judicial-purpose argument** — Judge Smith's *Gillotti* dissent (joined by then-Chief Judge Lippman) says almost exactly what the client believes, in almost exactly the client's own words, and is genuine, quotable, on-point authority for the proposition that at least two judges of New York's highest court believe SORA's more-intensive-monitoring provisions were built with contact offenders in mind. But it is a dissent on a different (if closely related) question than the one this assignment asked, the controlling majority rejected the closely related argument on its own competing theory of harm, and no case at any level has squarely decided the specific question posed here — why the Level 2/Level 3 notification-tier differential itself (as opposed to the RAI's scoring factors) exists and whom it is meant to reach.

This is not, on this record, a clean "yes" finding, and it should not be presented to the Writer or the client as one. It is closer to the pattern several other theories in this matter have followed: **real, non-frivolous, genuinely useful persuasive material that has never been squarely tested on the precise question asked, sitting alongside a controlling adverse holding on the closest analogous question that has been decided.** That combination — a real dissent worth quoting, a real majority holding that must be confronted, and a genuine, largely unexplored gap in the specific legislative history that produced §§ 168-h and 168-l(6)(c) — is the honest state of the record.
