## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1 — How is a SORA hearing actually conducted?** Does Correction Law
§ 168-n, and the case law construing it, make a SORA classification hearing
a paper-based proceeding decided on the Board's recommendation package and
the parties' submissions (with "reliable hearsay" admissible), or does a
respondent have a right, on request, to a live evidentiary hearing with
witnesses and cross-examination?

*Summary conclusion:* Paper-based, by both statutory design and recent,
squarely-on-point Court of Appeals authority. The statute's own hearing
language is "[t]he court shall allow the sex offender to appear and be
heard" — nothing more specific about witnesses or confrontation — and the
Court of Appeals held in 2024 that the existing statutory package (notice,
counsel, disclosure, "an opportunity to object and present evidence," and a
clear-and-convincing burden on the People) is itself constitutionally
adequate. Live testimony is not forbidden — *Gillotti* itself included
witnesses the defendant called — but it is not the default, and no authority
found gives a respondent an affirmative right to compel it from the Board or
the DA.

**Issue 2 — What is the specific mechanism for demanding strict proof?** Is
there an affirmative procedural vehicle, beyond the *Mingo* hearsay-reliability
objection already built into Point II, for demanding that the Board/DA prove
disputed facts through live or independently admissible evidence?

*Summary conclusion:* No freestanding vehicle by that name exists in the
statute or in any reported case I found, after a genuinely multi-angle
search. What exists is narrower and more useful: (a) § 168-n(3)'s own
disclosure/adjournment/subpoena mechanism, which reaches *documents* from
state or local record-holders, already invoked in the drafted Demand for
Disclosure; and (b) *Mingo*'s foundation-shifting rule — a specific,
fact-by-fact objection that a given hearsay assertion is not "reliable"
places the burden on the proponent to establish foundation, which *Mingo*
itself says can be done "either through a pre-hearing affirmation, an offer
of proof, or by testimony from a witness with knowledge of the internal
procedures." That is the closest thing to a live-testimony mechanism that
exists in this body of law — and it is the DA's fallback option to save a
challenged fact, not a right the respondent can invoke to put a particular
witness on the stand.

**Issue 3 — What does preservation require?** What must Respondent do on the
record at the September 11 hearing to avoid this argument being found
unpreserved on appeal?

*Summary conclusion:* § 168-n(3) itself routes SORA appeals through "articles
fifty-five, fifty-six and fifty-seven of the civil practice law and rules" —
meaning the operative preservation regime is CPLR 4017/5501, the civil
analog, not CPL 470.05. That regime and general SORA-specific case law both
require the same three things: a timely, clear, fact-specific objection, made
at the point the challenged evidence is actually offered or relied on, that
results in a ruling. A pre-hearing written demand is a strong first step but
should be renewed and pressed live at the hearing when the Case Summary is
actually offered, because *Gillotti* itself shows a general objection that
never specifies its theory is held unpreserved.

**Issue 4 — How does this fit with Points II and III?** Should the "demand
strict proof" theory be a freestanding pre-hearing motion, an argument inside
the existing memorandum, an oral demand preserved by objection at the
hearing, or some combination?

*Summary conclusion:* A combination, weighted toward embedding the demand
inside Point II/III rather than launching it as a separate "full evidentiary
hearing" motion. A standalone motion captioned that way invites a quick,
citable denial under *Watts* and the statute's own text. The stronger,
better-preserved version is a targeted, fact-specific oral demand made at the
hearing itself — tied to the specific unattributed Case Summary conclusions
Point II already challenges and the § 168-l(6) public-safety element Point
III already argues is missing — asking the court to require *Mingo*
foundation for each one and to rule on the record if it declines.

## 3. FACTS

**Favorable to Respondent:**
- Correction Law § 168-n(3)'s own text nowhere promises, and nowhere
  forecloses, live testimony; the operative phrase is "the court shall allow
  the sex offender to appear and be heard," which leaves room to argue the
  point rather than foreclosing it outright.
- *Mingo* itself supplies a real, on-point foundation framework — a
  respondent who specifically challenges a given fact's reliability forces
  the proponent to choose among three named methods of establishing
  foundation, one of which is live witness testimony.
- *Gillotti* shows SORA hearings do sometimes include live witnesses (there,
  offered by the defendant on departure), so a request for testimony is not
  facially incompatible with how a SORA hearing can be run.
- Correction Law § 168-n(3) already gives Respondent a real, usable
  pre-hearing disclosure and adjournment mechanism, already invoked in the
  drafted demand, that is independent of anything discussed in this
  memorandum.

**Unfavorable to Respondent:**
- The Court of Appeals held, in 2024, in *People v. Watts*, that the
  existing statutory safeguards — not anything more — "adequately balance
  the competing private and State interests in these civil proceedings,"
  cutting directly against an argument that due process (or the statute)
  requires anything beyond the paper-based package SORA already provides.
- No reported SORA case was found, after a genuinely thorough,
  multi-term search, granting a respondent's demand for live testimony or
  cross-examination of a case-summary preparer or PSR author as a matter of
  right.
- The general New York civil preservation rule is strict, and the Court of
  Appeals has been notably unwilling in recent years to relax it: the
  "purely legal issue" exception has, per the secondary source discussed
  below, not been applied by the Court of Appeals "once in the past four
  decades."
- SORA hearings are civil, not criminal, and the Sixth Amendment
  Confrontation Clause — by its own terms limited to "criminal
  prosecutions" — is not available as an independent hook for a live-witness
  demand. I did not locate a SORA case squarely so holding, but no authority
  found suggests otherwise, and this is consistent with the well-established
  general rule.

**Unknown — flagged, not assumed:**
- Whether any unreported or lower-court (non-appellate, non-precedential)
  SORA decision has ever granted a live-testimony demand; a Westlaw/Lexis
  full-text search of trial-level orders, which I do not have access to,
  would be needed to rule that out with confidence.
- Whether *People v. Barry* (2d Dep't 2023) — which one search summary
  associated with CPLR 4017/5501 preservation doctrine in a SORA appeal — in
  fact so holds; I could not fetch the opinion (blocked), so I am not
  relying on it for anything beyond flagging it as a lead. See NOT VERIFIED.

## 4. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### Issue 1: How is a SORA classification hearing actually conducted?

**Rule — the statute itself.** I obtained the complete text of Correction
Law § 168-n by pulling and parsing the raw HTML of a legislative-mirror site
myself **(a)** after both `nysenate.gov` and `nycourts.gov` blocked direct
access. Subdivision 3 — the operative hearing-procedure subdivision — reads,
in the portions that matter here:

> "The court shall allow the sex offender to appear and be heard. The state
> shall appear by the district attorney, or his or her designee, who shall
> bear the burden of proving the facts supporting the determinations sought
> by clear and convincing evidence. Where there is a dispute between the
> parties concerning the determinations, the court shall adjourn the hearing
> as necessary to permit the sex offender or the district attorney to obtain
> materials relevant to the determinations from the state board of examiners
> of sex offenders or any state or local facility, hospital, institution,
> office, agency, department or division. Such materials may be obtained by
> subpoena if not voluntarily provided to the requesting party. In making
> the determinations the court shall review any victim's statement and any
> relevant materials and evidence submitted by the sex offender and the
> district attorney and the recommendation and any materials submitted by
> the board, and may consider reliable hearsay evidence submitted by either
> party, provided that it is relevant to the determinations. Facts
> previously proven at trial or elicited at the time of entry of a plea of
> guilty shall be deemed established by clear and convincing evidence and
> shall not be relitigated. . . . Either party may appeal as of right from
> the order pursuant to the provisions of articles fifty-five, fifty-six and
> fifty-seven of the civil practice law and rules."

Two things about this text matter directly to the assignment. First, the
statute's hearing-rights language is "appear and be heard" — not "call
witnesses," "cross-examine," or "confront." The only evidence-gathering
mechanism the statute names is the adjournment/subpoena provision, and it is
expressly limited to "materials" — documents — from "state or local"
record-holders, not testimony from anyone. Second, the appeal provision
routes SORA appeals through the *civil* appellate articles of the CPLR, not
through the Criminal Procedure Law — a detail that matters again in Issue 3
below.

**Case law confirming the paper-based default.** The most directly on-point
authority I found is *People v. Watts*, 2024 NY Slip Op 00926 (Ct. App.),
which I read via an automated-fetch rendering of a case-law mirror,
corroborated against an independent search-result summary that produced the
same quoted language word for word **(b-corroborated)**. *Watts* is not a
case about live testimony specifically — the defendant there argued due
process required a competency examination before his risk-level hearing
could proceed, given his diagnosed schizophrenia — but the Court's reasoning
for rejecting that argument is the reasoning that matters here. The Court
held that:

> "the many safeguards already provided under SORA minimize the risk of
> inaccurate risk-level classification and adequately balance the competing
> private and State interests in these civil proceedings"

and identified those safeguards as "notice, counsel, disclosure of relevant
information, and an opportunity to object and present evidence at a hearing,
at which the People must prove the appropriateness of the classification by
clear and convincing evidence." That is a 2024 statement, from the Court of
Appeals, that the *existing* statutory package — not anything beyond it — is
what due process requires in a SORA proceeding. It is adverse authority to
any argument that a respondent is constitutionally or statutorily entitled to
more process than § 168-n(3) already supplies, and it should be surfaced to
the Court proactively rather than left for the Board or DA to raise, exactly
as the existing memorandum does with *Gunter* on the override question.

*Gillotti* itself is worth flagging on this point too, because it cuts both
ways. Reading it via an automated-fetch rendering **(b-single-source — not
independently corroborated for this specific passage and should be confirmed
before quotation in a filing)**, the Gillotti hearing record included both
documentary submissions ("the Board's RAI, the case summary, and the Air
Force Office of Special Investigations case file") and live evidence
offered by the defendant — "testimony of certain friends and relatives" and
"the report of Dr. David Heffler." That confirms a SORA hearing *can*
include live testimony; New York SORA practice does not categorically bar
it. But it is testimony the *respondent* offered in support of his own
departure application, not testimony compelled from the People's side to
prove a disputed fact — a materially different posture from what this
assignment is asking about.

Consistent with general and well-settled Sixth Amendment doctrine — the
Confrontation Clause applies to "criminal prosecutions" by its own text —
SORA's civil character removes that clause as an independent basis for a
live-testimony demand. I did not locate a SORA case squarely so holding
(this would be worth a five-minute confirming search on a full-text
database), but nothing found suggests the general rule does not apply, and
no party in this body of case law appears to have argued otherwise.

**Conclusion on Issue 1.** SORA classification hearings are, by statutory
design and as confirmed by the Court of Appeals' own 2024 characterization of
what due process requires, ordinarily documentary proceedings decided on the
Board's recommendation package, the parties' written submissions, and
reliable hearsay. Live testimony is not barred, and *Gillotti* shows it does
happen — but as something a respondent affirmatively offers, not something a
respondent can compel from the other side. The honest answer to the client's
underlying premise is that "SORA hearings ordinarily involve no live witness
testimony at all on the People's side" is correct as a description of
ordinary practice, and current Court of Appeals authority (*Watts*) treats
that ordinary practice as adequate process, not as a gap.

### Issue 2: Is there a specific procedural mechanism for demanding strict proof, and has any reported case addressed it?

**What the statute supplies.** As set out in Issue 1, § 168-n(3)'s
disclosure/adjournment/subpoena mechanism is real and already in active use
in the drafted Demand for Disclosure — but it reaches documents, not
testimony, and only from state or local record-holders. It is not, on its
own, a vehicle for compelling anyone to testify.

**What *Mingo* supplies — the actual closest analog.** I went back to
*Mingo* specifically to look for language about *how* a challenged
document's foundation gets established once a respondent successfully
objects to its reliability — a question the existing PSR-reliability memo
addressed at the level of the general "reasonable person would deem it
trustworthy" standard but did not develop on this specific point. Reading
*Mingo* through an automated-fetch rendering of a law-school case-law mirror,
corroborated by an independent search-result summary producing the same
language **(b-corroborated)**, the Court described what a proper foundation
showing for the challenged DA-forms in that case would have required:

> foundation could be established "either through a pre-hearing affirmation,
> an offer of proof, or by testimony from a witness with knowledge of the
> internal procedures"

That is the only place in this body of authority where "testimony from a
witness" appears as part of the operative legal standard for a SORA
hearing's proof. It confirms two things. First, live testimony is a
recognized, available tool inside the *Mingo* framework — a court applying
*Mingo* has express authority to require it, or to accept it, as one of
three ways a document's foundation can be shown. Second, and importantly,
the choice among the three methods belongs to the *proponent* — here, the
Board and the DA — not to the objecting respondent. A respondent's move is
to object to the specific fact's reliability; the *consequence* of a
successful objection, if the proponent wants to save the fact, may be that
the proponent chooses to offer testimony. Respondent does not get to specify
which of the three methods the DA must use, and the DA can just as easily
choose a pre-hearing affirmation or an offer of proof instead. That is worth
being candid about to the client: this is not a lever that puts a witness on
the stand on request; it is a lever that puts the proponent to a choice, one
option of which is testimony.

**Whether any reported case has granted or denied an affirmative demand for
live testimony.** I ran this search from several angles specifically
because the brief instructs me not to stop at the first unproductive term:
"cross-examine" combined with "probation officer," "preparer," and "case
summary"; "subpoena" combined with "witness testify" and "Board of
Examiners"; "full evidentiary hearing" and "trial-type hearing" combined
with "SORA," "denied," and "granted," and run against both Appellate
Division departments by name. None of these searches turned up a reported
SORA decision addressing a respondent's affirmative demand that the Board or
DA produce a live witness, as distinct from the ordinary *Mingo*
hearsay-reliability objection Point II already deploys. That is a genuine
gap in the reported law, not a settled "no" — but it means this specific
theory, framed as its own freestanding demand, would be asking a Monroe
County court to be the first to grant something no appellate authority has
either endorsed or rejected. That is exactly the same posture the existing
memorandum candidly acknowledges for the Point II federal-PSR distinction and
the Point III conjunctive-statute argument, and it should be presented to
this Court the same way — as a genuinely open question, not settled law
being ignored.

**Conclusion on Issue 2.** No freestanding "demand a full evidentiary
hearing" vehicle exists in the statute or in reported SORA case law. What
exists, and is real, is (a) the § 168-n(3) document-disclosure mechanism
already in use, and (b) the *Mingo* foundation framework, which — properly
invoked fact by fact — can force the proponent to choose among affirmation,
offer of proof, or testimony to save a specifically challenged assertion.
The second of these is the actual mechanism this assignment was looking for,
and it already lives inside Point II; it does not need, and should not
receive, a separate free-standing label.

### Issue 3: What does New York preservation doctrine require to preserve this for appeal?

**Which preservation regime actually governs.** Because § 168-n(3) routes
SORA appeals through "articles fifty-five, fifty-six and fifty-seven of the
civil practice law and rules" — read directly by me in the statutory text
quoted in Issue 1 **(a)** — the "CPL 470.05(2) analog" the assignment asked
about is not a loose analogy; it is a specific statutory pointer to the
CPLR's own preservation and appellate-scope provisions. I located and read
those provisions' text through a secondary source — a peer-reviewed-style
law review article on the New York preservation rule, which I downloaded and
extracted with a PDF-text tool myself rather than relying on an automated
summarizer **(a) — full read of the secondary source; the statutory and case
quotations within it are the source's own transcriptions, marked
accordingly below)**. CPLR 4017 provides:

> "At the time a ruling or order of the court is requested or made a party
> shall make known the action which he requests the court to take or, if he
> has not already indicated it, his objection to the action of the court.
> Failure to so make known objections, as prescribed in this section or in
> section 4110-b, may restrict review upon appeal in accordance with
> paragraphs three and four of subdivision (a) of section 5501."

And CPLR 5501(a)(3)–(4) limits appellate review to "any ruling to which the
appellant objected or had no opportunity to object or which was a refusal or
failure to act as requested by the appellant" and "any remark made by the
judge to which the appellant objected." Both quotations are the law review
article's own verbatim reproduction of the statutory text **(b — quote of
statute reproduced by a secondary source, not independently checked by me
against the official CPLR text)**.

The article's own synthesis of what this produces in practice, stated in its
own words and read directly by me:

> "The answer to that question is typically to make an objection (1) timely
> and (2) clearly, and (3) receive an unfavorable ruling."

and, quoting the Court of Appeals' 1899 decision in *Reed v. McCord*:

> "The essential function of an [objection] is to direct the mind of the
> trial justice to the point in which it is supposed that he has erred in
> law so that he may reconsider it and change his ruling if convinced of
> error."

**The "purely legal issue" exception is real but should not be relied on.**
The same article quotes *Bingham v. New York City Transit Authority*, 99
N.Y.2d 355, 359 (2003), for the rule that "[a] new issue — even a pure law
issue — may be reached on appeal only if it could not have been avoided by
factual showings or legal countersteps had it been raised below" **(b — same
secondary-source caveat as above)**. That traces to *Telaro v. Telaro*, 25
N.Y.2d 433, 439 (1969). I searched independently for how the Court of
Appeals has applied this exception recently and found *Sabine v. State of
New York* (2024), which — per two independent search-result summaries using
similar but not identical language, so treated as **(b-single-source,
uncorroborated for exact quotation)** — declined to apply the Telaro
exception and observed that the Court "has rarely applied [it] in this
manner and not once in the past four decades." That is a direct, current
warning against building this argument's preservation on the hope that a
reviewing court will treat it as a "purely legal" question exempt from the
ordinary rule. It should be treated as an unavailable fallback, not a
safety net.

**SORA-specific confirmation that the general rule applies with real
teeth.** *Gillotti* itself — same sourcing caveat as in Issue 1
**(b-single-source)** — illustrates the rule in this exact procedural
context. As to the defendant's factor 7 challenge, the Court held:

> "Since defendant never specifically opposed the People's request for the
> scoring of points under factor 7 on the theory that the assessment of such
> points is inappropriate in a child pornography case, defendant's claim in
> that regard is unpreserved."

That is not a generic civil-litigation preservation holding; it is the
Court of Appeals applying the rule inside a SORA proceeding, to a defendant
who opposed a point score generally but did not articulate the *specific
theory* he later argued on appeal. Independent search results (not
independently confirmed by me against a primary source, so treated as an
unverified lead rather than relied upon for a quotation) describe the same
principle applied to departure requests generally: a party "that had reason
and opportunity to request that the SORA court grant a downward departure
but did not do so has failed to preserve that issue for appellate review."
Both point the same direction: SORA courts and the Court of Appeals expect a
specific, theory-articulated objection made at the hearing, not a general
one, and not one raised for the first time in a post-hearing motion or on
appeal.

**Application to this record.** Putting the general CPLR rule and the
SORA-specific cases together, preserving the "demand strict, independently
admissible proof of every disputed fact beyond the bare 2012 conviction"
argument requires, at minimum:

1. A **specific, fact-by-fact objection** at the hearing — not a generic
   objection that "the Case Summary is unreliable" (which risks the same
   fate as the *Gillotti* factor-7 objection), but one that names the
   particular unattributed conclusions Point II already identifies (the
   "compelling evidence... not captured by scoring alone" sentence, the
   "prior detection, sanction and treatment have not deterred his conduct"
   sentence) and states the theory: that under *Mingo*, these specific
   assertions lack the foundation a federal PSR filtered through the
   Board's own paraphrase would require, and that under § 168-l(6) they are
   the only evidence offered for the independent "threat to public safety"
   finding.
2. A **request that the court actually rule** on that objection — including,
   ideally, asking the court to state whether it is relying on the
   challenged assertions and, if so, on what foundation. CPLR 4017's
   preservation trigger is tied to "the time a ruling or order of the court
   is requested or made" — a pre-hearing letter that is never mentioned
   again at the hearing itself is a weaker record than an objection renewed
   at the moment the Case Summary is actually offered and relied upon.
3. **A denial captured on the record**, so that if the court declines to
   require additional foundation, that refusal — not just Respondent's
   request — is itself preserved for review under CPLR 5501(a)(3)'s "refusal
   or failure to act as requested by the appellant."

**Conclusion on Issue 3.** The pre-hearing written Demand for Disclosure is
a real and useful first step, but on this record it should not be treated as
self-executing preservation. The safer, better-supported course is to renew
the demand specifically and by name at the hearing itself, tied to the exact
facts already identified in Points II and III, and to obtain an express
ruling — because that is what both the general CPLR preservation rule and
*Gillotti*'s own SORA-specific application of it actually require.

### Issue 4: How should this fit with the existing Points II and III, and what should the team actually do?

**Why a freestanding "Demand for a Full Evidentiary Hearing" motion is the
wrong vehicle.** A separate motion captioned that way would present the
Court with an easy, citable basis for summary denial: *Watts* already
describes the existing statutory package as constitutionally adequate, and
nothing in § 168-n(3)'s text promises anything more. Filing a motion framed
around a right SORA does not clearly provide risks two costs beyond the
denial itself. First, it invites the Board/DA to characterize the *entire*
defense position — including the genuinely strong Point II foundation
argument — as an attempt to import criminal-trial procedure into a civil
SORA hearing, which is not what Point II actually argues. Second, it
front-loads a loss on a weak framing before the Court has even reached the
much stronger, fact-specific *Mingo* objection.

**Why embedding the demand inside Point II/III is the right vehicle.**
Point II already argues, candidly and as an extension of *Mingo* rather than
settled law, that the Board's Case Summary — filtered through a federal PSR
and the Board's own further paraphrase — does not automatically qualify for
*Mingo*'s foundation-free treatment. The research in this memorandum adds
the missing operational piece: *Mingo* itself says what happens once that
objection succeeds — the proponent must supply foundation "either through a
pre-hearing affirmation, an offer of proof, or by testimony from a witness
with knowledge of the internal procedures." That sentence should be added to
Point II's existing discussion, and the relief requested at the hearing
should track it precisely: not "grant a full evidentiary hearing," but "as
to each of the following specific, unattributed conclusions the Board draws
from the presentence reports [naming them], require the People to establish
foundation under *Mingo* — by affirmation, offer of proof, or testimony —
before this Court gives them the weight the Board's recommendation depends
on."

That framing does three things simultaneously. It asks for something a
court applying *Mingo* plainly has authority to require, rather than
something *Watts* has already blessed the absence of. It ties directly into
Point III: if the Board cannot establish foundation for the "compelling
evidence... not captured by scoring alone" and "not deterred" conclusions,
those conclusions cannot do the work Point III already argues they cannot
lawfully do even if credited — namely, stand in for the individualized
"threat to public safety" finding § 168-l(6) separately requires. And it
gives the Court an easy, incremental way to grant *something* even while
denying the broader "hearing" framing — precisely the kind of narrower,
grantable relief that also produces a clean, well-preserved denial if the
Court declines it.

**Practical recommendation.** I recommend a three-part combination, not a
single freestanding document:

1. **Keep and press the existing written Demand for Disclosure** as drafted
   — it is grounded in real statutory text and should be filed regardless of
   the rest of this analysis.
2. **Do not file a separate "Demand for a Full Evidentiary Hearing" motion.**
   Instead, add a short subsection to Point II (or a short new paragraph at
   its end) that (a) quotes *Mingo*'s three-method foundation language, (b)
   applies it specifically to the "compelling evidence" and "not deterred"
   sentences already identified in the existing memorandum, and (c) states
   expressly that Respondent requests the Court require the People to
   satisfy that foundation standard as to those specific assertions before
   relying on them, and that Respondent objects to the Court's reliance on
   them absent such a showing.
3. **At the hearing itself, make the oral, fact-specific version of that
   same demand on the record when the Case Summary is offered**, ask
   the Court for an express ruling, and if the ruling is adverse, state
   plainly on the record that Respondent is preserving the objection under
   CPLR 4017/5501 for appellate review, referencing back to the written
   memorandum's Point II framing so the trial and appellate record connect
   cleanly.

This gives the client the specific, correct, on-the-record demand the client
wants preserved, built on the one real doctrinal hook that exists (*Mingo*'s
foundation framework) rather than a broader "right to a trial-type hearing"
theory that current Court of Appeals authority (*Watts*) already forecloses
as a matter of general due process. It is realistic, exactly as the client
expects, that the trial court denies the specific foundation demand and
proceeds on the Case Summary as submitted — but a denial built this way
preserves a genuinely reviewable question (whether the record contains
clear-and-convincing, adequately founded proof of the § 168-l(6) public-safety
element independent of the bare conviction), not a request for a form of
hearing SORA does not promise.

## 5. CONCLUSION

On Issue 1, I have high confidence that SORA hearings are, by design and
current Court of Appeals authority, ordinarily paper-based, and that no
right to compel live testimony from the People exists.

On Issue 2, I have high confidence that no freestanding "demand a full
evidentiary hearing" doctrine exists in reported SORA case law, and moderate
confidence that *Mingo*'s three-method foundation framework is the correct
and only real doctrinal hook for functionally the same result on a
fact-by-fact basis.

On Issue 3, I have high confidence in the general preservation framework
(CPLR 4017/5501, specific-and-ruled-upon objection, Telaro exception
unreliable as a fallback) and moderate-to-high confidence in the *Gillotti*
factor-7 preservation holding specifically, pending independent
confirmation of that exact quotation.

On Issue 4, my practical recommendation — embed the demand in Point II with
a targeted oral renewal and ruling request at the hearing, rather than a
standalone motion — reflects a considered judgment call, not a certainty;
reasonable counsel could instead choose to file a short separate motion
for the tactical value of a standalone appellate record, and I flag that as
a legitimate alternative the Writer and client should weigh, not a closed
question.
