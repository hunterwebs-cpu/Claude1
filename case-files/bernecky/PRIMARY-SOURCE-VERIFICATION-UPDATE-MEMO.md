## SECTION 5: *UNITED STATES V. MIKE* — IDENTITY CONFIRMED, CHARACTERIZATION MUST BE CORRECTED

This is the most consequential finding in this memo for the federal modification-authority track. *Mike* failed independent verification twice before this pass (per `CASES-NEEDED-FOR-VERIFICATION.md`, Priority Item #2). I have now read the full opinion — 632 F.3d 686 (10th Cir. 2011) — directly from the primary text.

**What the Modification Memo needs it for:** Thread III's "silence excludes the subject" theory — the proposition that because Special Condition (a) is silent about employer-owned devices, it does not reach them, supporting Part I.A of the existing motion draft's employer argument. The Modification Memo characterized *Mike* as "the single most factually on-point authority for the precise 'the condition doesn't mention X, so it doesn't reach X' framing," while candidly flagging it had never independently read the opinion.

**What *Mike* actually holds on this point — and it does not support that framing:**

On the computer-monitoring conditions specifically, the Tenth Circuit was faced with the *opposite* argument from the one the Modification Memo needs. The government (not Mike) argued for the narrow, employer-excluding reading:

> "In its response, the Government argues that the conditions 'cannot be read fairly to encompass public computers or computers owned by employers.'" (632 F.3d at 694, quoting Appellee Br. 28.)

**The Tenth Circuit rejected the government's own narrowing argument:**

> "We disagree. Computer monitoring condition two states that '[t]he defendant further understands that he/she will warn others of the existence of the monitoring software placed on his/her computer or any such computer the defendant may have access.' ... Due to the presence of the above language, we find that the probation officer could reasonably believe that the computer monitoring conditions apply not only to computers that Mike owns, but also to those owned by others, including Mike's employer. As a consequence, we remand these conditions to the district court to clarify their scope. If on remand the court decides that it intends for the computer monitoring conditions to apply to computers owned by Mike's employers, it must make the necessary findings..." (632 F.3d at 694-95.)

In other words: *Mike* holds that **open-ended condition language *can* reasonably be read to reach employer-owned property**, not that silence forecloses it. The court remanded for the district court to *decide* the scope and, if it wants to extend to employer devices, make particularized findings — it did not hold that the condition's silence about "employer" excluded employer computers as a matter of construction. If anything, this is the government's playbook, not the defense's, and a court applying *Mike*'s reasoning to Special Condition (a)'s open-ended "computer monitoring policy in effect" language could just as easily conclude the condition can be read to reach a broader scope than the CIMP Agreement's face suggests, subject only to a findings requirement.

**There is a second, genuinely useful holding in *Mike* — but it is a different doctrine.** The "Third Party Notification Condition" required Mike to notify potential employers of his prior convictions. The Tenth Circuit vacated that requirement:

> "Based on our holding in *Souser*, 405 F.3d 1162, Mike contends that to the extent this condition requires him to notify potential employers or educational programs about his criminal convictions, it is infirm because such notification constitutes an occupational restriction and the court did not make the findings required by law. The Government agrees. So do we. As a result, we vacate this condition to the extent it requires such notification. If on remand, the court still wishes to require Mike to notify his employer and/or educational program, it must make the necessary findings." (632 F.3d at 699-700.)

This rests on the **occupational-restriction doctrine** (U.S.S.G. § 5F1.5(a)(1)-(2), via *United States v. Souser*, 405 F.3d 1162 (10th Cir. 2005)) — a particularized-findings requirement before a court (not Probation) may impose a restriction touching employment — not on a textual-silence canon. This is analytically much closer to the *Peterson* line the CIMP Memo and Modification Memo already use for Item 19 (third-party notification) than to a freestanding "the condition doesn't say X" argument.

**What this means for the Writer, concretely:**
1. **Do not cite *Mike* for the proposition that a condition's silence about employer-owned devices excludes them.** That is not what the case holds, and a court or opposing counsel who pulls the opinion will see the government made — and lost — a version of that exact argument, in the other direction.
2. **The narrower, defensible use of *Mike* is the occupational-restriction/particularized-findings point**: any requirement that Bernecky notify an employer, or that extends monitoring/search to an employer's own systems, is the kind of significant-liberty-interest step that (per *Souser*'s reasoning, expressly adopted in *Mike*) must be imposed by the court itself with particularized findings, not left to Probation. This is a real, usable point — it just needs to be reframed away from "silence excludes X" and toward "extending to employer property/notification requires the court's own findings," which dovetails with the CIMP Memo's existing Item 19 analysis under *Peterson*.
3. **The Modification Memo's Thread III recommendation to "lead with *Kunz*/*Peterson* (in-circuit, controlling, already independently confirmed) and use *Mike* as secondary, out-of-circuit reinforcement"** was directionally right for the wrong reason — it should now be followed not just out of caution about an unverified case, but because the verified case does not actually reinforce the silence theory the way it was hoped to.
4. **Mike is also, incidentally, a useful cite for the general delegation-doctrine "significant liberty interest requires particularized findings" proposition** — its holding on inpatient-vs-outpatient delegation (632 F.3d at 695-96) is the same holding *Matta* quotes approvingly (see Section 6 below), and *Mike*'s own text confirms the quotation *Matta* attributes to it.

**Updated confidence:** *Mike* is now (a) — read in full by me, directly, from the primary source. But the substantive characterization needs correction before the Writer relies on it in Part I.A. This item can be marked **CLOSED — CORRECTED**, not simply confirmed.

## SECTION 6: *UNITED STATES V. MATTA* — CONFIRMED

Priority Item #1 (`CASES-NEEDED-FOR-VERIFICATION.md`), failed independent verification across two prior research passes (CIMP Memo and Modification Memo). Now read in full: 777 F.3d 116 (2d Cir. 2015), Second Circuit, decided January 26, 2015. Arises in the **Eastern** District of New York (Judge Irizarry) — this is consistent with how the existing memos use it (as the seminal, circuit-wide delegation-doctrine case, not as a W.D.N.Y.-specific precedent; that distinction belongs to *Kunz*, which the memos correctly keep separate).

**What *Matta* actually holds:** the district court impermissibly delegated to Probation the choice between inpatient and outpatient drug treatment as a condition of supervised release. This is a general delegation-doctrine case; it has nothing to do with computer monitoring specifically, but is cited by *Kunz*, *Browder*, and *Carlineo* for its delegation-doctrine language exactly as the CIMP and Modification Memos describe.

**Every quotation attributed to *Matta* across the CIMP Memo and Modification Memo is CONFIRMED VERBATIM:**

| Quoted language | Memo's pincite | Confirmed at |
|---|---|---|
| "a district court may delegate to a probation officer decisionmaking authority over certain minor details of supervised release" | *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122 | Confirmed, 777 F.3d at 122 (quoting *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 85) |

**Good-law signal:** No negative treatment found; *Matta* is cited approvingly, and its language is directly reproduced, within the primary sources I read (it is quoted at length within its own text, and its "significant liberty interest" framework tracks *Mike*'s verbatim). ## SECTION 7: *PEOPLE V. MOSS* — CONFIRMED, INCLUDING MONROE COUNTY ORIGIN

Priority Item #3. Now read in full: 43 N.Y.3d 584 (2025), N.Y. Court of Appeals, argued Feb. 11, 2025, decided March 20, 2025.

**Monroe County/Fourth Department origin — CONFIRMED, exactly as the Override Memo described:** "APPEAL, by permission of the Court of Appeals, from an order of the Appellate Division of the Supreme Court in the Fourth Judicial Department, entered December 22, 2023. **Holding — CONFIRMED as the Override Memo characterized it:** Dwight Moss had a 2006 felony conviction (course of sexual conduct against a child) that triggered Override 1. A *later* resentencing court, in an *unrelated* 2016 case, found substantial evidence the 2006 plea had been unconstitutionally coerced — but only for the narrow purpose of declining to use it as a sentencing predicate in that other case; the 2006 conviction was never formally vacated or reversed. The Court of Appeals held the override still applies:

> "Given that defendant failed to pursue any procedural pathway to vacate the 2006 conviction, we see no reason to depart from the Guidelines' text stating that the override is triggered if '[t]he offender has a prior felony conviction for a sex crime' (Guidelines, override 1). We therefore apply the Guidelines and hold that the override was properly implemented..." (43 N.Y.3d at 590.)

This confirms the Override Memo's central point: an "undisturbed" prior conviction triggers the override regardless of collateral doubts about its validity, absent a formal vacatur through CPL 440.10 or direct appeal. **Confirmed, quotable, with the correct pincite (43 N.Y.3d at 590, not merely "2025 NY Slip Op 01673" as previously cited without a page number).**

**One nuance the Override Memo did not have, worth flagging for the Writer:** *Moss* also states, in dicta favorable to defendants generally (though not to Bernecky specifically, since there is no dispute about the validity of his 2012 conviction): "Nor did defendant request a downward departure at the SORA hearing based on the conviction's asserted unconstitutionality—a discretionary remedy available under SORA... Indeed, this case's unusual facts would seem to present the type of circumstance that a SORA court could properly consider in deciding whether to depart from an offender's presumptive classification." (43 N.Y.3d at 589, citing *Gillotti*, 23 N.Y.3d at 861.) This confirms the Court of Appeals sees departure, not a validity challenge, as the correct vehicle for override-adjacent arguments — consistent with the Override Memo's own bottom-line recommendation not to build Bernecky's strategy around a validity challenge.

### 10c. *People v. Adams*, 216 A.D.3d 1376 (3d Dep't 2023) — Priority Item #10 — RESOLVED, INTER-MEMO CONFLICT DEFINITIVELY SETTLED

The Base Memo characterized *Adams* as a Kings County case, points/override status uncertain, ending in an **affirmed Level 3**. The Digital-Offense Memo, in a self-described tentative correction, characterized it as **Third Department, Albany County, points-based, resulting in Level 2**.

**Having read the opinion in full, the Digital-Offense Memo's correction is confirmed correct in every particular, and the Base Memo's original characterization is confirmed wrong in every particular:**
- **Court/county:** Third Department, on appeal from **Albany County** (Andra Ackerman, J.) — not Kings County. Confirmed.
- **Basis of presumptive Level 3:** **Points-based, not override.** The Board's own RAI assessed 60 points (presumptive Level 1); the People sought and obtained an additional 60 points under Factors 3 (30 pts, three-or-more victims), 7 (20 pts, stranger relationship), and 12 (10 pts, failure to accept responsibility), for a total of 120 points and a presumptive Level 3. The opinion never mentions an override. Confirmed points-based, not override-based — resolving the uncertainty both prior memos flagged.
- **Outcome:** County Court found the presumptive Level 3 classification correct, but **granted** a downward departure to **Level 2** — crediting "exceptional" treatment participation, no physical contact with victims, no paid CP subscription, and possession of relatively few images — while **denying** further departure to Level 1 because of failure to fully accept responsibility and the young age of the depicted children. The Appellate Division affirmed the Level 2 designation. **This is a granted, not a denied, departure** — the opposite of the Base Memo's original characterization.

**Disposition:** The Base Memo's *Adams* discussion (Section 5.B, Case 2) should be corrected or removed; every detail in it is now confirmed inaccurate. The Digital-Offense Memo's Section 5.B correction should be treated as fully confirmed, and its own residual caveat ("this correction is itself sourced only to WebSearch summaries... tentative") should be lifted — it is now a direct primary-source read.

**Practical significance for Bernecky:** *Adams* is now confirmed as an example of a court **granting** downward departure from a CP-driven presumptive Level 3 down to Level 2 based on absence of contact, absence of a paid subscription, low image volume, and exceptional treatment participation — several of which overlap with facts favorable to Bernecky (no contact, no violence scored). But it remains a points-based case with **no override in play**, so — consistent with the Digital-Offense Memo's own conclusion — it does not resolve whether the same reasoning would move a court to depart from Override 1 specifically.
