**D. The CIMP Participant Agreement — not Special Condition (a)'s bare text — is what actually governs Mr. Bernecky's day-to-day computer and internet use, and five of its provisions either exceed what the Judgment authorizes or require the same narrowing construction the Second Circuit has already given this identical form.**

Since supervision began on or about July 30, 2026, what actually governs Mr. Bernecky's day-to-day computer and internet use is not Special Condition (a)'s text standing alone. It is the "Computer Monitoring Program Participant Agreement" (the "CIMP Agreement") that Probation required him to sign at intake — a twenty-one-item form used across this District's CIMP caseload, the same form the Second Circuit construed on appeal from this same district in *United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748 (2d Cir. 2023). This Part is necessarily an as-applied challenge, not a facial one. The Agreement did not exist, and was never placed before, Judge Larimer at Mr. Bernecky's June 12, 2018 sentencing; it is a document Probation itself drafted and required Mr. Bernecky to sign at intake in 2026 — eight years later — and no part of it appears anywhere in the record the sentencing court actually reviewed. Parts I.A through I.C above challenge Special Condition (a)'s own text, as the sentencing court actually wrote it; this Part challenges something the sentencing court never wrote and never saw at all — how Probation has chosen to implement that text through a form of its own making. *Kunz* supplies the framework for evaluating it. Where a CIMP-form provision is ambiguous, or would demand something absurd read with total literalism, the court construes it narrowly rather than striking it. *See id.* at 762-66 (encryption and alteration-notice clauses). But where a CIMP-form provision cannot be reconciled with the special condition's own text at all, the court holds that Probation "exceed[ed] the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court," and that Probation "may not enforce" the offending provision. *Id.* at 767-68 (single-device restriction). *United States v. Browder* states the same rule in blunter terms: a Probation-drafted provision that "would contradict, rather than execute" the sentence actually pronounced is one "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose." 866 F.3d 504, 514 (2d Cir. 2017). Measured against that framework, five provisions of the Agreement call for the Court's attention, addressed below in the order that reflects how far each strays from what Special Condition (a) and Special Condition (f) actually authorize: Item 21's seizure authority, which fails the framework outright; Item 3's device-authorization gate, which fails it the same way *Kunz* has already held a CIMP clause unenforceable once in this identical program; Items 9 and 17's encryption bans, which *Kunz* has already construed — word for word — and which this motion asks the Court simply to apply; Item 16's blanket security-software ban, an overbreadth problem *Kunz*'s construction never reached; and Item 5's categorical ban on private browsing, an independent *Browder* problem *Kunz* never addressed at all.

Item 21 of the Agreement provides:

> "I shall permit the seizure and removal of hardware and data storage media for further analysis by law enforcement or the probation officer based upon reasonable suspicion that a violation of a condition of supervision or unlawful conduct by any person has or is about to occur."

Special Condition (a) authorizes something narrower in three separate respects, and Item 21 exceeds each of them.

First, the actor. Special Condition (a) authorizes only "the U.S. Probation Office" to install monitoring applications and conduct examinations of Mr. Bernecky's devices. Special Condition (f), the Judgment's general search condition, names no actor at all — it authorizes "a search of [Mr. Bernecky's] person, property, vehicle, place of residence or any other property under his control," without saying who may conduct it — but it, too, reaches only Mr. Bernecky's own property, never a third party's. Item 21 adds an actor neither condition contains: "law enforcement," empowered to seize hardware on its own authority, independent of Probation. Congress made a broader model of exactly that authority available for a sentencing court to adopt, and this sentencing court did not adopt it. 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(23) is a ready-made discretionary condition authorizing a search of a defendant's "computer, other electronic communication or data storage devices or media" "by any law enforcement or probation officer with reasonable suspicion concerning a violation of a condition of probation or unlawful conduct by the person." That language was available in 2018 for the sentencing court simply to adopt as Mr. Bernecky's own special condition. It did not; it wrote Special Condition (a) and Special Condition (f) instead, in narrower language limited to the U.S. Probation Office and to Mr. Bernecky's own property. A form Probation drafted for its own administrative use cannot import, on its own initiative, statutory language Congress made available and the sentencing court did not select for this Judgment.

Second, whose conduct triggers the seizure. Special Condition (a) ties its examination trigger to "impermissible/suspicious activity" on "computer equipment owned or used by the defendant" — Mr. Bernecky's own activity, on his own equipment. Item 21 substitutes "unlawful conduct by any person," meaning a roommate's, a visitor's, or a family member's suspected and wholly unrelated crime becomes Probation's basis for seizing Mr. Bernecky's own hardware. Even the broadest model available to the sentencing court does not go that far: § 3563(b)(23) itself ties its "unlawful conduct" trigger to "the person" — the defendant — not to any person who happens to be present. The Sentencing Guidelines' own recommended search condition confirms the same limit: the model condition the Second Circuit has quoted for exactly this category of offense authorizes a search "upon reasonable suspicion concerning a violation of a condition of supervised release or unlawful conduct by the defendant." U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(7), quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 758-59. Every version of this authority Congress or the Sentencing Commission has actually made available ties the trigger to the defendant's own conduct. Item 21's "any person" trigger appears in no statute, no guideline, and no provision of this Judgment — it is Probation's own addition.

Third, timing. Special Condition (a) speaks of impermissible or suspicious activity "occurring" — something happening, or having already happened — and Special Condition (f) requires "reasonable suspicion," a standard that itself presupposes some conduct has already left a trace to be suspicious of. Item 21 adds a trigger neither condition contains: seizure where a violation or unlawful conduct "is about to occur." That converts what the Judgment authorizes as a monitoring and after-the-fact search tool into a predictive one, authorizing Probation to seize Mr. Bernecky's property on a forecast rather than on anything that has actually happened. Nothing in Special Condition (a) or Special Condition (f) says a word about anticipated conduct, and a form Probation drafted for its own convenience cannot write that authority in where the sentencing court left it out. (One caveat, in the same spirit as this motion's practice elsewhere of disclosing what a cited case does not hold: *Kunz*'s panel never reached whether the Fourth Amendment tolerates an anticipatory, any-person trigger like Item 21's, since counsel there disavowed that challenge at oral argument under *United States v. Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d 173 (2d Cir. 2004); 68 F.4th at 754 n.1. Mr. Bernecky relies on *Kunz* here only for what it actually held — that a CIMP-form provision exceeding the sentencing court's own special-condition text will not be enforced — not for any view on that separate question.)

Item 2 of the Agreement is not the problem, and this motion leaves it untouched:

> "I shall notify the U.S. Probation Office, in advance, of any computer(s), connected device(s), internet capable devices and/or gaming devices I own or have access to. I shall notify the probation officer, in advance, of any additional computer/connected device(s) I acquire or have access to during the course of my supervision."

A notice requirement imposes no gate on what Mr. Bernecky may own; it keeps Probation informed of what exists. Special Condition (a) itself requires exactly this: advance notification "of any computer(s), automated service(s), or connected device(s) that will be used during the term of supervision." Item 2 executes that language faithfully, and Mr. Bernecky has no quarrel with it: he does not object to telling Probation what he owns; he objects to needing Probation's permission before he may own or use it at all.

Item 3 is that different thing:

> "I shall not use, own, or access any device which allows internet access other than what I have been authorized to use by the Probation Office. This includes, but is not limited to, internet appliances, mobile telephones, tablets, electronic games, gaming consoles, blu ray players, and SMART TV's."

It forbids Mr. Bernecky from using, owning, or even accessing any internet-capable device unless Probation has separately authorized that specific device, with no textual standard governing what earns authorization and no limit on what Probation may withhold it for. Special Condition (a) authorizes Probation to install monitoring software on, and examine, devices Mr. Bernecky already owns or uses; it says nothing about Probation deciding, in the first instance and by no stated standard, what he may acquire at all.

That is functionally the same defect the Second Circuit has already struck down in this identical program. *Kunz* confronted a CIMP clause under which Probation could have limited Kunz to a single internet-capable device, and held that it "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court" because the sentencing court's own special-condition text — like Special Condition (a) here — said nothing supporting a Probation-controlled device restriction. 68 F.4th at 767-68. The panel was direct about why: a restriction of that kind "would pose a significant burden on [a supervisee's] liberty, and therefore would need to be imposed by the court and justified by particularized on-the-record findings" — not left to "any special condition granting Probation discretion to decide" the question itself. *Id.* Item 3 is that same vice in a more sweeping form: instead of capping Mr. Bernecky at one device, it lets Probation decide, device by device and by no stated standard, whether he may have any device at all. If a single-device cap requires "an express, case-specific rationale" from the sentencing court before Probation may impose it, *id.*, a blanket authorization requirement covering every device Mr. Bernecky might ever want to use requires no less — and no such rationale appears anywhere in this record.

A fact confirmed only after supervision began shows this is not an abstract concern. Mr. Bernecky's own cell phone — his personal device, on his own carrier plan — now displays, on its lock screen, the message "This device belongs to your organization." That is not custom text the CIMP vendor wrote to assert a property claim; it is text the Android operating system itself displays automatically whenever the software running on a phone has been given the highest level of control Android allows any program to have over a device — the same level Google's own instructions for that kind of software say is meant for equipment a company owns and issues to its employees, not for a person's own personal phone. Google, *Full Device Management*, Android Enterprise Help, https://support.google.com/work/android/answer/9562029 (accessed Aug. 11, 2026). Special Condition (a) authorizes technology that can surveil Mr. Bernecky's device — that installs an application "as necessary to surveil . . . activity" already occurring on it, meaning technology that watches and reports. It does not authorize technology that controls the device. What is actually running on Mr. Bernecky's phone does the latter: it can remotely erase the entire device, block its own removal, and dictate what other software may be installed on it at all — and because that level of access reaches the device's own contents rather than merely observing them, it carries the theoretical capacity to add something to the device, not just watch what is already there. That is not a difference of degree. Surveillance is passive observation; this is active control over the device itself, including the power to alter what is on it. A condition that authorizes only the former should never be read to hand its enforcer the technical means to do the latter. Mr. Bernecky raises this fact for the narrower and better-supported point this entire Part is built on: even where Probation does grant "authorization" under Item 3, the method chosen to implement that authorization has already gone further than surveillance requires, and the device's own operating system is the evidence.

Mr. Bernecky does not ask the Court to leave Probation with no tool to police which devices actually present a monitoring risk, or to require that any CIMP enrollment use no privileged access whatsoever — only that both the gate and the method used to enforce it be tied to what supervision actually requires. Part I.C above proposes a functional line for a related but distinct question: whose devices Special Condition (a)'s monitoring and examination authority reaches at all, once "operated" and "used" are struck. Item 3 poses a narrower question — not whose devices, but which of Mr. Bernecky's own devices need Probation's pre-clearance before he may use them at all — and it calls for its own tailored answer: pre-authorization should attach only to a device that has (i) general-purpose web-browsing capability, or (ii) an application-installation mechanism through which an ordinary user could add general web-browsing, anonymization, or peer-to-peer file-sharing capability — not mere app-installation in the abstract — and that (iii) is compatible with whatever CIMP monitoring software Probation actually uses. The same functional distinction Part I.C illustrates with ordinary household devices does the same work here: a device that connects to the internet but offers no browsing or file-transfer path of its own is not, on this test, a device Item 3 could reasonably gate; a device that does offer one is. And whatever device-management privilege Probation's vendor uses to enroll an authorized device should be no broader than what surveilling that device's activity actually requires — not the organization-ownership tier Android reserves for hardware a company, not an individual supervisee, actually owns.

In exchange for that narrower gate, Mr. Bernecky offers Probation something it does not currently have under the Judgment's own text at all: suspicion-free physical access. Special Condition (a) already conditions physical examination of Mr. Bernecky's devices on a trigger — "as triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity" — meaning Probation's authority to walk in and inspect a device, as the Judgment is actually written, depends on some suspicion already having arisen. Mr. Bernecky proposes to give Probation more than that: he consents to physical inspection of any of his personal devices, on Probation's request, at any time, without any requirement of prior suspicion — a more searching term than Special Condition (a) itself imposes, offered because it makes the narrower device-authorization gate an easy exchange for the Court to approve rather than a net loss to Probation's actual monitoring interest. That consent is necessarily limited to devices within Mr. Bernecky's own legal authority to consent to; it does not extend to a device separately owned by, or under the sole or joint control of, a third party who has not himself consented, since Mr. Bernecky cannot consent away a right that belongs to someone else. *See United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974) (third-party consent to a search is valid only where the consenting party possesses "common authority over or other sufficient relationship to the premises or effects sought to be inspected").

This exchange, not a categorical device rule, is also what the parsimony clause calls for. 18 U.S.C. § 3583(d)(2) requires that any condition impose "no greater deprivation of liberty than is reasonably necessary," and *Kunz* has already recognized what an internet-capable device means to an ordinary life today: such devices are "nearly essential" to modern life. 68 F.4th at 773 (citing *United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d at 96 (2d Cir. 2019)). A rule that lets Probation withhold authorization from any device, on no stated standard, and enroll whatever device it does authorize under a management tier built for hardware someone else owns, is not the narrowly tailored gate § 3583(d)(2) requires; a rule tied to the function that actually matters, paired with Mr. Bernecky's own offer of suspicionless physical access, is.

Items 9 and 17 present a related but distinct problem:

> Item 9: "I understand that I will not use any encrypted email accounts."
> Item 17: "I shall not use any type of encryption unless pre-approved by my probation officer."

This is not new territory. *Kunz* construed its own CIMP paragraph 18 — word for word the same text as Item 17 — and its own paragraph 10 — word for word identical to Item 9. 68 F.4th at 757. Faced with an argument that a literal reading of paragraph 18 would require pre-approval before visiting any ordinary "https" website, the panel agreed a literal reading would be absurd but did not strike the clause. It construed the restriction narrowly and affirmed the condition as construed: "so long as no encryption is initiated by or at the behest of [the supervisee], and so long as any encryption initiated by external forces does not meaningfully impair Probation's ability to monitor [his] behavior . . . we construe the encryption restrictions to allow the reasonable use of services and resources that happen to employ encryption in some way." *Id.* at 764. The panel's textual hook, applied across each of the CIMP clauses it construed this way, was that they personalize the obligation to what the supervisee himself will or will not actively do or "use" — not to a process "entirely outside of his control and of which he himself has no advance notice." *Id.* at 762-64.

Item 9 uses the identical operative verb: Mr. Bernecky "will not use any encrypted email accounts." A court applying *Kunz*'s own reasoning to that identical construction would very likely read it the same way it read paragraph 18 — barring Mr. Bernecky from himself affirmatively choosing an end-to-end-encrypted service to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, not from holding an ordinary Gmail, Outlook, or comparable account that the provider itself encrypts in transit and at rest, invisibly to him, by default. Mr. Bernecky does not ask this Court to hold Item 9 impossible to comply with and therefore void; that framing asks the Court to accept the maximalist literal reading *Kunz*'s own panel already rejected for the sibling clause, and inviting the same rejection here would accomplish nothing he actually needs. What he asks for instead is a written order, entered now, construing Items 9 and 17 the way *Kunz* construed paragraph 18: reaching only encryption Mr. Bernecky himself affirmatively initiates to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, and expressly excluding the ordinary, provider-side transport and at-rest encryption built into mainstream commercial email and web services. (Candor requires noting that *Kunz*'s narrowing discussion never mentions paragraph 10 — the email clause — by name, addressing only paragraphs 7, 16, and 18, *id.* at 762-66; the argument that the same construction governs Item 9 is thus an extension by direct analogy, not an on-point holding, and Mr. Bernecky presents it as exactly that.)

Item 9 also has a gap Item 17 does not. Item 17 already contains its own escape valve — "unless pre-approved by my probation officer" — and Item 9 has none. Even under the narrowed reading requested here, Item 9 as written gives Mr. Bernecky no way ever to seek approval for a legitimate use of an encrypted email service — for instance, if a future employer's IT department required PGP-signed correspondence, or if counsel or the Court itself communicated through an encrypted portal. Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to add the same pre-approval mechanism Item 17 already has to Item 9, so that the narrowly construed prohibition on user-initiated encryption is not an absolute, unwaivable bar with no path to a legitimate use Probation itself might have no objection to.

Item 16 presents a problem *Kunz* never had occasion to construe:

> "I shall not purchase, download, possess and/or install any anti-virus, anti-spyware, firewall, or internet security application without advanced authorization by the U.S. Probation Office and my probation officer."

*Kunz*'s narrowing-construction discussion addresses paragraphs 7, 16, and 18 of its own CIMP form — the notice-before-alteration clause, the "ALL repairs" clause, and the encryption clause. 68 F.4th at 762-66. It does not address paragraph 17, the security-software clause that is Item 16 here. That silence means Item 16 stands on its own, independent of *Kunz*'s treatment of its neighbors.

Read literally, Item 16 forbids something Mr. Bernecky cannot avoid: every modern operating system ships with its own antivirus and firewall software already installed and active by default — Windows Defender on Windows, a comparable built-in firewall on macOS — and a user does not "purchase, download, possess and/or install" what the manufacturer installed before the device ever reached him. Taken at its word, Item 16 would require Mr. Bernecky to seek Probation's advance authorization merely to leave his own operating system's factory-default protections running, or else disable them to comply — a choice that would leave the very device Probation is monitoring more vulnerable to compromise, undermining the government's own interest in reliable, tamper-resistant monitoring rather than serving it. Nothing in Special Condition (a) mentions security software at all; its closest textual hook is the anti-tampering concern for the monitoring software specifically, a concern that runs in the opposite direction from a rule that discourages ordinary computer hygiene.

The problem is not confined to what already runs by default. The same federal government responsible for Mr. Bernecky's supervision affirmatively tells the public, as basic cybersecurity practice, to run exactly the kind of software Item 16 requires him to ask permission before running. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency advises that "[a] reputable antivirus software application is an important protective measure against known malicious threats," that it "can automatically detect, quarantine, and remove various types of malware, such as viruses, worms, and ransomware," and that "CISA recommends that all computers and mobile devices on your home network run antivirus software" — the same guidance that, in the same list of recommended practices, tells users to "[i]nstall a network firewall" and "[i]nstall firewalls on network devices." CISA, *Home Network Security*, https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/home-network-security (accessed Aug. 11, 2026). Item 16 requires Mr. Bernecky to obtain Probation's advance authorization before doing what one arm of the same government that supervises him instructs every American, unconditionally, to do to protect himself. That is not merely a rule that fails to serve the government's own stated interest in a secure, tamper-resistant device; it is a rule that runs backward against a different arm of that same government's own public guidance, forcing Mr. Bernecky to choose between complying with Item 16 and doing the ordinary thing CISA tells everyone else in the country to do without asking anyone's permission first.

Mr. Bernecky does not ask the Court to eliminate Probation's legitimate interest in knowing what security software runs on his monitored devices. He asks the Court to narrow Item 16 to what that interest actually requires: notice to Probation, not its prior authorization, before installing or running any antivirus, anti-spyware, firewall, or other internet security software — the same notice-not-permission line this motion already asks the Court to draw for Item 3's device-authorization gate, discussed above. That every operating system already runs some security software by default only illustrates why a permission requirement is unworkable to begin with; it is not the line the relief requested here is drawn around. Whether the software in question came pre-installed or is something Mr. Bernecky adds himself, Probation's interest is satisfied by knowing it is there — not by holding a veto over whether he may protect his own device before he is allowed to do so.

Item 5 presents a final, independent problem: "While using my monitored device, I shall not use any form of 'in-private' browsing at any time." This clause does not appear among the provisions *Kunz* addressed at all; it stands on *Browder* alone. Special Condition (a) authorizes Probation "to install any application as necessary to surveil all activity on computer(s) or connected device(s)" — meaning the premise of the monitoring software Probation actually deploys is that it captures activity regardless of what browser mode a person happens to use. If that premise holds, Item 5 does nothing the monitoring itself does not already accomplish, and it is surplusage. If it does not hold, that is a fact about the adequacy of the monitoring method Probation has chosen — already addressed in Part I.B above — not a basis for a separate, categorical ban on a mode of browsing that Special Condition (a) never once mentions. Either way, Item 5 does independent work the Judgment never authorized, which is *Browder*'s "contradict, rather than execute" problem in its cleanest form. 866 F.3d at 514.

A narrower, related concern warrants clarification alongside the broader ask. Mr. Bernecky uses DuckDuckGo for ordinary browsing. DuckDuckGo's own description of its protections is limited to blocking third-party trackers and cookies from following his activity across the open web — its stated purpose is to "stop most 3rd-party trackers from loading in the first place." DuckDuckGo, *Web Tracking Protections*, DuckDuckGo Help Pages, https://duckduckgo.com/duckduckgo-help-pages/privacy/web-tracking-protections (accessed Aug. 11, 2026). Nothing in that description claims, or was designed, to hide anything from software running locally on the device itself, and DuckDuckGo's tracker-blocking is always on by default rather than a mode a user switches into or out of the way Item 5's "in-private browsing" describes. Google's own documentation of a genuine private-browsing mode makes the contrast concrete: even an actual Incognito session does not defeat monitoring by whoever controls the device or network it runs on — "organizations that manage your network, like your school, employer, or internet service provider, may be able to observe your activity in Incognito." Google, *Browse in Incognito Mode*, Chrome Help, https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/95464 (accessed Aug. 11, 2026). If a genuine Incognito session cannot defeat monitoring by whoever manages the device, ordinary use of a search engine that only blocks third-party advertising trackers plainly cannot either. Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to confirm that Item 5 reaches only browser-native private or incognito session modes — the kind Chrome, Safari, and Firefox each offer — not ordinary use of a privacy-respecting search engine or browser that hides nothing from the monitoring software already installed on his device.

**Requested relief as to Part I.D:** an order (1) striking "by law enforcement" from Item 21, leaving "the probation officer"; striking "or unlawful conduct by any person," limiting Item 21's trigger to Mr. Bernecky's own conduct; and striking "or is about to occur," limiting Item 21's trigger to a violation or unlawful conduct that has actually occurred; (2) replacing Item 3 with a provision requiring Probation's advance authorization only for a device that has general-purpose web-browsing capability, or an application-installation mechanism through which an ordinary user could add general web-browsing, anonymization, or peer-to-peer file-sharing capability, and that is compatible with the CIMP monitoring software Probation actually uses — coupled with Mr. Bernecky's consent to physical inspection, without prior suspicion, of any device within his own legal authority to consent to, and a limit confining any device-management privilege Probation's vendor uses to enroll an authorized device to no more than what surveilling that device's activity requires; (3) an order construing Items 9 and 17 to reach only encryption Mr. Bernecky himself affirmatively initiates to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, excluding ordinary provider-side transport and at-rest encryption, together with an order adding to Item 9 the same pre-approval mechanism Item 17 already contains; (4) an order narrowing Item 16 to require notice to Probation, not its prior authorization, before installing or running any antivirus, anti-spyware, firewall, or other internet security software, regardless of whether that software came factory-installed on the device or was added by Mr. Bernecky himself; and (5) an order confirming that Item 5 reaches only browser-native private or incognito browsing modes, not ordinary use of a privacy-respecting search engine or browser that does not defeat device-level monitoring.
