# Part I.D — The CIMP Participant Agreement

*Drafted by Vince, integrated into `MOTION-v2.md` as the fourth subpart of Part I (Special Condition (a) — The Computer and Internet Monitoring Program). Standalone copy kept here for reference/review.*

---

**D. The CIMP Participant Agreement — the document that actually governs Mr. Bernecky's supervision, not Special Condition (a)'s bare text alone — imposes at least four restrictions the Judgment does not authorize, in the same way this Circuit has already condemned in this identical program.**

Since Mr. Bernecky's supervision began on or about July 30, 2026, what actually governs his day-to-day computer and internet use is not Special Condition (a)'s text standing alone. It is the "Computer Monitoring Program Participant Agreement" (the "CIMP Agreement") that Probation required him to sign at intake — a twenty-one-item form used across this District's CIMP caseload, the same form the Second Circuit construed on appeal from this same district in *United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748 (2d Cir. 2023). At least four of its items go beyond what Special Condition (a)'s own text, or any statute the sentencing court could have drawn on, actually authorizes. Addressed in turn below: the seizure authority in Item 21; the blanket device-authorization gate in Item 3; the encryption bans in Items 9 and 17; and the private-browsing ban in Item 5.

Item 21 of the CIMP Agreement provides:

> "I shall permit the seizure and removal of hardware and data storage media for further analysis by law enforcement or the probation officer based upon reasonable suspicion that a violation of a condition of supervision or unlawful conduct by any person has or is about to occur."

Special Condition (a) authorizes something narrower in three separate respects, and Item 21 exceeds each of them. First, the actor: Special Condition (a) authorizes only "the U.S. Probation Office" to install monitoring applications and conduct examinations, and Special Condition (f), the Judgment's general search condition, says nothing about "law enforcement" at all — it reaches only Mr. Bernecky's own "person, property, vehicle, place of residence or any other property under his control." Congress knows how to write a condition that reaches both actors: 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(23) is a discretionary condition authorizing search of a defendant's "computer, other electronic communication or data storage devices or media" "by any law enforcement or probation officer with reasonable suspicion concerning a violation of a condition of probation or unlawful conduct by the person." That language was available to the sentencing judge in 2018, and he did not select it — he wrote Special Condition (a) and Special Condition (f) using narrower language limited to Probation and to Mr. Bernecky's own property. A form Probation itself drafted cannot import, on its own authority, the broader statutory language Congress made available and the sentencing court did not choose. That is the identical defect *Kunz* condemned when it held that a CIMP-form provision "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court" wherever the form's own text reaches further than the special condition's actual language, 68 F.4th at 767-68, and it is *Browder*'s rule in blunter terms: a provision that "would contradict, rather than execute" the sentence actually pronounced is one "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose." 866 F.3d at 514.

Second, whose conduct triggers the seizure. Special Condition (a) ties its examination trigger to "impermissible/suspicious activity" on "computer equipment owned or used by the defendant" — Mr. Bernecky's own activity, on his own equipment. Item 21 substitutes "unlawful conduct by any person," meaning a roommate's, a visitor's, or a family member's suspected and wholly unrelated crime becomes Probation's basis for seizing Mr. Bernecky's own hardware. Even Congress's own broadest version of this authority does not go that far: § 3563(b)(23) itself ties its "unlawful conduct" trigger to "the person" — the defendant — not to any person who happens to be present. The Sentencing Guidelines' own model condition confirms the same point: the search condition *Kunz* quotes authorizes seizure "upon reasonable suspicion concerning a violation of a condition of supervised release or unlawful conduct by the defendant." U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(7), quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 758-59. Every version of this authority that Congress or the Commission has actually written ties the trigger to the defendant. Item 21's broader trigger is Probation's own addition.

Third, timing. Special Condition (a) speaks of impermissible or suspicious activity "occurring" — something happening, or already having happened. Item 21 adds a trigger the Judgment contains nowhere: seizure where a violation or unlawful conduct "is about to occur." That converts a monitoring tool into a predictive one, authorizing Probation to seize Mr. Bernecky's property on a forecast rather than on anything that has actually occurred. Nothing in Special Condition (a) or Special Condition (f) says a word about anticipated conduct, and Probation's own form cannot write that authority in where the sentencing court left it out.

One caveat is owed to the Court directly, because this motion has already learned the cost of overstating a holding. *Kunz*'s own counsel expressly disavowed any Fourth Amendment challenge to this exact seizure authority under *United States v. Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d 173 (2d Cir. 2004), at oral argument, and the panel accordingly never addressed whether the Fourth Amendment tolerates an anticipatory, any-person trigger like Item 21's. 68 F.4th at 754 n.1. Mr. Bernecky does not ask this Court to treat *Kunz* as having decided that question. He cites *Kunz* here for what it actually held — that a CIMP-form provision exceeding the sentencing court's own special-condition text will not be enforced — and asks this Court to reach the same result on that ground alone.

Item 2 of the Agreement is not the problem, and this motion leaves it untouched:

> "I shall notify the U.S. Probation Office, in advance, of any computer(s), connected device(s), internet capable devices and/or gaming devices I own or have access to. I shall notify the probation officer, in advance, of any additional computer/connected device(s) I acquire or have access to during the course of my supervision."

A notice requirement imposes no gate on what Mr. Bernecky may own; it keeps Probation informed of what exists. Special Condition (a) itself requires exactly this: advance notification "of any computer(s), automated service(s), or connected device(s) that will be used during the term of supervision." Item 2 executes that language faithfully.

Item 3 is a different animal:

> "I shall not use, own, or access any device which allows internet access other than what I have been authorized to use by the Probation Office. This includes, but is not limited to, internet appliances, mobile telephones, tablets, electronic games, gaming consoles, blu ray players, and SMART TV's."

Notice tells Probation what exists. Item 3 goes further: it forbids Mr. Bernecky from using, owning, or even accessing any internet-capable device unless Probation has separately authorized that specific device, with no textual standard governing what earns authorization and no limit on what Probation may withhold it for. Special Condition (a) authorizes Probation to install monitoring software on, and examine, devices Mr. Bernecky already owns. It says nothing about Probation deciding, in the first instance and by no stated standard, what he may acquire at all.

That is functionally the same defect the Second Circuit already struck down in this identical program. *Kunz* confronted a restriction under which Probation would have limited Kunz to a single internet-capable device, and held that it "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court" because the sentencing court's own special-condition text — like Special Condition (a) here — said nothing supporting a Probation-controlled acquisition gate. 68 F.4th at 767-68. The vice *Kunz* identified was never the number of devices; it was unreviewed, standing Probation discretion over acquisition itself, exercised without any individualized, case-specific finding from the sentencing court. *Id.* Item 3 is that same vice in different packaging: instead of capping Mr. Bernecky at one device, it lets Probation decide, device by device and by no stated standard, whether he may have any device at all. *Kunz* requires "an express, case-specific rationale" from the sentencing court before Probation may exercise that kind of control over what a supervisee may own — not a probation officer's ad hoc, standardless call. *Id.*

Mr. Bernecky does not ask the Court to leave Probation with no tool to police which devices actually present a monitoring risk. He asks the Court to replace Item 3's blanket gate with the narrower rule the actual risk calls for — one that tracks function, not a manufacturer's marketing category, and that does not turn on whether a device can install any application whatsoever, because that test condemns itself the moment it meets a device that can install applications but cannot meaningfully be used to defeat CIMP monitoring. Pre-authorization should attach only to a device that has (i) general-purpose web-browsing capability, or (ii) an application-installation mechanism through which an ordinary user could add general web-browsing, anonymization (Tor or a VPN), or peer-to-peer file-sharing capability — not mere app-installation in the abstract — and that (iii) is compatible with whatever CIMP monitoring software Probation actually uses.

Mr. Bernecky's own Roku television shows why this distinction matters and does real work rather than manufacturing a loophole. Roku's own support documentation states that its devices "provide access to stream video and music from the internet" but do "not provide the ability to browse the internet"; no Roku model, streaming or television, includes a native web browser. Every application in Roku's Channel Store must pass a mandatory certification review — automated static- and behavior-analysis testing that excludes browsers and peer-to-peer or Tor-capable channels before they ever reach a consumer. The only path around that review is "Developer Mode," and Roku's own developer documentation shows that mode is deliberately, not incidentally, walled off from ordinary use: it requires a hidden physical remote-control sequence just to reveal the option, and the device still cannot sideload anything by itself — it requires a second, separately networked computer or phone to push code onto it through a local web interface. An ordinary user, operating the Roku the way it is designed to be used, has no path to a browser, to Tor, or to a peer-to-peer client. Roku accordingly fails prong (ii) — not because it cannot install any application at all, but because no ordinary-user path runs from that limited, curated installation capability to general browsing, anonymization, or file-sharing.

The same distinction does real work throughout an ordinary household, which is the point of proposing it as a rule rather than an exception carved out for one device. A Philips Hue lighting bridge and a connected iRobot Roomba both connect to the internet, and neither has a browser, an open app-installation mechanism, or any general file-transfer capability — both fail prongs (i) and (ii) exactly as the Roku does, and neither should require Probation's advance authorization any more than a lamp or a vacuum would have in 2018. A Samsung Family Hub refrigerator belongs on the other side of the same line, for the same reason: Samsung's own marketing advertises that "you could browse the internet on your refrigerator," and independent review confirms a real touchscreen browser and applications like YouTube. Under the proposed test, the refrigerator requires authorization the same as a laptop would — not because it is, or is not, a refrigerator, but because it actually has the capability the test exists to catch. The line is capability, not category, and testing it against a genuine counter-example is what shows the line does real work rather than simply excusing whatever device Mr. Bernecky happens to want.

In exchange for that narrower gate, Mr. Bernecky offers Probation something it does not currently have under the Judgment's own text at all: suspicion-free physical access. Special Condition (a) already conditions physical examination of Mr. Bernecky's devices on a trigger — "as triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity" — meaning Probation's authority to walk in and inspect a device, as the Judgment is actually written, depends on some suspicion already having arisen. Mr. Bernecky proposes to give Probation more than that: he consents to physical inspection of any of his personal devices, on Probation's request, at any time, without any requirement of prior suspicion — a more searching term than Special Condition (a) itself imposes, offered because it makes the narrower device-authorization gate an easy exchange for the Court to approve rather than a net loss to Probation's actual monitoring interest. That consent is necessarily limited to devices within Mr. Bernecky's own legal authority to consent to; it does not extend to a device separately owned by, or under the sole or joint control of, a third party who has not himself consented, since Mr. Bernecky cannot consent away a right that belongs to someone else. *See United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974) (third-party consent to search is valid only where the consenting party has actual common authority over the item searched).

This exchange, not a categorical device rule, is also what the parsimony clause calls for here. 18 U.S.C. § 3583(d)(2) requires that any condition impose "no greater deprivation of liberty than is reasonably necessary" to serve the statute's purposes, and *Kunz* has already recognized what an internet-capable device means to an ordinary life today — such devices are "nearly essential" to modern life. 68 F.4th at 773 (citing *United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d at 96 (2d Cir. 2019)). That deprivation is larger today, for identical text, than it would have been in 2018: ordinary life now runs through networked devices no one thought to name at sentencing, and the more electronically ubiquitous daily life becomes, the more a categorical permission gate takes from a person, not less. A rule that lets Probation withhold authorization from any device, on no stated standard, is not the narrowly tailored gate § 3583(d)(2) requires; a rule tied to the function that actually matters, paired with Mr. Bernecky's own offer of suspicionless physical access, is.

Items 9 and 17 present a related but distinct problem. Item 17 provides: "I shall not use any type of encryption unless pre-approved by my probation officer." Item 9 provides: "I understand that I will not use any encrypted email accounts." *Kunz* construed its own CIMP paragraph 18 — word for word the same text as Item 17 — and its own paragraph 10 — word for word identical to Item 9. 68 F.4th at 757. Faced with an argument that a literal reading of paragraph 18 would require pre-approval before visiting any ordinary "https" website, the panel agreed a literal reading would be absurd but did not strike the clause. It construed the restriction narrowly and affirmed the condition as construed: "so long as no encryption is initiated by or at the behest of [the supervisee], and so long as any encryption initiated by external forces does not meaningfully impair Probation's ability to monitor [his] behavior . . . we construe the encryption restrictions to allow the reasonable use of services and resources that happen to employ encryption in some way." *Id.* at 764. The panel's textual hook was that the clause personalizes the obligation to what the supervisee himself will or will not "use" — his own affirmative conduct, not "conduct . . . effected by the vendor, invisible to the user." *Id.* at 762-64 & n.5.

Item 9 uses the identical operative verb: Mr. Bernecky "will not use any encrypted email accounts." A court applying *Kunz*'s own reasoning to that identical construction would very likely read it the same way it read paragraph 18 — barring Mr. Bernecky from himself affirmatively choosing an end-to-end-encrypted service to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, not from holding an ordinary Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo, or iCloud account that the provider encrypts in transit and at rest, invisibly to him, by default. Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo Mail, and iCloud Mail all encrypt mail in transit and at rest as a matter of course today, consistent with RFC 3207, RFC 8314, and RFC 8461 — background technical fact offered here, as *Kunz* itself accepted a comparable factual premise about "https" browsing, to support the construction requested, not as an independent ground to strike the clause. Mr. Bernecky does not ask the Court to hold Item 9 impossible to comply with and therefore void; that framing asks the Court to accept the maximalist literal reading *Kunz*'s own panel already rejected for the sibling clause, and inviting the same rejection here would accomplish nothing he actually needs.

What he asks for instead is a written order, entered now, construing Items 9 and 17 the way *Kunz* construed paragraph 18: reaching only encryption Mr. Bernecky himself affirmatively initiates to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, and expressly excluding the ordinary, provider-side transport and at-rest encryption built into mainstream commercial email and web services. Candor requires disclosing that *Kunz*'s substantive discussion never mentions paragraph 10 — the email clause — by name; its narrowing discussion addresses only paragraphs 7, 16, and 18. *Id.* at 762-66. The argument that the same construction should govern Item 9 is an extension by direct analogy to *Kunz*'s treatment of an identical clause in the same Agreement, not an on-point holding, and Mr. Bernecky presents it as exactly that.

Item 9 also has a gap Item 17 does not. Item 17 already contains its own escape valve — "unless pre-approved by my probation officer" — and Item 9 has none. Even under the narrowed reading requested here, Item 9 as written gives Mr. Bernecky no way ever to seek approval for a legitimate use of an encrypted email service — for instance, if a future employer's IT department required PGP-signed correspondence, or if counsel or the Court itself communicated through an encrypted portal. Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to add the same pre-approval mechanism Item 17 already has to Item 9, so that the narrowly construed prohibition on user-initiated encryption is not an absolute, unwaivable bar with no path to a legitimate use Probation itself might have no objection to.

Item 5 presents a final, independent problem: "While using my monitored device, I shall not use any form of 'in-private' browsing at any time." This clause does not appear among the provisions *Kunz* addressed at all; it stands on *Browder* alone. Special Condition (a) authorizes Probation "to install any application as necessary to surveil all activity on computer(s) or connected device(s)" — meaning the premise of the monitoring software Probation actually deploys is that it captures activity regardless of what browser mode a person happens to use. If that premise holds, Item 5 does nothing the monitoring itself does not already accomplish, and is surplusage. If it does not hold, that is a fact about the adequacy of the monitoring method Probation has chosen — already addressed in Part I.B above — not a basis for a separate, categorical ban on a mode of browsing that Special Condition (a) never once mentions. Either way, Item 5 does independent work the Judgment never authorized, which is *Browder*'s "contradict, rather than execute" problem in its cleanest form. 866 F.3d at 514.

A narrower, related concern warrants clarification alongside the broader ask. Mr. Bernecky uses DuckDuckGo for ordinary browsing, which blocks third-party advertising and tracking companies from following his activity across the open web — a protection Google's own Incognito documentation makes clear is a different thing entirely from defeating device-level monitoring, since Google itself warns that "organizations that manage your network, like your school, employer, or internet service provider, may be able to observe your activity in Incognito" even in a genuine private-browsing session. DuckDuckGo's own documentation describes only protections against third-party trackers; it makes no claim, anywhere, of hiding anything from software running locally on the device itself, and its tracker-blocking is always on by default rather than a mode a user switches into or out of. Ordinary use of DuckDuckGo accordingly has no effect on what a device-level CIMP monitoring application can observe. Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to confirm that Item 5 reaches only browser-native private or incognito session modes — the kind Chrome, Safari, and Firefox each offer, and that DuckDuckGo's own one-tap "Fire Button" local data-erase feature approximates if and when actually invoked — not ordinary use of a privacy-respecting search engine or browser that hides nothing from the monitoring software already installed on his device.

**Requested relief as to Part I.D:** an order (1) striking "by law enforcement" from Item 21, leaving "the probation officer"; striking "or unlawful conduct by any person," limiting Item 21's trigger to Mr. Bernecky's own conduct; and striking "or is about to occur," limiting Item 21's trigger to a violation or unlawful conduct that has actually occurred; (2) replacing Item 3 with a provision requiring Probation's advance authorization only for a device that has general-purpose web-browsing capability, or an application-installation mechanism through which an ordinary user could add general web-browsing, anonymization, or peer-to-peer file-sharing capability, and that is compatible with the CIMP monitoring software Probation actually uses — coupled with Mr. Bernecky's consent to physical inspection, without prior suspicion, of any device within his own legal authority to consent to; (3) an order construing Items 9 and 17 to reach only encryption Mr. Bernecky himself affirmatively initiates to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, excluding ordinary provider-side transport and at-rest encryption, together with an order adding to Item 9 the same pre-approval mechanism Item 17 already contains; and (4) an order confirming that Item 5 reaches only browser-native private or incognito browsing modes, not ordinary use of a privacy-respecting search engine or browser that does not defeat device-level monitoring.
