## 3. ISSUE AND SUMMARY CONCLUSION

**Issue:** Have New York courts recognized, as a basis for downward departure — including departure from an automatic override — that a purely digital, possession/distribution-based child-pornography offense does not by itself establish the physical/sexual danger-to-the-community risk that SORA's risk-level and notification scheme is designed to address, such that departure is warranted to avoid over-classifying a non-contact offender under a scheme built around contact-offense risk?

**Summary conclusion:** Yes, in part, and no, in the part that matters most for Bernecky. New York's Court of Appeals in *People v Johnson*, 11 N.Y.3d 416 (2008), and *People v Gillotti*, 23 N.Y.3d 841 (2014), has recognized — as a matter of majority holding, not merely dissent — that reflexively scoring child-pornography offenders under RAI Factors 3 and 7 (factors built around a contact-offense model of "victims" and "relationship with victim") "may result in an excessive risk calculation" and that a SORA court deciding a CP offender's downward-departure application should "give particularly strong consideration to the possibility that adjudicating the offender in accordance with the guidelines point score and without departing downward might lead to an excessive level of registration." Lower New York courts — including the Fourth Department itself, which governs this case — have **actually granted** downward departures to CP-possession offenders on reasoning of this kind: *People v Yen*, 33 Misc 3d 1234(A) (Sup Ct, N.Y. County 2011); *People v Marrero*, 2012 NY Slip Op 22209 (Sup Ct, N.Y. County 2012); and, most importantly, *People v Stagles*, 2023 NY Slip Op 06613 (4th Dep't Dec. 22, 2023). A full two-judge Court of Appeals dissent in *Gillotti* itself (Smith, J., joined by Lippman, C.J.) goes considerably further than the majority, arguing categorically that the RAI's classification rules were "intended by the authors of the guidelines to apply to physical contact, and not to defendants who possessed and shared child pornography," and that "the vast majority" of such offenders "should be classified at level one" — but **this is a dissent, not the holding**, and no majority opinion I located goes that far.

**The gap that matters for Bernecky:** every one of the cases in which this reasoning actually produced a departure **was a points-based case with no override in play** — the offender had no prior felony sex-crime conviction. I conducted a genuinely diligent search specifically for a case combining (a) a CP-type offense, (b) an Override-1-triggering prior felony sex-crime conviction, and (c) a defendant actually arguing the "purely digital, no physical risk" theory as a basis for departure from the override. I found exactly one case with the first two elements — *People v Butler*, 2024 (2d Dep't, Suffolk County, Nov. 13, 2024) — a near-perfect factual analogue to Bernecky (override triggered by a prior sex-crime conviction, instant offense a CP-possession-type offense, defendant reoffended while in treatment/on supervision). Departure was **denied** and Level 3 was affirmed — but the defendant in *Butler*, on the record I could reconstruct, did not raise the specific "non-contact/no physical risk" theory at all; his arguments were family support and treatment completion, both rejected on ordinary *Gunter*-line grounds. **I did not find any case — for or against — where a New York court has actually ruled on this specific theory in the override context.** That is itself the most important, reportable finding of this memo: the theory is a real, doctrinally rooted argument with genuine appellate pedigree in the points-based context and within Bernecky's own governing department, but it is, as far as a diligent search can show, **untested territory at the override level**. Bernecky's papers would be making new law in the Fourth Department, not applying settled law, if this argument is pressed against Override 1.

## 5. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### A. The doctrinal root: *Johnson*, the Board's Position Statement, and *Gillotti*

**Framing the RAI's Factor 3/7 problem for CP offenders.** RAI Factors 3 (number of victims) and 7 (relationship with victim) were written with an in-person offender in mind — someone who abuses one or a small number of known or targeted children. Applied mechanically to a person who has viewed or shared many images of many different, unknown children, those factors will almost always max out (50–60 points) regardless of whether the individual offender presents any elevated contact-offense risk at all. This is the ACP Guide's own framing (which I read directly, in full, from the extracted PDF text — not a paraphrase of a paraphrase): "unlike an in-person offender who may sexually abuse one or more children, most child-pornography consumers who view such conduct remotely invariably will qualify for 50 or 60 points total in these two categories for viewing sexual depictions of 2-3 or more children whom they typically do not know and will never meet."

**People v Johnson, 11 N.Y.3d 416 (2008).** *Sourcing: reconstructed from the ACP Guide (read directly by me) and corroborated by independent WebSearch queries; I was not able to read the Court of Appeals opinion's own text.* Per this reconstruction, the Court held that children depicted in child pornography are "victims" within SORA's meaning, so Factor 7 points based on a stranger relationship are not categorically barred — but the Court also recognized that reflexively assigning those points "may result in an excessive risk calculation," which "can be offset... by a discretionary downward departure by the court in appropriate cases." **This is important and easy to get backward: *Johnson*'s holding runs in favor of the Board being permitted to score Factor 7, not in favor of a defendant avoiding it — the departure "offset" is the safety valve, not a scoring exemption.**

**The Board's Position Statement, and a likely resolution of the Base Memo's open Issue 3.** After *Johnson*, the Board issued a Position Statement — described in the ACP Guide as recognizing that adding Factor 3/7 points in every CP case "can produce an unintended, anomalous result which does not account for differences in individual offenders," and stating the Board would continue to score those points but "would depart from the presumptive risk level, when appropriate" based on factors including the number and nature of images, subscriber status, organization of the material, absence of adult sexual relationships, and use of the images for sexual self-gratification. The Base Memo flagged as an open, unresolved question whether the "June 1, 2012 Position Statement" cited in Bernecky's own Case Summary is this same Factor-3/7 document or a different one addressing override-type dangerousness. **In researching this theory, I came across a strong lead resolving that question**: *People v Marrero*, 2012 NY Slip Op 22209 (Sup Ct, N.Y. County, July 31, 2012), decided at the trial level barely a month after the statement issued, frames its own central question as "an issue of first impression" about **"whether the Board's June 1, 2012 Position Statement changes how child pornography cases must be scored"** under Factors 3 and 7 — the same date, and the same Factor-3/7 subject matter, as the statement Bernecky's Case Summary invokes to support a general "inability to manage his sexual urges" finding. *This is a lead, not a confirmation* — I still have not read the Position Statement's actual text, and I recommend the Writer treat this as strong circumstantial corroboration of the Base Memo's hypothesis (that the Board's citation of this statement for a broad dangerousness finding stretches a document that is actually about Factor 3/7 scoring mechanics), not as settled.

**People v Gillotti, 23 N.Y.3d 841 (2014) — the majority.** *Sourcing: reconstructed primarily from a WebFetch extraction of caselaw.findlaw.com, corroborated by the ACP Guide (read directly) and independent WebSearch queries returning consistent wording across multiple separate queries — the strongest corroboration I was able to achieve for any case in this memo, but still not a direct read of the official reporter text.* Three points from the majority bear directly on this theory:

1. The Court held that "scoring points under factors 3 and 7 may overestimate the risk of reoffense and danger to the public posed by quite a few child pornography offenders," and that "in deciding a child pornography offender's application for a downward departure, a SORA court should, in the exercise of its discretion, give particularly strong consideration to the possibility that adjudicating the offender in accordance with the guidelines point score and without departing downward might lead to an excessive level of registration." **This is the single closest piece of majority, Court-of-Appeals-level language to the client's theory that I located.** It is CP-specific, it is about departure (not just scoring), and it directly acknowledges that following the Guidelines' point score for a CP offender risks over-registering someone whose true risk profile doesn't match a contact offender's.
2. The Court acknowledged genuine scientific uncertainty on the very question the client's theory turns on: "the relationship between an offender's child pornography offenses and the likelihood he or she will commit hands-on offenses is uncertain due to the continuing development of scientific research." This cuts for Bernecky — it forecloses the Board or the People from asserting as settled science that CP possession reliably predicts contact offending.
3. But the majority did **not** adopt the client's theory in its strong form. It reasoned that CP consumption can, "via the consumption of child pornography, encourage others to commit the hands-on sexual abuse needed to produce that material," and that repeat viewing of images of many different children can reflect "an especially compulsive cycle that will make those particular defendants more likely to commit additional psychologically harmful child pornography offenses" — i.e., the majority's theory of harm is (a) demand-side complicity in the abuse that produced the images and (b) risk of *further child-pornography offending*, not necessarily contact offending. **This is a real, citable, adverse data point**: the Court of Appeals has affirmatively rejected the premise that CP consumption is harmless or risk-irrelevant to actual children — it has simply located the harm in continued image-based offending and in contributing to producer-side abuse, not in a prediction of hands-on contact by the viewer. The Writer should not present the "no physical risk" theory to the court as though it were unopposed law; it is a real argument with a real, articulated counter-theory already on the books from the Court of Appeals itself.

**On *Gillotti*'s own facts** (per the ACP Guide, read directly): Gillotti, a 19-year-old found with over 1,000 CP images, had a psychologist testify that "porn-viewers do not have a high rate of engaging in abusive physical contact" — precisely the client's theory, offered through an expert. The trial court nonetheless denied departure, finding aggravating facts (his lack of candor, the volume and content of his collection) outweighed that testimony. The Court of Appeals did not reach the merits of that denial; it remitted only because the Appellate Division had held Gillotti to the wrong (clear-and-convincing) standard. **The upshot: even on facts squarely raising this theory through expert testimony, a New York trial court denying departure on a totality-of-the-circumstances basis has not, on this record, been reversed on the merits by the Court of Appeals** — the remand was about the standard of proof, not about whether the "low physical-contact-rate" evidence should have carried the day.

**The Gillotti dissent — Judge Smith, joined by Chief Judge Lippman.** *Sourcing: WebFetch extraction of findlaw.com and the ACP Guide, both read/extracted directly; corroborated across independent queries.* This is where the client's theory appears in its strongest and most explicit form in any New York opinion I located. The dissent states that "the language of the sex offender classification rules assigns points to possessors of child pornography for the 'number' of victims, and the 'stranger' classification of victims, in a way that was intended by the authors of the guidelines to apply to physical contact, and not to defendants who possessed and shared child pornography, whether or not they are as dangerous as physical offenders" — citing **People v Yen**, discussed below — and argues that community notification "will not necessarily stop that individual from continuing to view child pornography in the privacy of his home," so labeling CP-only offenders at Level 2 or 3 does not serve SORA's protective purpose. The dissent's conclusion: "the vast majority of them should be classified at level one," with registration resources better spent on "contact offenders — for example rapists, and abductors of children."

**This must be characterized accurately and not oversold: it is a two-judge dissent, not a holding, and it carries no binding force.** It is genuinely useful — as a roadmap of the strongest version of this argument, articulated by two Court of Appeals judges including a then-Chief Judge, and as a citation trail to *Yen* — but it should never be quoted to the hearing court as though it states New York law. If cited at all, it should be expressly identified as a dissent and used only to show that the argument has serious judicial pedigree, not that it has been adopted.

### B. Has the theory ever produced an actual departure grant? Yes — but never yet against an override.

**People v Yen, 33 Misc 3d 1234(A), 2011 NY Slip Op 52240(U) (Sup Ct, N.Y. County 2011).** *Sourcing: reconstructed from WebSearch summaries corroborated across multiple independent queries and from its being quoted within the Gillotti dissent (per the findlaw extraction); I was not able to locate or read the trial court's own opinion text, which appears to exist only as an unpublished table decision not indexed on the sources I could reach.* Decided at the trial level, pre-*Gillotti*, this is the origin of the "physical contact... not... possessed and shared" language the Gillotti dissent later quoted. The court reasoned that Factor 3/7 scoring, mechanically applied, would presumptively classify "many possessors of child pornography who are not serious threats to the community" at Level 2, and "anticipated that many SORA applications made as to such defendants should result in downward departures to level one." **Good-law caveat:** *Yen* predates *Gillotti* by three years. *Gillotti*'s later holding that the Board's Position Statement does *not* bar Factor 3/7 scoring is in real tension with *Yen*'s apparent view that such scoring should be avoided or presumptively departed from — *Yen*'s scoring-level reasoning should be treated as substantially superseded by *Gillotti*, while its underlying observation about the guidelines' contact-offense design remains alive (it is, after all, what the *Gillotti* dissent itself relied on in 2014, after *Yen* was decided). It was a trial-level decision in a coordinate county, never binding on Monroe County, and I could not run any citator on it.

**People v Marrero, 2012 NY Slip Op 22209 (Sup Ct, N.Y. County, July 31, 2012).** *Sourcing: WebFetch extraction of caselaw.findlaw.com, corroborated by independent WebSearch.* Also pre-*Gillotti*, also trial-level and non-binding here, but directly useful: the court there **granted** a downward departure to Level 1 (from the People's requested Level 2), following *Yen*'s reasoning and citing it for the identical "physical contact... not... possess and share" language. Marrero's own departure argument — that he viewed the images "because he identified with the pain and abuse the children... had suffered," not for sexual gratification — was rejected as not credible, but the court still departed downward, apparently on the broader Factor 3/7-anomaly reasoning rather than on Marrero's own stated motive. Same good-law caveat as *Yen*: pre-*Gillotti*, and its scoring-avoidance holding is in tension with *Gillotti*'s later rule that Factor 3/7 points are properly assessable.

**People v Stagles, 2023 NY Slip Op 06613 (4th Dep't, Dec. 22, 2023) — the most important supporting case, because it is post-*Gillotti* and in Bernecky's own governing department.** *Sourcing: I read this directly and in full myself, in the form of a case digest inside the Indigent Legal Services Office's "Decisions of Interest" bulletin (PDF downloaded and text-extracted by me) — this is a secondary summary of the actual decision, not the decision's own text, but it is a source I extracted directly rather than one filtered through an AI search summary, and I independently corroborated its citation and core facts through WebSearch.* The Fourth Department **modified** a Cayuga County Court order, reducing a CP-possession offender from Level 2 to Level 1. The digest states: "County Court erred in applying a clear and convincing standard and denying a downward departure. The appellant, who was convicted of possessing child pornography, established by a preponderance of the evidence that there were mitigating factors not adequately accounted for by the guidelines. The court's assessment of 90 points under factors 3 and 7 overestimated his risk of re-offense and danger to the public (see People v Gillotti, 23 NY3d 841, 860 [2014])." The favorable facts credited: he was 19, had no prior criminal record, was never accused of contact sex abuse, was cooperative and admitted guilt readily, never shared the (relatively few) images he possessed, deleted the files months before police contact, and received a probationary sentence.

**Why *Stagles* both helps and sharply limits Bernecky's argument.** It helps because it confirms the theory is not just live but *successful*, recently, in the Fourth Department — the exact court whose law will govern this case on any appeal. It limits Bernecky because **every one of the credited mitigating facts is a fact Bernecky does not have**: Stagles had no prior conviction (no override at all); Bernecky's prior conviction is itself the override trigger. Stagles never shared his images; Bernecky's prior conviction was specifically for *distribution*, and the instant offense followed less than two years after supervision began on that prior conviction. Stagles received probation; Bernecky received 120 months' imprisonment. Stagles's case never implicated a Board finding that "prior detection, sanction and treatment have not deterred his conduct" — Bernecky's does, in the Board's own words. *Stagles* is genuinely useful for the legal proposition (Gillotti's "excessive registration" language applies with real force to CP-possession-only offenders, in this Department, post-*Gillotti*) but it is a weak factual analogue and should not be presented to the court as though its facts resemble Bernecky's — they do not, and the People will say so immediately if it is oversold.

**People v Adams, 2023 NY Slip Op 02813 — a correction to the Base Memo.** The Base Memo flagged uncertainty about whether Adams's presumptive Level 3 arose from an override or from points, and described it as a Kings County case ending in an affirmed Level 3. On further research (WebSearch corroborated across two independent queries), this appears to be **a Third Department case out of Albany County**, points-based (the Board's own RAI recommended 60 points/Level 1; the People sought 120 points/Level 3, presumably through Factor 3/7 scoring), and it appears to have resulted in a **Level 2** designation, not Level 3. I flag this as a correction for the Writer's awareness — it does not change this memo's conclusions, since Adams is points-based either way and not an override case, but the Base Memo's citation of it should not be relied upon without independent confirmation of the corrected details here, which are themselves still secondary-source reconstructions, not a primary-source read.

### C. Has the theory ever reached the override context? One close case — and it did not test the theory.

I searched specifically and repeatedly for a case combining an Override-1 trigger with a CP-type offense and a departure argument resting on the non-contact/no-physical-risk theory. I found one case matching the first two elements:

**People v Butler, 2024 (2d Dep't, Suffolk County, Docket No. 2022-10253, decided Nov. 13, 2024).** *Sourcing: WebFetch extraction of caselaw.findlaw.com, which returned substantive case-specific content (not a generic summary) — the most detailed source I obtained on any single case in this memo, but still not a read of the official reporter text.* The instant offense was possession of "images and videos depicting the sexual abuse of children between the ages of 3 and 10 years old" — a CP-possession-type offense. Override 1 applied based on "a prior conviction of a sex crime." The Second Department affirmed the presumptive Level 3 and the denial of departure. Critically for this memo: **the defendant's departure arguments, as reflected in the opinion, were family support and completion of one sex-offender treatment program — not the non-contact/no-physical-risk theory.** The court's reasoning tracks *Gunter* almost exactly: "the assessment of those points made no difference in the defendant's presumptive risk level, as he was a presumptive level three sex offender pursuant to the automatic override irrespective of the points," and even assuming mitigating factors existed, the totality of the circumstances — commission of the instant offense while on probation, failure to complete prior treatment, and "continued consumption of child pornography while undergoing a course of treatment" — warranted denial. **That last phrase should be read by the Writer as a near-verbatim mirror of Bernecky's own worst fact** (reoffending while actively in phase-two Endeavor treatment) and of the Board's own stated reason for denying departure here ("prior detection, sanction and treatment have not deterred his conduct").

**What *Butler* does and does not establish.** It establishes that this exact fact pattern — override triggered by a CP-type prior, CP-type instant offense, reoffense during treatment/supervision — produces an affirmed Level 3 denial when the departure arguments offered are the ordinary ones (family, treatment). It does **not** establish, one way or the other, what a court would do if the defendant had specifically argued that the digital-only, non-contact nature of both offenses meant SORA's risk-of-physical/sexual-danger rationale doesn't apply to him with the force it applies to a contact offender. That argument was not made, and *Butler* neither accepts nor forecloses it. **I found no case, in either direction, that has actually decided this precise question in the override context.** I searched using varied terms across several sessions within this assignment (override plus CP plus non-contact; override plus CP plus "purely digital"; Fourth Department specifically; a "both prior and instant offense are CP" fact pattern specifically) and did not find one. Given the demonstrated unreliability of unverified AI search summaries documented in the Base Memo, I treat this absence as a genuine, reportable gap rather than a confirmed empty result — but it is a gap found after a real, multi-angle search, not an assumption.

**A tangentially related, but importantly distinguishable, Court of Appeals case.** *People v Brown*, 2023 NY Slip Op 05973 (Nov. 21, 2023), held that applying SORA's sex-offender registration requirement to a defendant who robbed his aunt at gunpoint while his 10-year-old cousin was present — with "no sexual contact or motivation... at all" and no risk of future sexual offense — violated due process, because "where a SORA court expressly finds that there was no sexual conduct or motive and no risk of future sexual offense, application of the sex offender label cannot be justified on the ground that a defendant may pose a risk of future sexual misconduct." This confirms that New York's highest court takes seriously the idea that SORA's sex-offender label must track an actual risk-of-sexual-harm nexus, which lends some general conceptual support to the client's framing. **But it should be flagged clearly and not overstated**: *Brown* is about *initial registerability* (whether SORA applies at all), not risk-*level* classification or departure; the underlying crime there was not a sex offense at all and had no sexual motivation whatsoever, found as fact by the SORA court itself. Bernecky's case presents neither of those features — he has two sexually motivated federal CP convictions, and no court will find (nor should counsel argue) that he "posed no risk of sexual threat at all." Citing *Brown* for more than the general proposition that SORA's sex-offender consequences must be tied to actual risk would invite an easy, sharp distinction from the People.

### D. The two non-conviction incidents: significance to this specific theory

This is worth addressing head-on, exactly because the client's own framing ("no physical/sexual risk") is the framing most likely to draw the People's attention to the 2005/2007 disclosures.

**Admissibility is not in serious doubt.** SORA hearings apply a "reliable hearsay" standard under Correction Law § 168-n(3); *People v Mingo*, 12 N.Y.3d 563 (2009) — *sourced from a WebFetch extraction of a Cornell Law School hosting of the Court of Appeals opinion, which is the closest to a primary-source read I achieved for any single case in this memo, though I still could not fully verify it against the official reporter* — held that hearsay is admissible if "a reasonable person would deem it trustworthy" given the circumstances of its development, and confirmed that the Board's own case summaries and PSRs are within the kinds of proof the People may rely on, including as to conduct for which a defendant was "neither indicted nor convicted." *Mingo* itself did not have occasion to address the specific weight due to self-reported, uncharged conduct from an offender's own adolescence — the opinion, on this extraction, gives no explicit guidance on that narrower question. But its general holding forecloses any argument that the 2005/2007 incidents are simply inadmissible because they never resulted in charges.

**The RAI's own non-scoring is a real, affirmative fact for Bernecky, not a technicality to downplay.** Because Factors 2, 3, and 7 are all zero notwithstanding the narrative's inclusion of these incidents, the Board's own quantitative instrument — not defense argument — is the source of the conclusion that this history did not rise to scorable contact-offense conduct. That is a genuinely strong point and should be made affirmatively, not defensively.

**But *Gillotti*'s third step is discretionary weighing of the totality of circumstances, and an unscored fact can still be an aggravating fact at that step.** Nothing in the authority reviewed here suggests that a fact must be RAI-scored to be considered by a court exercising its discretionary judgment on departure. If the People or the Board's counsel invoke the 2005/2007 incidents to argue that Bernecky is not, in fact, a "purely digital, no-physical-risk" offender — that he has a documented history, however old and however non-adjudicated, of physical proximity to and sexual arousal around actual children — that argument would not be barred by anything located in this research, and the Writer should assume it will be made. The strongest available response is not to contest admissibility but to contextualize: the incidents were self-disclosed (not discovered), occurred when Bernecky was a minor and a teenager rather than as part of the adult offense pattern that is actually before the court, involve no allegation of any overt sexual act by him, and were followed by "no deception indicated" findings that the Board's own polygraph process credited rather than treated as concealment. Whether that reframing will be persuasive to Judge Turner is a judgment call for the Writer and trial counsel, not a legal-research question with a citable answer — I flag it here as a fact issue the theory must be prepared to meet, per this assignment's specific instruction, rather than one the Writer should discover for the first time from opposing counsel.

## 6. CONCLUSION

New York law supports the *existence* of this departure theory — the Court of Appeals majority in *Gillotti*, building on *Johnson*, has said in terms specific to child-pornography offenders that a SORA court should give "particularly strong consideration" to the risk of "excessive" registration when a CP offender seeks downward departure, and the Fourth Department has granted such a departure post-*Gillotti* in *Stagles*. That is a real, citable, majority-opinion foundation — not just the *Gillotti* dissent, though the dissent states the theory in a stronger and more directly on-point form and is worth citing as persuasive (clearly labeled as such).

What New York law does **not** yet show, on a genuinely diligent search, is this theory succeeding — or even being tested — **against an automatic override**. Every departure grant I found rests on facts with no override in play at all. The one case combining a CP-type override with a CP-type instant offense, *Butler*, resulted in an affirmed Level 3 on a fact pattern that echoes Bernecky's most damaging fact (reoffending during treatment) — but without the defendant ever raising this specific theory, so it neither validates nor forecloses it here.

**My confidence level:** Low-to-moderate that this theory, standing alone, would move Judge Turner to depart from Override 1. It is a legitimate, well-grounded legal argument worth making — better grounded, in fact, than a generic treatment or family-support argument, because it has real majority-opinion support specific to CP offenders and a favorable, controlling-department precedent in *Stagles* — but it is being deployed in a context (override, not points; a defendant who reoffended during treatment; a defendant whose prior conviction is the override trigger) that no case I located has tested, and the one closest analogue on facts (*Butler*) went against the defense on other grounds that closely track Bernecky's own weakest facts. This is not a reason to abandon the argument — new arguments succeed in individual cases even without prior precedent, and *Gillotti*'s own "particularly strong consideration" language gives the Writer a genuine doctrinal hook that applies with real force to Bernecky's facts (both offenses are CP-only; no contact conduct is scored anywhere on his RAI). It is a reason not to overstate its likelihood of success, and a reason to pair it with, rather than substitute it for, the other departure arguments already identified in the Base Memo.
