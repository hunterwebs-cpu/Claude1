## ZERO. Scope and method

This is a **fact-verification memo, not a legal-research memo and not an argument**. It does
not draw legal conclusions, does not recommend a litigation strategy, and does not resolve any
disputed fact in either side's favor. Its only purpose is to state, precisely and with page-level
citations, what the primary documents in the Drive folder actually say, and to compare that
against what the Board's Case Summary (transcribed in full in
`SORA-CLASSIFICATION-PACKAGE-TRANSCRIPTION.md`) currently claims.

**Every document below was read by me in full** — either through the Drive tool's native text
extraction (for six of the eight files) or, where that extraction returned only page headers with
no body text (Doc. 23 and Doc. 38, both apparently scanned/flattened images with no embedded
text layer), by downloading the PDF, rendering each page to a 300-dpi image, and running
Tesseract OCR on every page myself. OCR output was legible and internally consistent (correct
docket numbers, page-of-page counters, and signature blocks match the surrounding record), but
OCR is not infallible — a handful of individual characters are flagged below where the text is
genuinely ambiguous (e.g., "5of11" vs. "5 of 11" spacing, an "It is" that OCR rendered as ";"). No
quotation below was taken from a secondary source or from an AI-generated memo; everything
quoted here was read directly off the primary document by me. Nothing in this memo was
"reconstructed" — where I could not verify a fact from a document in this folder, I say so and
flag it as unresolved rather than filling the gap with inference dressed as fact.

## 3. The three flagged discrepancies — findings

### A. The "could not help what he was doing" quote

**What the Case Summary transcription says** (p. 2/photo 170, part of the narrative describing a
"July 7, 2010" interview with special agents): "He stated that he could not help what he was
doing, and he wished he could change it." This sentence appears in the transcription as **indirect
(reported) speech, without quotation marks** — consistent with the Case Summary's style
throughout, which never uses quotation marks anywhere in its four pages.

**What Doc. 24 (6:11-CR-06045, p. 3 of 5) actually says, verbatim, in quotation marks:**

> He indicated that he "**can't help it. I wish I could change it.**" The defendant admitted that he
> masturbated to the images and that he used to "trade a lot". The defendant finally indicated that
> he "tried to slow down" and that while he did not want to get into trouble, he "**can't stop**."

**Finding:** These are almost certainly **the same underlying statement**, not two different
statements. Both sources describe the same event (a 2010 admission about peer-to-peer
downloading, with the same reported age range of victims, "10 to 12 years old" in Doc. 24 versus
"between 10 and 12 years old" in the Case Summary), and both cite back to the same 2011/2012
PSR (Doc. 24 cites "PSR at 70" two sentences later for the YMCA passage; the Case Summary cites
"the Federal Presentence Investigation Report (PSR)" generally). However:

- The Case Summary's rendering is **not a verbatim quotation of anything in this folder**. "Could
  not help what he was doing" is a paraphrase of "can't help it" that changes the antecedent from
  an unspecified "it" to the more pointed "what he was doing" — a subtle but real difference in
  precision, though not, on this record, a difference in substance.
- **"Can't stop" is a separate, distinct quoted statement in Doc. 24** — describing a different
  sentence ("tried to slow down... can't stop") than "can't help it. I wish I could change it." The
  Case Summary's single paraphrased sentence does not clearly capture this second, distinct
  admission at all; it may be folded into "could not help what he was doing" or may simply be
  omitted from the Case Summary's account. I cannot determine which from documents in this
  folder.
- Neither Doc. 24 nor the Case Summary is the PSR itself. **No PSR is in this folder.** I cannot
  confirm that Doc. 24's quotation marks reproduce the PSR's own language exactly, or that the
  Case Summary's paraphrase is drawn from a different, more literal passage of a PSR this folder
  does not contain. Both documents draw on the same underlying 2011-era PSR content (both
  describe the same interview, same subject matter, same approximate victim age range), but I
  cannot verify beyond that.

**Conclusion:** This is not simply "the Case Summary got it wrong" nor "these are two different
interviews." The fairest, most precise statement of the record: **the phrase attributed to Bernecky
in the Case Summary does not appear anywhere in this folder in that exact wording; the closest
verified primary-source language is Doc. 24's "can't help it. I wish I could change it," a real,
verbatim, quoted statement from the same interview the Case Summary is describing, plus a
separate quoted statement ("can't stop") that the Case Summary's paraphrase does not clearly
preserve.**

### B. The YMCA/proximity incidents

**What the Case Summary transcription says** (p. 3/photo 171), within a passage about a **May
2017 sexual-history polygraph re-test** conducted during 2018-case-era treatment:

> During the pretest interview, he listed two individuals with whom he had sexual contact with
> that included a 12-year-old boy when he was 18 years old and a 9-year-old boy when he was 16
> years old. He reported that he worked at a camp for children and while in the day camp bus in
> 2005, the 9-year-old tried to sit on his lap and in 2007, the 12-year-old sat down on his back at
> the after-school program he worked at. He reported that these instances caused him to become
> sexually aroused.

That is: **two specific, dated incidents** — 2005 (bus, 9-year-old, lap) and 2007 (after-school
program, 12-year-old, back) — sourced to a **2017 polygraph pretest interview**.

**What Doc. 24 (6:11-CR-06045, p. 3 of 5, filed January 2012) actually says, verbatim:**

> It should be further noted that the defendant worked at the YMCA from 2004 through his arrest
> in 2010. He worked both as a camp counselor and in the after-school program. He admitted to
> agents that when he was 17, kids at the YMCA would climb on him at the pool and he would get
> sexually aroused because they would rub on him. PSR at 70.

That is: **one generalized, undated admission** — plural "kids," at the "pool" (not a bus, not
specifically the after-school program), age 17 (not 16 or 18), "PSR at 70," describing an admission
made no later than the 2011 PSR (i.e., years before the 2017 polygraph).

**Finding — I decline to resolve this in either direction, per the client's instruction:**

1. **These may be genuinely different incidents, not the same incident described twice.** Doc.
   24's account is a general, recurring pattern ("kids... would climb on him," repeated/ongoing) at
   the pool, self-reported around 2010–2011. The Case Summary's account is two specific, dated,
   named-victim-count incidents (a bus in 2005, an after-school program in 2007 — no pool
   mentioned at all), self-reported during a 2017 polygraph pretest. A camp counselor/after-school
   employee plausibly could have separate memories of separate specific incidents (bus, after-school
   program) in addition to a general pattern at the pool — these are not mutually exclusive on their
   face.
2. **Alternatively, this may be the same underlying set of facts, recalled and reported with
   different levels of specificity at different times** — a general admission in 2010 followed by
   more granular, pointed disclosure under 2017 polygraph-pretest questioning (which is exactly the
   kind of setting designed to extract specific named instances). If so, the "pool" detail and the
   "bus"/"after-school program" detail would be in real tension, since the Case Summary's 2017
   account does not mention a pool at all.
3. **The likely direct source of the Case Summary's two-incident account is the 2018 PSR's
   Paragraphs 79–81** — the paragraphs AFPD Hosken specifically objected to as describing
   "statements before and during multiple polygraph examinations he experienced while in
   treatment" (Doc. 24, 6:18-CR-06018, p. 2, ¶6). That description matches the Case Summary's own
   framing of the bus/after-school passage as sourced to "a sexual history polygraph re-test" almost
   exactly. **This is my inference, not a confirmed fact** — I do not have the 2018 PSR in this
   folder and cannot confirm Paragraphs 79–81 actually contain this specific passage.
4. **The ages given for Bernecky himself do not obviously reconcile on the documents in this
   folder.** Doc. 24 says he was 17 for the pool admission (undated, but tied to the 2004–2010 YMCA
   employment window). The Case Summary says 16 for the 2005 incident and 18 for the 2007
   incident. Per the DOB on the Board's own RAI (6/3/1988), Bernecky would have turned 17 in June
   2005 and 18 in June 2006 — meaning a "2007" incident at "age 18" is chronologically plausible
   only if it occurred before he turned 19 (i.e., before June 2007), and a "2005" incident at "age 16"
   is plausible only if it occurred before he turned 17 in June 2005. Doc. 24's single reported age of
   "17" does not cleanly map onto either of the Case Summary's two specific ages without additional,
   unavailable date detail.

**Neither the 2012 PSR (revised, cited by Doc. 24) nor the 2018 PSR (referenced by AFPD Hosken's
objection to Paragraphs 79–81) is present in this folder. This discrepancy cannot be resolved
without one or both of those PSRs, or the underlying polygraph report(s) themselves. I am not
resolving it in the Board's favor, in Respondent's favor, or at all — I am reporting that the two
primary documents in hand describe materially different particulars (number of incidents,
location, ages, and source/date of the admission) for what may or may not be the same underlying
conduct, and flagging that reconciling this requires documents this folder does not contain.**
