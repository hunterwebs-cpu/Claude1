## I. Introduction

This memorandum was dispatched to verify one discrete, factual/legal premise the client wants built into the Point III (public-safety-element) argument: that denying Jeff a Level 3 classification now, in favor of Level 2, is **not a permanent, irreversible outcome**, because Correction Law § 168-o gives the State a real, statutory mechanism to seek an **upward** modification later if it develops genuine post-classification evidence of dangerousness. The client's own framing (client-directives.md at the "reclassification safety valve" entry) explicitly flagged the exact subsection, standard, and any waiting period as unconfirmed and dispatched this research before the point is drafted into the memorandum of law.

**Scope note — two tracks that must not be conflated.** This matter's existing case file already correctly separates two different post-conviction SORA proceedings: (1) an **offender-initiated downward modification** under § 168-o(2) (the better-known track, and a different mechanism, procedurally and evidentiarily, than what the client is asking about here), and (2) the far less litigated **district-attorney-initiated upward modification** under § 168-o(3), which is what the client's safety-valve argument actually needs. This memorandum focuses on the second track but reports on the first only where necessary to keep the two straight. A third, unrelated track — **upward *departure* at the initial classification hearing itself** under § 168-n(3) — is a different mechanism again (it operates *before* a final classification exists, not after), and several cases this research turned up (*People v. Rodas*, *People v. Cortez*, *People v. Newkirk*, all 2024-25) belong to that third track, not to § 168-o(3). They are noted below only to flag that a careless search will surface them as false positives.

**Source verification note.** The full statutory text of § 168-o quoted below was read directly by me from the New York State Senate's official codified-laws portal (nysenate.gov), which displays "Viewing most recent revision (from 2014-09-22)" as of this fetch (today's date: 2026-08-15) — meaning this is confirmed as the current, most-recently-amended text, not a stale mirror. ## II. Issues and Summary Conclusions

**Issue 1: Does Correction Law § 168-o allow the District Attorney to petition for an *upward* modification of a SORA risk level after the initial determination, and if so, under which subsection?**

**Summary conclusion: Yes.** Subsection 3 of § 168-o expressly authorizes the district attorney — and only the district attorney; not the Board, not the court sua sponte — to file such a petition. Confirmed directly from the primary statutory text.

**Issue 2: What showing/standard does § 168-o(3) require?**

**Summary conclusion:** A conjunctive, two-part showing: (a) the sex offender has either been **convicted of a new crime**, or been the subject of a **determination**, after a CPL § 410.70 or Executive Law § 259-i proceeding, that he **violated a condition** of conditional discharge, probation, parole, or post-release supervision imposed for a "designated crime"; **and** (b) "the conduct underlying the new crime or the violation is of a nature that indicates an increased risk of a repeat sex offense." The DA bears the burden of proving these facts by **clear and convincing evidence** — the identical standard used at the initial classification hearing under § 168-n(3).

**Issue 3: Is there a waiting period, procedural prerequisite, or limitation on the DA's petition?**

**Summary conclusion:** The statute imposes **no explicit waiting period** on the DA's petition (contrast subsection 1's offender relief petition, capped at once every two years, and subsection 2's offender modification petition, capped at once annually — the statute simply says nothing comparable for subsection 3). But the absence of a time bar is not the same as availability on demand: **filing is expressly discretionary, not mandatory, and is gated by the conjunctive predicate above** — the DA cannot petition merely because it has developed a subjective belief in dangerousness; there must be an actual new conviction or an actual adjudicated violation, plus conduct meeting the "increased risk of a repeat sex offense" showing. Subsection 4 supplies the common procedural mechanics for all three petition types (relief, offender modification, DA modification): the court forwards the petition to the Board for an updated recommendation, notice and an opportunity to respond, appointment of counsel for the offender if indigent, a mandatory hearing, and a written order with findings of fact and conclusions of law. If the DA's petition is granted, the offender may appeal as of right.

**Issue 4: Is there NY case law construing § 168-o(3)'s standard for an upward DA-initiated petition specifically?**

**Summary conclusion: Largely no — an honest, material gap.** This research pass located no reported case that I was able to independently read in full, adjudicating a § 168-o(3) DA petition on its merits and construing the "increased risk of a repeat sex offense" element. The reported cases this research surfaced under the "clear and convincing"/"highly probable" gloss for SORA modification proceedings generally (*People v. Stewart*, *People v. Warrior*) were not confirmed as subsection-3-specific rather than subsection-2 cases; *People v. Willis* appears (per secondary-source description only) to be a subsection-2 offender-downward case, not a DA upward case. This should be disclosed as a gap, not papered over — see Section V and the NOT VERIFIED section.

**Issue 5: Have courts or commentators addressed § 168-o as making an initial classification "non-final," or otherwise supporting the point that a lower initial classification doesn't permanently foreclose later public-safety action?**

**Summary conclusion:** No court opinion was found using "non-final" or equivalent language to describe § 168-o's effect. However, a named, identifiable secondary source — a New York State Defenders Association practitioner guide — independently frames § 168-o as creating **three distinct types of post-determination proceedings** (offender relief, offender downward modification, and DA upward modification), which as a matter of the statute's own structure confirms that an initial SORA classification is not a one-shot, permanently fixed outcome; it is expressly revisitable in both directions by statute. This is a sound characterization of the statutory scheme that counsel can make in its own voice — supported directly by the text of §§ 168-o(1)-(3) — but it should not be presented as a quotation of, or attributed to, any judicial opinion, because none was found making that observation as a holding.

## IV. Discussion / Analysis

### A. The statutory text, read directly (Tier (a) — primary source)

Correction Law § 168-o, "Petition for relief or modification," current text (confirmed as the most recent revision, per nysenate.gov, effective since 2014-09-22, no later amendment found or reflected on that page as of this research):

**Subsection 1** (offender petition for full relief from registration): available only to a Level 2 offender not designated a sexual predator, sexually violent offender, or predicate sex offender, registered at least 30 years, by clear and convincing evidence; capped at "not... more than once every two years." **Inapplicable to Jeff** given his predicate-offender designation, and not the mechanism this argument relies on.

**Subsection 2** (offender petition to modify level of notification — the well-known downward track):

> "Any sex offender required to register or verify pursuant to this article may petition the sentencing court or the court which made the determination regarding the level of notification for an order modifying the level of notification. The petition shall set forth the level of notification sought, together with the reasons for seeking such determination. The sex offender shall bear the burden of proving the facts supporting the requested modification by clear and convincing evidence. Such a petition shall not be considered more than annually."

**Subsection 3** (DA petition to modify level of notification — the mechanism this argument needs), quoted in full:

> "The district attorney may file a petition to modify the level of notification for a sex offender with the sentencing court or with the court which made the determination regarding the level of notification, where the sex offender (a) has been convicted of a new crime, or there has been a determination after a proceeding pursuant to section 410.70 of the criminal procedure law or section two hundred fifty-nine-i of the executive law that the sex offender has violated one or more conditions imposed as part of a sentence of a conditional discharge, probation, parole or post-release supervision for a designated crime, and (b) the conduct underlying the new crime or the violation is of a nature that indicates an increased risk of a repeat sex offense. The petition shall set forth the level of notification sought, together with the reasons for seeking such determination. The district attorney shall bear the burden of proving the facts supporting the requested modification, by clear and convincing evidence. In the event that the district attorney's petition is granted, the sex offender may appeal as of right from the order, pursuant to the provisions of articles fifty-five, fifty-six and fifty-seven of the civil practice law and rules..."

**Subsection 4** (shared procedure for petitions under 1, 2, or 3): the court forwards the petition to the Board for an updated recommendation; the offender is entitled to appointed counsel if indigent; where the DA files, the Board must notify the offender and counsel at least 30 days before making its updated recommendation and permit submission of relevant information; the court must, at least 30 days before ruling, provide the updated recommendation to both sides and set a hearing date; the court "may consult with the victim prior to making a determination"; and the court "shall render an order setting forth its determination, and the findings of fact and conclusions of law on which the determination is based."

**No subsection beyond 4 was found in the primary text.** One web-search synthesis (not independently confirmed) suggested a "subsection 3-a" addressing Mental Hygiene Law Article 9/Article 10 (SOMTA) custody and a de novo SORA determination. I could not confirm this against the primary text I read directly, which shows only four subsections as the "most recent revision." This should be treated as **unconfirmed and likely erroneous** unless independently checked against a current McKinney's or the Senate's own current listing before any drafting relies on it — see NOT VERIFIED.

### B. Applying the text to Issues 1–3

**Issue 1 — yes, and specifically subsection 3.** The statutory language is unambiguous: "The district attorney may file a petition to modify the level of notification for a sex offender..." Nothing in subsections 1 or 2 gives the DA an affirmative petition right (subsections 1 and 2 belong to the offender; the DA's only role there is a right to *appeal* a grant). Subsection 3 is the DA's own, dedicated petition mechanism, and by its own terms and by secondary-source description (Section V below), it functions as an upward-only track — nothing in its text authorizes the DA to petition for a *downward* modification, and a practitioner guide confirms this squarely: "Undoubtedly this will be a petition for upward modification... the statute only allows the DA to petition for modification under aggravating, not mitigating circumstances." This is exactly the DA-initiated, post-determination mechanism the client's argument needs.

**Issue 2 — the standard is real, not nominal, and cuts against overclaiming.** The showing required is **conjunctive**: (a) an actual new conviction, or an actual adjudicated violation via the specific statutory proceedings named (CPL § 410.70 or Executive Law § 259-i) — not mere allegation, arrest, or suspicion — **and** (b) that the conduct underlying that new crime or violation "is of a nature that indicates an increased risk of a repeat sex offense." The DA bears the burden by clear and convincing evidence — the same rigorous standard used at Jeff's own upcoming initial hearing. This matters for how the client's argument should be phrased: the statute does **not** let the State reclassify Jeff upward merely because it later "becomes concerned" in the abstract, or because it revisits the same underlying facts already before the court on 9/11/2026. It requires a **new, independent triggering event** — a new offense or an adjudicated supervision violation — plus proof that event itself signals increased dangerousness. That is a real, substantively-gated pathway, not an empty formality, and the memo should present it that way rather than suggesting the State can act on a whim.

**Issue 3 — no waiting period for the DA, but a real gate all the same.** The absence of the annual/biennial caps imposed on the offender's own petitions (subsections 1 and 2) means the DA is not statutorily barred from filing quickly after a triggering event. But "no time bar" is not "unconditional standing" — the conjunctive predicate in subsection 3(a)-(b) is itself the gate, and filing "is discretionary, not mandatory" (per the secondary source discussed below), meaning the DA is never obligated to file even where the predicate exists. Separately, subsection 4's procedural machinery (Board updated recommendation, 30-day notice periods, mandatory hearing, written findings) means a subsection 3 petition is not summary — it replicates much of the initial classification hearing's procedural protections for the offender, including appointed counsel if indigent and an appeal-as-of-right if the petition is granted.

### C. Issue 4 — case law construing subsection 3 specifically (the honest gap)

This is the weakest-sourced part of the research and should be disclosed as such rather than forced. The primary practitioner source used here — Alan Rosenthal, *A Defense Attorney's Guide to SORA Modification Proceedings* (NYSDA, April 2018; Creative Commons-licensed, individually authored, not an anonymous or AI-generated source) — devotes a dedicated section to "Modification – Petition by District Attorney" under subsection 3, confirms the elements and burden above essentially verbatim against the statute, but **does not cite any reported case actually adjudicating a subsection-3 petition on its "increased risk of a repeat sex offense" merits.** Its case citations cluster around subsection 2 (offender-initiated) and around general SORA modification procedure (e.g., that a hearing is mandatory, that a Board recommendation is not binding, that documents must be disclosed):

- *People v. Damato*, 58 A.D.3d 819 (2d Dept. 2009) — cited for the proposition that modification must proceed by petition (not motion), that a hearing is mandatory, and that the court must forward the petition to the Board for a recommendation. Also cited for a distinct and useful holding: where an offender's post-release circumstances change (there, imprisonment following a probation violation), "the DA must petition for modification" — the court cannot reclassify on its own. This directly reinforces that subsection 3 is the *exclusive* vehicle for a court-recognized upward move outside a wholly new classification hearing.
- *People v. Iverson*, 90 A.D.3d 1561 (4th Dept. 2011) — cited (per the secondary source) for a related but distinct point: an offender previously classified who is then convicted of a **new registerable offense** is subject to an entirely **new** classification hearing, without any need for the People to file a § 168-o(3) petition at all. If accurate, this reinforces the client's broader point even more strongly than subsection 3 alone — it means the system has *more than one* mechanism for the State to address newly-arising dangerousness, not just the § 168-o(3) petition route.
- *People v. Lashway*, 25 N.Y.3d 478 (2015) and *People v. McClinton*, 153 A.D.3d 738 (2d Dept. 2017) — cited for disclosure obligations in modification proceedings generally, not specific to subsection 3's substantive standard.
- *People v. Willis*, 130 A.D.3d 1470 (4th Dept. 2015); *People v. Stewart*, 61 A.D.3d 1059 (3d Dept. 2009); *People v. Warrior*, 57 A.D.3d 1471 (4th Dept. 2008) — cited for the general "clear and convincing evidence" standard applicable to SORA modification petitions and its "highly probable" gloss, but not confirmed by me as subsection-3-specific (secondary description of *Willis* suggests it involved denial of an offender's *downward* petition, i.e., subsection 2, not a DA subsection-3 petition).

**None of these were read by me in full from the primary opinion text in this pass.** All are Tier (b) — reconstructed from a secondary source, single-source (the NYSDA guide plus unconfirmed WebSearch synthesis), not independently corroborated by my own reading of the opinions. This is a real gap that must be closed — by actually pulling and reading these opinions, or by finding a squarely-on-point subsection-3 case — before any of them is cited as authority in a filing. I found no case anywhere, in this pass, that reports the outcome of a *litigated, contested* § 168-o(3) petition (i.e., a case where a DA actually invoked subsection 3, post-classification, seeking an upward risk-level change, and an appellate court reviewed the merits). That absence may reflect that such petitions are rare and often unopposed or settled at the trial level without a published opinion — but it should be reported honestly as "not found," not glossed over.

### D. Issue 5 — "non-final"/safety-valve framing

No case opinion was found describing § 168-o, or a SORA classification generally, using "non-final," "provisional," or equivalent language. The client's argument that "denying Level 3 now does not foreclose it forever if genuine cause later develops" is **not** a paraphrase of any judicial holding — it is, and should be presented as, **counsel's own construction of the statute's plain text and structure**: a statute that expressly creates a dedicated, DA-initiated upward petition mechanism, triggered by new post-classification evidence and proved to the same clear-and-convincing standard used at the initial hearing, necessarily means that an initial classification is not the State's only opportunity to protect public safety. That is a defensible, textually grounded argument. It should not be dressed up with a quotation attributed to a court, because none was found making that observation, and inventing one would violate the paraphrase-into-quote discipline this research is built on.

## V. Conclusion

The core premise the client wants to argue is **legally sound and supported by the primary statutory text**, read directly: Correction Law § 168-o(3) does give the District Attorney a genuine, real, and specifically-tailored mechanism to petition for an upward modification of Jeff's risk level after the fact, if it later develops a new conviction or an adjudicated supervision violation demonstrating increased dangerousness, proved by clear and convincing evidence. Denying Level 3 in favor of Level 2 now is therefore not a one-shot, permanently foreclosing outcome — a lower initial classification leaves the State's ability to protect public safety later fully intact, conditioned on real evidence rather than the bare fact of the existing prior conviction already before the court.

That conclusion should be argued **carefully and precisely**, not expansively:
- Do not suggest the DA can act on demand or on mere reconsideration of the same facts — the statute requires a genuinely new triggering event (new conviction or adjudicated violation) plus a showing that its own conduct indicates increased risk.
- Do not attribute the "not a permanent/final decision" framing to any court — no case was found making that observation as a holding; it is counsel's own sound reading of the statutory structure and should be presented in that voice.
- Do not cite *Willis*, *Stewart*, *Warrior*, *Damato*, *Iverson*, *Lashway*, or *McClinton* for any point beyond what is stated above without first reading each opinion directly and independently confirming its subsection, holding, and pincite — all are currently Tier (b) only.

On balance, this is a favorable, usable point for the memorandum — but it is a **structural/textual argument about what the statute permits**, not a case-law-proven point, and it should be drafted accordingly.
