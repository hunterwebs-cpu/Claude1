## 1. Chapter 2 — The Rules of the Road (pp. 30–63)

This is the book's foundational chapter: eleven rules of plain, forceful composition, aimed
squarely at lawyers. Stark's framing throughout is that legal readers today — judges,
clients, other lawyers — read the way everyone else reads on a screen: fast, distracted,
and unwilling to do work the writer should have done for them.

**Rule 1 — Use strong verbs (prefer active voice).** Stark's benchmark: good writers use
the passive voice around 25% of the time; lawyers use it "at least half the time," law
professors closer to 90%. The passive lets a writer hide the actor ("This action was
filed..."), which both weakens prose and can literally omit information the reader needs.
Two legal-specific reasons to avoid it: (1) legal writing is a form of *giving directions*
(to clients, to courts), and directions are naturally active ("Take I-95..."); (2) legal
argument, like advertising, is a form of persuasion, and persuasive rhetoric is almost
always active ("Just Do It," "Ask not what your country can do for you..."). Exception: the
passive is genuinely useful when you want the reader to focus on the object of an action
rather than the actor (a defense lawyer writing "the gun was fired" rather than "Smith
fired the gun"), or when concealing the actor is your actual goal (a use he flags as
usually illegitimate — "sanitizes and institutionalizes lawyers['] writing," quoting Judge
Patricia Wald, p. 34). Corollary: also cut weak verbs ("seems," "appears," "is") and the
generic passive ("It is widely known...").

**Rule 2 — Eliminate legal jargon.** Stark's test: read the sentence aloud in a McDonald's;
if a stranger wouldn't understand it, it's too far from ordinary language. Targets:
archaic verbiage ("heretofore," "said document"), unnecessary Latin (*infra*, *supra*,
*arguendo* — "just give the page number"), and lawyer-only idioms ("in the instant case,"
"on all fours"). He singles out law students and pro se prisoners as the worst offenders,
because unfamiliarity with jargon makes people overuse it to signal membership.

**Rule 3 — Write clearly and simply, keep things short.** Built around Zinsser's *On
Writing Well* categories, broken into six sub-techniques:
- **(a) Eliminate redundancies** ("accident occurred at twelve noon" — noon is always
  twelve; "armed gunman" — gunmen are armed by definition).
- **(b) Substitute a word for a phrase** ("because" for "due to the fact that," "under" for
  "pursuant to," "if" for "in the event that," etc.) — Stark traces this verbosity to the
  historical practice of scriveners paid by the word.
- **(c) Avoid euphemisms** ("owl site center" for a nest; "unlawful or arbitrary
  deprivation of life" for killing) — this is a form of Orwellian dishonesty, not just
  clutter.
- **(d) Eliminate unnecessary adjectives and adverbs.** Adjectives are frequently
  ambiguous ("it was a cold day" means different things in Boston and Palm Springs) —
  replace with a concrete fact ("twenty-three degrees"). Adverbs of intensification
  ("totally," "clearly," "certainly") tend to *weaken* rather than strengthen an argument.
  Judge John Minor Wisdom: "The adjective is the enemy of the noun; the adverb is the enemy
  of the verb" (p. 40).
- **(e) Eliminate transition words** ("therefore," "thus," "furthermore") — if the writing
  is logically ordered, these aren't needed, and Zinsser's point that they rob the reader of
  the pleasure of discovering the logic themselves (p. 42).
- **(f) Keep things short.** Elmore Leonard's rule — "I try to leave out the parts that
  people skip" (p. 43) — applies doubly online, where readers' attention collapses after
  about the first forty words on a screen. If you must include marginal material for
  completeness, push it to the back of the document rather than mixing it through.

**Rule 4 — Don't hedge; if you must hedge, explain why.** Hedging words ("seems,"
"apparently," "maybe") have a real place for drafters of contracts, legislation, and
pleadings (people reading *against* you looking for gaps), but elsewhere they read as
paranoia. Key mechanical fix: when you do hedge an assertion, *always* follow with a clause
explaining the source of the doubt — this both clarifies for the reader and forces you, as
the writer, to hedge less often because you have to justify each hedge. Separately: opinions
can be qualified, but *facts* should not be — conflating the two ("Apparently we're in New
York") undermines the writer's authority.

**Rule 5 — Keep sentences to twenty-five words or less.** The more complex the underlying
information, the shorter the sentence should be. Illustrated with a real 376-word sentence
from a corporate merger disclosure as the cautionary example (p. 46).

**Rule 6 — Move subordinate clauses to the beginning or end of a sentence, not the
middle.** "Jane, before leaving for the weekend, filed the complaint" buries the S-V-O
structure; "Before leaving for the weekend, Jane filed the complaint" doesn't. When a
clause truly cannot be moved (because it defines the subject), set it off with dashes
rather than commas — dashes stop a distracted reader more effectively.

**Rule 7 — Write for your readers, not for yourself.** Don't narrate your own thinking
("I think that this is wrong" — just write "This is wrong"). Avoid throat-clearing openers
("It is clearly the case that," "My sense is that"). Avoid stream-of-consciousness
structure that takes the reader through your research/planning process rather than to the
conclusion.

**Rule 8 — Use specific imagery, not vague generalities.** Concrete, vivid substitution
makes prose memorable — modeled on ad copywriting (David Ogilvy's Rolls-Royce line: "At
sixty miles an hour, the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric
clock," p. 50) and on Holmes's "falsely shouting fire in a theater." Caveat: use this
technique *sparingly* — Stark cites a Justice Stevens concurrence that overreached into
strained metaphor ("the foliage of the First Amendment...its roots were germinated") as a
cautionary example of the same tool misused.

**Rule 9 — Understated prose is the strongest form of expression.** Restraint reads as
credibility; overstatement reads as insecurity. Stark coins "the Rex Ryan School of Written
Advocacy" for lawyers (he notes, "especially...many male litigators") who think hyperbole
and personal attacks on the other side are persuasive — they aren't; they just make the
writer look "egomaniacal." Quoting Orwell: "The great enemy of clear language is
insincerity" (p. 51).

**Rule 10 (Tenth Rule) — Use simple language, favoring Anglo-Saxon-derived words over
Latinate/French ones.** Simple, short, native-English words carry more force ("help" over
"assist/aid"; "give up" vs. Churchill's deliberate, jarring choice of the French-derived
"surrender" to draw a contrast). A 2011 empirical study (Sean Flammer, cited via Wayne
Schiess) found judges rated a plain-English version of a legal argument more persuasive
than the legalese version 60%–34% overall, and federal appellate judges 77%–23% (p. 56) —
concrete data supporting the plain-language push.

**Rule 11 (Eleventh Rule) — Use the tools of the New Age (format for on-screen/mobile
readers).** Two client complaints Stark says he hears constantly: (1) documents aren't
organized for functional use — a 15-page single-spaced memo is useless if you need an
answer *in the moment* (e.g., mid-hearing); (2) documents don't focus on what the reader
actually needs (the practical bottom line), because lawyers are trained to explain the
"why" (legal reasoning) when clients and judges mostly want the "what to do." Formatting
fixes modeled on MapQuest/Wikipedia: use the second person, provide visual structure
(boxes, different fonts, numbered lists rather than essay prose), lead with the conclusion,
use hyperlinks where the medium allows it (with real judge/arbitrator testimonials about
liking hyperlinked briefs, p. 60–61).

## 2. Chapter 4 — The Art of Argument (pp. 77–92)

**Framing:** Argument is the core of all legal writing, even non-litigation writing, because
lawyers are constantly trying to persuade someone of something. Stark reports interviewing
judges who told him the biggest problem with the submissions they read isn't inaccuracy —
it's that both sides' briefs are so unpersuasive the judge ends up "perplexed" rather than
convinced either way (p. 78).

**Why precedent-based argument alone under-persuades:** Outside the courtroom, nobody finds
"we've always done it this way" convincing (we live in a culture "obsessed with the
current"). Citing Justice William O. Douglas's account of Chief Justice Hughes: "At the
constitutional level where we work, ninety percent of any decision is emotional. The
rational part of us supplies the reasons for supporting our predilections" (p. 79). Prior
case law functions less as *proof* and more as *evidence supporting a theory* the writer
has already independently identified.

**Method: "Outline the argument, research it later."** Do only enough initial research
(never more than an hour) to get the general shape of the law, then set the research aside
and ask: if I had to explain to a judge, without citing any authority, why we should win,
what would I say? Write that down first. Only *then* go back and do full research — you'll
usually find the case law supports your original intuition (because cases stand for
principles, and principles are what you should be reasoning from), and it's far more
efficient than the reverse process of accumulating fifty pages of research and trying to
distill it down to one (p. 80).

### The Four Principles of Advertising Lawyers Should Know (pp. 82–87)

1. **Before you begin writing, ask yourself "What's the big idea?"** Force yourself to
   state your position in one or two sentences (what ad theorist Rosser Reeves called the
   "unique selling proposition"). Advocates who are afraid to commit to an emphasis end up
   emphasizing nothing — "Miller Lite: tastes great, less filling" persuades; a lawyer's
   version ("tastes great, less filling, comes in an easy-opening can, cheaper than Bud...")
   would not. **Concrete allocation rule**: if you're presenting five arguments, the lead
   argument should be roughly two-thirds of the brief, the second about a fifth, and the
   remaining three should split the last eighth or tenth (p. 83).
2. **Devise a one- or two-sentence slogan that embodies your position.** Examples: Laurence
   Tribe's Hawaii Housing Authority brief opening ("The issue in this case is whether the
   United States Constitution freezes our fiftieth state into its feudal past"); Alexander
   Bickel's Pentagon Papers line ("A prior restraint...stops more speech, more effectively.
   A criminal statute chills. The prior restraint freezes"). Two sub-rules: the slogan should
   *explain why* (unlike pure ad taglines, legal slogans can't just be memorable — "Reverse
   Miranda — Just do it" wouldn't work in the Supreme Court); and memorable taglines tend to
   share features — pithiness, alliteration, strong verbs, concrete images, rhythmic
   repetition, short Anglo-Saxon words (drawing on Heath & Heath's *Made to Stick*: simplicity,
   unexpectedness, concreteness, credibility, emotion, stories).
3. **Make it interesting.** "You can't bore a reader into buying a product, you can only
   interest him or her in buying it" (Madison Avenue maxim, p. 86). The law itself is
   usually boring to judges — they've heard the same legal arguments hundreds of times
   ("familiarity builds apathy," ad theorist Bill Bernbach). The *facts* are where you have
   room to be genuinely new and interesting, because in most cases, once judges have read
   the facts, "their minds are made up" (quoting Judge Patricia Wald, p. 86).
4. **Study the product and the industry you're "advertising."** Legal research rarely
   turns up anything genuinely new (and if it does, a judge bound by precedent will likely
   discount it); *factual* research is where real discovery and differentiation happen.
   Good advocates behave like investigative reporters, using discovery to surface concrete,
   telling detail — illustrated with Philip Meyer's account of *Williams v. Taylor* counsel
   digging up a specific, graphic childhood social-services report rather than simply
   labeling the client's background "traumatic" (pp. 87–89).

### The Three Beginning Principles of Argument Writing (pp. 89–92)

1. **Facts often make the argument; arguments don't often make the facts.** Facts matter
   more than lawyers assume: they come first (first impressions are strong), they appeal
   legitimately to a judge's sense of equity, and — crucially — judges are far more
   dependent on *your* rendering of the facts than on your version of the law, since they
   consider themselves the legal experts already. Old maxim: "If you've got the facts,
   pound the facts. If you've got the law, pound the law. If you've got neither, pound the
   table" (p. 90).
2. **Be accurate.** Beyond citation-checking, this is about not stretching the truth on
   facts or characterizations — a conscientious judge who catches you doing so will
   discount everything else you say, and "it's usually the end of the case" (p. 92).
3. **Don't overargue.** Not every sentence needs to land a blow; conceding minor points
   increases credibility. Quoting Judge Frank Coffin: he is unduly swayed by a brief that
   "contains not one pejorative adjective or innuendo concerning one's opponent or the trial
   judge" (p. 92).

## 3. Chapter 5 — The Role of Narrative in Argument (pp. 94–109)

**Thesis:** Storytelling is not incidental to legal writing but an essential, teachable
skill, even though law school never teaches it. Stark builds the chapter around a close
reading of a *New York Times* human-interest story (about a self-supporting teenager, Brian
Raymond) and extracts six transferable rules.

**The Six Rules of Strong Narrative:**

1. **Think of yourself as a storyteller.** Audiences engage with narrative in a way they
   don't with analytical recitation — Chief Justice Roberts, quoted: "I don't care if it's
   about a dry contract interpretation; you've got two people who want to accomplish
   something...that's a story. And you've got to tell a good story" (p. 101).
2. **Write about people, not abstractions.** Judicial opinions are a poor model here — they
   deliberately depersonalize parties into "tortfeasor," "plaintiff," etc., because
   subjectivity undermines a judge's claim to be stating an objective rule. A litigator's
   goals are different: humanize your clients. Anecdote: a lawyer who kept referring to his
   defendant client by first name — the jury came back with "We find Bobby not guilty" (p.
   103). E.B. White: "Don't write about Man...Write about a man" (p. 103).
3. **Plot is overrated.** Readers already know the ending in most legal writing (the judge
   knows who wins from your brief's structure) — that's fine; what holds attention is *how*
   you get there, not suspense. Corollary from *The New Yorker* editorial tradition: often
   cut your last paragraph, the one where you restate "the point" — trust the reader (p.
   105).
4. **Create "word pictures."** Concrete sensory detail beats abstraction (Chekhov: "Don't
   tell me the moon is shining; show me the glint of light on broken glass," p. 106).
   Practical technique notes: (a) detail is better spent describing *place* than describing
   *people* — readers project themselves onto under-described characters more easily; (b)
   go for details that activate the senses (sight/sound/feel), not just visual description.
5. **The more specific the details, the better.** Specific numbers and facts are more
   memorable and signal writer credibility (readers infer sloppiness from vague phrases like
   "spent thousands of dollars"). Countervailing caution from a federal judge (Andrew
   Guilford): "Don't include nonessential dates," since readers waste effort trying to
   remember them (p. 109).
6. **Use realistic dialogue that reveals character.** Direct quotation lets readers "hear"
   parties and witnesses rather than being told about them secondhand.

## 4. Chapter 6 — Writing the Facts (pp. 113–142)

**Framing rule that precedes the fifteen numbered rules:** *You cannot write your facts
until you know your arguments.* Writing "the facts first, then figuring out the argument"
is backwards — facts have to be selected and sequenced to serve a legal theory you've
already roughed out. Before drafting, ask three questions: (1) What's the story I want to
tell? (2) What do I want readers to think after reading my facts? (3) What facts relating
to my issues will they find most compelling? Draft from memory/theory first, *then* check
every assertion against the record and cut anything unsupported (pp. 115–116).

### The Fifteen Rules of Writing Facts in Litigation

1. **Always include a fact section**, even in short motions, even when both sides
   stipulate to the facts — never let the court read only the other side's version.
2. **Always distinguish your statement of facts from your statement of the case**
   (procedural history vs. the underlying story) — don't open your best material with a
   tedious procedural recitation.
3. **In your first paragraph, tell readers what you're going to tell them** — a summary
   paragraph gives context for everything that follows.
4. **After the opening, lead with your strongest facts — don't feel bound to chronological
   order.** Stark treats this as *the single most common mistake* litigators make. Modern
   narrative (novels, film, even his own son's storytelling once he passed early childhood)
   doesn't proceed strictly chronologically; only lawyers and small children insist on
   telling stories start-to-finish. Practical device: before drafting, ask (a) given my
   client, what do I want to argue? (b) what's the story that supports that argument? (c)
   given that argument, where's the best place to *begin*? Illustrated with a "Three Bears
   v. Goldilocks" hypothetical showing the same facts told from two different starting
   points depending on which side you represent (pp. 119–123).
5. **Deal with the facts against you.** Better the court hears bad news from you than
   discovers it and concludes you tried to hide it — "the cover-up is almost always worse
   than the crime" (p. 125). Concrete deemphasis techniques: bury a bad fact in a subordinate
   clause mid-sentence, sandwich it between two favorable facts, use the passive voice to
   soften agency ("the door was opened" vs. "Sherman opened the door"), give it minimal
   descriptive color, or place it at the *end* of the fact section. Alternatively, confront
   it head-on with candor when it's central — conceding what's true "actually enhances your
   credibility" (p. 129).
6. **Don't argue in your fact section.** Three specific traps: (a) don't state legal
   conclusions as facts ("had the last clear chance" is a conclusion, not a fact — describe
   the observable behavior instead); (b) adjectives/adverbs are usually not facts but
   opinions about facts ("it was a rough ride" → "passengers bounced out of their seats
   every thirty seconds"); (c) you cannot ascribe feelings or mental states to people
   ("she felt bad" → "tears streamed down her face," letting the reader draw the inference)
   (pp. 130–131).
7. **Show, don't tell.** Cinematic/playwright mindset — what would you put on stage to
   dramatize the scene? Illustrative anecdote: a defense lawyer's fact section opened, "On
   the morning of the burglaries in question, my client tried to eat a live cat for
   breakfast," which does more work than any expert's diagnosis of incompetency (p. 131).
8. **Use quotations to make your story come alive.** Direct quotes activate the reader's
   senses and let characters speak for themselves — illustrated with Abe Fortas's *Gideon v.
   Wainwright* brief quoting the actual trial colloquy verbatim.
9. **Each fact in your story must be there for a purpose.** Chekhov's rule (a gun on stage
   in Act One must go off later) applies even more strictly to litigation writing than to
   fiction — every detail you include should trace to a legal point you're building toward,
   or cut it. "Dullness won't sell your product, but neither will irrelevant brilliance"
   (Bill Bernbach, p. 135).
10. **Deal with your best facts only once.** Repetition drains emotional force rather than
    reinforcing it — illustrated with a trial anecdote about a witness whose testimony lost
    its power the more times opposing counsel made her repeat it, and with the diminishing
    impact of the repeatedly-replayed Rodney King video (pp. 135–136).
11. **You can write about things that are not in the record — but flag that you're doing
    it.** Modeled on Sherlock Holmes's "curious incident of the dog in the night-time" (the
    dog's *silence* was the clue). Use sparingly — overuse of "what didn't happen" tips
    into fiction (pp. 136–138).
12. **In a story without much plot, use topic headings.** Contract/UCC cases and other
    fact patterns without narrative arc benefit from being organized into labeled sections
    rather than forced into a single flowing story.
13. **Give judges only the facts they need for the motion currently before them** — don't
    dump the whole case history into every filing.
14. **If your jurisdiction requires numbered facts, write the narrative first, then
    number it** — drafting facts number-first pushes you back into rigid chronology.
15. **Avoid putting new facts in your argument section** — establish every factual
    assertion in the fact section first; a judge won't credit a fact asserted for the first
    time in argument.

**Closing point of the chapter:** the winner isn't the lawyer who tells the best story —
it's the lawyer who tells the best story *supported by a strong legal argument.* And:
"the lawyer who turns a lawsuit into a debate usually wins the debate but loses the
lawsuit" (p. 142) — a line that sets up the theme of Chapter 7.

## 5. Chapter 7 — Writing Arguments (pp. 143–175)

### Six Initial Precepts

1. **The lower you are in the court system, the shorter your submissions should be** —
   trial judges have less time than appellate judges; don't waste it.
2. **In most cases, concentrate on winning the case, not helping the judge write the
   opinion.** These two goals conflict more often than lawyers realize; law school trains
   people to care about doctrinal development (the opinion), but the client only cares about
   the result. Ask yourself explicitly, before drafting, which goal you're actually serving.
3. **A lawsuit is not a debate — don't make responding to the other side the focus of your
   argument.** Your job is to explain why *you* should win, not to rebut point-by-point; even
   a perfect rebuttal only gets you back to "square one." Corollary work-habit rule:
   responding parties (appellees, defendants) should draft their own affirmative argument
   *before* reading the other side's brief, so they don't end up litigating on ground the
   opponent chose (illustrated with a Frederick Wiener anecdote, p. 146–147).
4. **Keep your style understated; avoid military and sports metaphors** ("battle," "level
   playing field," "hold the fort"). Illustrated with the Aeschines/Demosthenes contrast:
   the orator who got the crowd to say "How well he speaks" lost to the one who got them to
   say "Let us march against Philip" (p. 147).
5. **Keep filings as short as possible, regardless of venue.** Quoting Chief Justice
   Roberts ("I have yet to put down a brief and say, I wish that had been longer") and
   Justice Kennedy. Data point: median Supreme Court opinion length has more than doubled in
   fifty years (*Brown v. Board* ran under 4,000 words; a comparable 2007 case ran ~47,000)
   (p. 149).
6. **Anticipate the electronic-filing age** — hyperlinks, graphics, timelines, and other
   Wikipedia/MapQuest-style formatting devices will only become more available and more
   expected.

### The Sixteen Rules of Writing Arguments

1. **Lead with your best argument**, but deal with procedural threshold issues first
   (briefly), then return to substance quickly.
2. **Use the "CRAC" method: Conclusion, Rule, Analysis, Cases.** State your conclusion up
   front, then the rule supporting it, then your factual analysis, then supporting
   authority — this mirrors how people actually argue outside a courtroom, unlike the
   traditional "issue → rule → analysis → conclusion" academic backing-in.
3. **Use case law effectively**, with five sub-rules:
   - (a) Avoid long descriptions of law — a brief isn't an exam; give judges only what they
     need.
   - (b) Rarely analyze a single case for more than a sentence; save extended discussion for
     truly controlling authority. Always distinguish holding from dicta.
   - (c) Avoid asking the court to overrule precedent unless that genuinely is your
     strategy — and if it is, say so explicitly rather than tiptoeing around it.
   - (d) Short quotes beat long quotes; *no* quote is often better than a short one.
     Paraphrase accurately rather than quoting when you can put the holding in your own
     words.
   - (e) Don't overload your argument with citations — string cites of more than three
     cases rarely add persuasive value ("I've yet to meet the judge who looks at the fifth
     case cited in a long string and exclaims, 'I love that case! You win!'" p. 159).
   - (f) Adapt citations to the court you're in — stay within-jurisdiction; out-of-state
     cites can actually *undercut* a strong in-state citation by implying the in-state
     authority isn't strong enough to stand alone.
4. **Package citations at the ends of sentences and paragraphs**, not mid-sentence, so
   citations don't derail the reader's parsing of the substantive claim. When a citation
   string is long, start a new paragraph with the next substantive sentence so the citation
   doesn't bleed attention into the following point.
5. **Avoid responding too aggressively** — address the other side's argument only insofar
   as doing so reinforces a point you're already making; repeating an argument to rebut it
   often ends up reinforcing it in the reader's memory.
6. **Never label or characterize your opponents.** Even soft phrasing like "the appellant's
   reliance...is misplaced" substitutes a label for analysis. Attack the position, not the
   person.
7. **Take a clear stand summarizable in two sentences.** Quoting Judge Alex Kozinski:
   "Simple arguments are winning arguments; convoluted arguments are sleeping pills on
   paper" (p. 163).
8. **Be careful with arguments in the alternative** — they can look evasive/panicked and
   dilute your primary theory; if used, flag explicitly that the alternative argument isn't
   your main theme.
9. **Limit the use of footnotes** in briefs (they're more acceptable in memos). Stark treats
   the migration of academic-style footnoting from law reviews into judicial opinions and
   then into briefs as a genuine degradation of writing quality — footnotes let writers avoid
   deciding what belongs in the reader's primary line of sight. Legitimate footnote uses: (a)
   responding to a minor point from the other side; (b) a long quote you don't want cluttering
   the text; (c) necessary long string cites; (d) preserving-but-not-emphasizing an
   alternative argument; (e) a minor point you don't mind the judge skipping; (f) direct
   commentary aimed at law clerks about how to draft the opinion.
10. **Start a new section roughly every four pages** so readers can consolidate what they've
    read; use simple hierarchical labeling (I, II, III, occasionally A/B) — over-subdividing
    confuses rather than clarifies.
11. **Always view your case from the judge's perspective** — your case is one of hundreds to
    them; don't hype its importance. Adjust argument order to match what actually worries a
    judge on that motion type (e.g., on a preliminary injunction, lead with irreparable harm,
    since that's usually the standard uppermost in a judge's mind, even though most lawyers
    lead with likelihood of success out of habit) (pp. 166–168).
12. **Avoid humor and displays of literary erudition.** Nearly all attempts at litigation
    humor fall flat; don't quote Shakespeare to judges who don't care about Shakespeare —
    "the only Shakespeares your judicial readers care about are the ones...currently serving
    on the local appellate bench" (p. 168).
13. **Use history as much as you can** (in the fact section, not the argument section, to
    preserve the appearance of objectivity) — modeled on the "Brandeis brief" technique from
    *Muller v. Oregon*.
14. **In complex or unusual cases, tell the judge what you're doing and why**, the way you
    would if explaining orally — signposting ("I'm going to spend the next few pages on the
    legislative history of X because...") is not a weakness in writing the way it might feel
    like one.
15. **Do not use the future tense** to predict what the other side will do ("the other side
    will not object") — if you're wrong, you look foolish; write "the other side has assured
    me it will not object" instead.
16. **Don't save anything for the final paragraph** — by the time a judge reaches your last
    page, attention has faded; use the ending only to restate the result requested, plainly.

### Writing the Introduction and Headings

- **Always include a one-page (or shorter) introduction**, even where not required — it's
  the most-read part of the brief and frames everything that follows.
- **Make headings specific and persuasive, not generic labels.** "The Due Process Argument"
  tells the reader nothing; "Smith's Due Process Rights Were Violated When She Was Fired as a
  Teacher Without Being Given Notice or a Hearing" does real work. A well-drafted heading
  should function as a compressed version of the CRAC paragraph that follows it.

### Work Habits for Litigators

1. **No brief should be written by committee** — discrete research tasks can be
   farmed out, but drafting needs a single, unified authorial voice.
2. **Writers should do most of their own research** — research and argument structure are
   inseparable; delegating research to someone who won't write the argument degrades the
   final product.
3. **Be careful of web research** (Stark's version of "verify your sources," including a
   wry, era-specific Lincoln-misattribution joke about internet quotes).
4. **Separate writers from oral advocates** in litigation groups where possible — they are
   different skills, rarely possessed by the same person to the same degree, and the
   strongest oral advocate is often a poor brief-writer who tries to "charm" the page the way
   they charm a courtroom.
5. **Read a strong brief in your substantive area before writing your own** — learn by
   emulating documents that have already worked in that specific field.

## 6. Chapter 11 — Technical Writing (pp. 229–241)

Applies to anything dense or unfamiliar to the reader — complex factual explanations,
patent applications, technical e-mail. Stark's models are deliberately non-legal: board-game
rules, cookbooks, MapQuest/Google Maps directions.

### The Fourteen Rules of Technical Writing

1. **Make sure you understand the material yourself before you begin.** Illustrated with a
   painful oral-argument exchange where a lawyer can't actually define "marginal cost
   analysis" when a judge asks. Test: can you restate the concept in your own words? If not,
   you don't understand it yet.
2. **Start with the bottom line** (same rule as Chapter 1, repeated because technical
   writers especially tend to bury it while getting anxious about the material).
3. **After the bottom line, give readers a written table of contents** — a genuine roadmap,
   not just a heading list, so readers feel oriented before wading into dense material.
4. **Frequently divide material into sections** — roughly every four double-spaced pages,
   sometimes more often; sectioning exists to *simplify the reader's task*, not to reflect
   the material's actual complexity.
5. **At the start or end of each section, summarize where you've been and preview where
   you're heading.** Technical readers are "anxious readers" who forget what they just read
   and need frequent reassurance/reorientation — like highway signage confirming you're
   still on the right road.
6. **Number your points** — numbered lists are more memorable and more skimmable than prose
   paragraphs (but don't over-atomize; thirty-seven numbered points defeats the purpose).
7. **Use the second person** — imperative, direct address ("Make a left on Green Street")
   is easier to process than third-person description, which is why law-school
   hypotheticals work the way they do.
8. **Use graphics** — a real image, chart, or timeline routinely outperforms a paragraph of
   description in a visual-first reading culture.
9. **Layout matters independently of content.** Cites an informal study in which
   identical contract *content*, differently formatted (double/triple-spaced, italicized
   highlights, wide margins), was rated as better *written* by readers who were really only
   responding to presentation.
10. **Be conversational; avoid terms of art**, and where a term of art is unavoidable,
    define it minimally — most readers don't read footnoted definitions and won't remember
    them if they do.
11. **Don't talk down to your readers** — don't announce repeatedly how difficult the
    material is ("this is easy!"); it just intimidates. Explain and move on.
12. **Make the people or entities you're discussing memorable** — this applies to corporate
    and agency names as much as to human characters; initialisms and acronym soup ("Under
    the APA and NEPA, you must file an EIS, ASAP, with DOI...") actively lose readers. Use
    real names or descriptive labels instead.
13. **Refer to common experiences or use narrative** to ground unfamiliar technical
    material — e.g., explaining oil-well mechanics by analogy to bicycles, or the extended
    "Abby plays Nintendo" narrative frame used in the *Lewis Galoob Toys v. Nintendo*
    "questions presented" section to make a copyright/fair-use technical question intuitive.
14. **Don't be wedded to the essay format** — Q&A structure, numbered lists, and other
    non-essay forms are often more effective for technical explanation than continuous prose,
    especially for client-facing material like explaining a new regulation.

## 7. Chapter 13 — Writing Memos (pp. 261–267)

**Framing:** Even in the e-mail/e-document era, lawyers still write research memos but
rarely think carefully about their organization. Four starting principles before drafting:
(1) with clients, lead with *what* they need to do, not *why* (non-lawyers care far less
about doctrine/rationale than lawyers assume); (2) organize for accessibility using the same
Wikipedia/MapQuest logic as elsewhere in the book (executive summaries, lists instead of
essays, hyperlinks in e-memos); (3) remember a memo is analytically different from a brief —
a brief exists to *convince*, a memo exists to *dissect*, so it can present both sides in
more detail and use footnotes more freely; (4) treat memos with the same discretion caution
as e-mail — assume an unintended reader may eventually see it (p. 262).

### The Seven Sections for Organizing a Memo

1. **Question for Research** — state the question in a sentence or two at the top.
2. **The Answer or Conclusion** — answer immediately, as briefly as possible; if multiple
   questions were asked, answer each one right after restating it rather than batching
   answers separately.
3. **The Written Table of Contents** — preview your organizational structure so a reader
   can jump straight to the section they need.
4. **The Facts** — include even a brief factual recitation, since it's often how a future
   reader will judge the memo's relevance to their own, different problem.
5. **The Analysis** — the substantive heart; break into subsections every few pages; use
   conclusory headings; long quotes are more tolerable here than in a brief.
6. **Future Questions for Research** — flag unresolved threads so they aren't lost once the
   matter moves on.
7. **Bibliography** — Stark is emphatic that almost no legal memos include one, and they
   should: readers want to know what the writer relied on, and a "selected/annotated
   bibliography" saves the writer from re-explaining every dead-end line of research inline
   (the "dry holes," p. 264).

Short memos can skip the written TOC and future-research sections and compress the facts to
a sentence or two.

### The Politics of Memos (office-management guidance, not drafting craft per se)

1. **Give or get memo assignments in writing** — verbal instructions are routinely
   misunderstood, and a written assignment forces the assigning lawyer to think it through
   more carefully. Practical script for a junior associate handed a verbal assignment: "Do
   you mind if I put that in an e-mail and run it past you?"
2. **Make sure everyone agrees, in writing, on the expected length and time budget** — both
   protects the associate from unfair blame later and correlates directly with memo quality
   (a writer given twenty hours produces a materially better memo than one given six).

## 8. Conclusion & Appendix (pp. 283–289)

### Conclusion — "The Real Damage of Bad Legal Writing"

This closing chapter is less a rules-list than an argument about *why* legal writing is bad
as a structural/cultural matter, not merely a skills gap:

- Lawyers write badly partly because it serves their **economic interest** — jargon signals
  possession of specialized, billable knowledge, the same "linguistic self-preservation"
  every profession practices (Stark's dentist analogy: "subject to a molar extraction").
- More fundamentally, poor writing follows from **how lawyers are trained to see the
  world**, not merely from what they don't know. Four specific distortions Stark traces
  through the book: (1) lawyers see the world through **precedent**, which structurally
  discourages novelty and produces repetitive, backward-looking prose; (2) legal reasoning
  is oriented around **rules, not facts**, which fights against the narrative instincts that
  make writing compelling; (3) law aspires to **objectivity**, which pushes writers toward
  labels, footnotes, and a refusal to acknowledge concepts like hope or candor; (4) legal
  training normalizes writing as a form of **institutional self-effacement** — the passive
  voice, "it could be argued," the erasure of the first person — which is why so little
  legal writing reveals anything about its author.
- A sharper, more provocative claim: lawyers' habitual use of language *as deception*
  ("Indeed, attorneys are the culture's most respected con artists," p. 287) erodes their
  own relationship to language over a career, and that erosion — not mere lack of
  skill — is what ultimately shows up on the page as bad prose. Stark frames improving as a
  writer as partly a project of **regaining moral seriousness about language**, not just a
  technical skill upgrade.

### Appendix — The Sixteen Rules of Professionalism in Legal Writing (p. 289)

Presented by Stark as a distinct, compact ethical checklist (general rules 1–8, litigation-
specific rules 9–16):

**General Rules**
1. Never lie, under any circumstance.
2. Don't plagiarize.
3. Don't use euphemisms to disguise the truth.
4. If it's not required, hedging is a form of dishonesty.
5. Avoid hyperbole that distorts the truth of your assertions.
6. Ghostwriting is a form of dishonesty.
7. An affidavit should be the assertions of the person who signs it.
8. Always cite accurately.

**Additional Rules in Litigation**
9. Don't engage in personal attacks.
10. In a brief, you must deal with key unfavorable facts and case law in that jurisdiction.
11. Don't argue or editorialize in your fact statements.
12. Adjectives are usually not facts but opinions about them, and don't belong in fact
    statements. Show, don't tell.
13. Don't restate the other side's arguments or cases in a way that is unfair to what they
    actually argued.
14. Don't guess — if you don't know or are unsure, say so.
15. Do not misstate the facts or the law.
16. Remember Rule 11 (FRCP): an attorney who drafts pleadings must ensure they are
    supported by the facts and law.

Notes.)*

## 9. Flagged for the `writer` skill — actionable rules vs. color/anecdote

The instructions for this memo note I don't have direct access to this repo's `writer`
legal-drafting skill file, so what follows is not a diff against it — it's a curated
shortlist of Stark's material that reads as a **concrete, encodable rule** (a specific
do/don't, a named technique, a structural checklist item), pulled out from the surrounding
anecdote and color so it's easy to scan and cross-check against whatever the skill currently
says. Items are grouped by type.

### Structural / organizational rules (high confidence — encode as checklist items)
- **Lead with strongest facts/argument; abandon strict chronology** (Ch. 6 Rule 4, Ch. 7
  Rule 1). This is arguably the single rule Stark treats as most-violated by working
  lawyers and most consequential to persuasive effect.
- **CRAC (Conclusion → Rule → Analysis → Cases)** as the default paragraph/section
  structure for legal argument, explicitly opposed to the "back into your conclusion"
  academic IRAC habit (Ch. 7 Rule 2).
- **One-page-or-shorter introduction, always included**, framing the theory of the case
  before any argument begins (Ch. 7, "Writing the Introduction").
- **Specific, persuasive headings** functioning as compressed CRAC statements, not generic
  category labels (Ch. 7). This directly parallels — and reinforces — a "writer" skill
  principle already described in this task's own framing ("persuasive short headings, not
  generic category labels").
- **Separate statement of facts from statement of the case** (procedural history) as
  distinct sections (Ch. 6 Rule 2).
- **New section roughly every 3–4 pages**, simple hierarchical labeling only (I, II, III;
  occasionally A/B) — avoid over-subdividing (Ch. 7 Rule 10; Ch. 11 Rule 4).
- **Citations packaged at the ends of sentences/paragraphs**, never mid-sentence; long
  citation strings should trigger a paragraph break before the next substantive point
  (Ch. 7 Rule 4).
- **String cites capped at ~3 cases**, with narrow exceptions for showing multi-jurisdiction
  consensus or citing a circuit with recognized subject-matter expertise (Ch. 7 Rule 3(e)).
- **Stay within-jurisdiction on citations** — out-of-jurisdiction cites can visibly weaken
  an otherwise strong in-jurisdiction citation (Ch. 7 Rule 3(f)).

### Fact-section discipline (high confidence — these read like bright-line drafting rules)
- **No legal conclusions in the fact section**; no adjectives/adverbs as facts; no ascribed
  mental states — only observable, quotable, or record-supported facts (Ch. 6 Rule 6,
  a–c). This is a fairly bright-line, checkable rule during self-review of a draft.
- **Every fact must serve an identified purpose** — Stark's "Chekhov rule": if a detail
  doesn't trace to a legal point, cut it (Ch. 6 Rule 9).
- **State your best fact once, not repeated** — repetition dilutes emotional force rather
  than reinforcing it (Ch. 6 Rule 10).
- **No new facts in the argument section** — establish every fact in the fact section first
  (Ch. 6 Rule 15).
- **Address unfavorable facts affirmatively rather than omitting them**, with a specific
  toolkit for controlled de-emphasis (subordinate clause placement, passive voice, minimal
  descriptive detail, end-of-section placement) reserved for genuinely minor bad facts, and
  direct, credibility-building acknowledgment reserved for major ones (Ch. 6 Rule 5).

### Sentence/paragraph-level mechanics (medium confidence — style rules, easy to encode as a checklist)
- Active voice as default; ~25% passive ceiling as a rough personal-discipline benchmark.
- 25-words-or-fewer sentence guideline, scaling down further as subject-matter complexity
  rises.
- No sentence longer than 2–3 lines without a paragraph break for quotes; no block quotes
  longer than 2–3 sentences in litigation writing without moving the material to an
  appendix/footnote.
- Footnotes: sharply limited in briefs (six enumerated legitimate uses only); more freely
  used in memos.
- Avoid military/sports metaphors and words like "obviously"/"clearly"/"this court must" —
  these read as overreach rather than confidence (Ch. 7 Rule 4, Rule 12; Ch. 2 Rule 9).
- Never label or characterize the opposing party/counsel — attack the position, not the
  person (Ch. 7 Rule 6).

### Named techniques worth cross-referencing by name if the skill discusses argument strategy
- **"Outline the argument, research it later"** — the core Ch. 4 method for building an
  argument from an initial intuitive thesis rather than backward from accumulated research.
- **The "Big Idea" / one-sentence slogan test** — force every argument down to a
  one-to-two-sentence core thesis before drafting; test every subsequent paragraph against
  it ("How does this relate to the main idea I'm trying to get across?").
- **The 2/3–1/5–remainder allocation** for space given to a lead argument vs. secondary
  arguments when presenting multiple grounds (Ch. 4).
- **"CRAC"** (see above) as a named, teachable acronym.
- **"Chekhov's gun" rule for fact selection** (see above) — useful as a named heuristic for
  self-editing draft fact sections.

### Judgment calls / lower-confidence flags (worth a second look, less bright-line)
- Stark's instruction to **lead with irreparable harm rather than likelihood of success**
  on preliminary-injunction motions, because that's what's actually "uppermost in the
  judicial reader's mind" even though most lawyers default to the announced legal order of
  elements (Ch. 7 Rule 11) — this is jurisdiction/judge-dependent and worth treating as
  situational guidance rather than a universal rule.
- The claim that **judges want candid concession of weak points** and that this measurably
  *increases* credibility is repeated with several anecdotes (Judge Coffin, Jacob Stein
  brief) but is inherently hard to verify/quantify — treat as a directional principle
  ("don't overclaim") rather than a specific ratio or formula.
- Stark's broad claim that only ~3 cases should ever appear in a string cite is presented
  as near-absolute; a drafting skill may reasonably want to soften this to "avoid
  unnecessary string cites" rather than a hard numeric cap, since some jurisdictions/judges
  expect more.

### General color / not independently actionable (explicitly excluded from the list above)
For completeness: the extensive rhetorical/advertising theory (Rosser Reeves, David Ogilvy,
Bill Bernbach material), the literary-technique material in Ch. 5 (word pictures, sensory
detail, the Hemingway/Chandler comparison), the historical/etymological material on
Anglo-Saxon vs. Latinate vocabulary, and the Conclusion's argument about lawyers'
psychological relationship to language are all genuinely useful *background and rationale*
for why the rules above work, but they aren't themselves drafting rules a skill file would
encode as a checklist item — they're the "why," not the "what to do."
