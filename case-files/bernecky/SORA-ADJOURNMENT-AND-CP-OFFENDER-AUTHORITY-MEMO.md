## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1 — Adjournment.** What standard governs a request to adjourn a SORA classification hearing, particularly where counsel was newly appointed with no preparation time, and does New York import any Sixth-Amendment-style effective-assistance protection into this civil, statutory right-to-counsel context?

**Short answer:** New York applies its ordinary, non-constitutional adjournment-discretion standard — the request must be for material evidence/preparation, made in good faith (not for delay), and not the product of the movant's own lack of diligence — rather than any heightened Sixth Amendment or *Strickland*-derived standard. SORA's right to counsel is purely statutory (Correction Law § 168-n(3)), and courts describe the statute's package of protections (notice, counsel, disclosure, hearing) as *itself* satisfying due process, without a separate ineffective-assistance overlay. I found one directly on-point 2024 Second Department SORA case reversing a denial of an adjournment sought so newly-consulted counsel could review case-specific documents. I found no case addressing the specific "counsel assigned literally the day of the hearing" fact pattern, and no case balancing an adjournment request against a respondent's *already-voluntarily-registered* status — that combination appears to be an open question, not a foreclosed one.

**Issue 2 — The Sotomayor quote.** Can the client's recalled quote be confirmed?

**Short answer:** Yes, in substance and exact wording, with a confirmed source case and confirmed authorship — but only at tier (b), not tier (a), because I could not load the raw opinion text myself (every court-database mirror I tried returned a bot-block). The quote is real, is from a search-warrant/probable-cause opinion, and was authored by then-Judge Sotomayor. Its use in a SORA brief would be a persuasive analogy across doctrinal contexts, not controlling authority, and should be labeled as such if used.

**Issue 3 — Broader CP-offender-vs-contact-offender authority.** Is there more authority, beyond *Gillotti*'s "scientific uncertainty" line, supporting (and cutting against) the proposition that CP offenders as a class do not present the same contact-offense risk as hands-on offenders?

**Short answer:** Yes to both halves. *Gillotti* itself contains a stronger, more quotable passage than the single line already used in the draft (an actual statistic — a 4% hands-on-reoffense rate — reported from expert testimony in the opinion's own text), and the Board's own June 2012 Position Statement (now confirmed read in full) supplies a list of aggravating factors that, read in reverse, is itself an admission that *absence* of those factors correlates with lower risk. A federal empirical source (U.S. Sentencing Commission's 2012 recidivism report) supplies real numbers I read myself. But there is real, and important, adverse authority: *Gillotti*'s majority opinion itself flatly rejects the categorical "I never touched anyone" argument as a basis to ignore statutory scoring factors, a Bronx Criminal Court decision (*Fortis*) denied departure over an identical "small quantity" argument, and — most importantly for this specific client — one strand of the social-science literature the Board's own Position Statement cites (Seto, Hanson & Babchishin 2011) reports a *self-report* contact-offense rate among CP offenders far higher than the official/conviction-based rate, which is uncomfortably close to home given Bernecky's own disclosed 2005/2007 proximity-arousal incidents.

## 4. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### Point I — The Adjournment Standard

**A. The statutory right to counsel and the statute's own adjournment provision.**

Correction Law § 168-n(3) is the governing statute. I obtained its full text from a public-law mirror site (newyork.public.law) that reproduces the New York Senate's official codification and states its own source and "last modified" date (Sept. 22, 2014) for cross-checking; I independently cross-verified the right-to-counsel language and the "dispute" adjournment language against a second, separately-generated extraction of the same nysenate.gov page, and the two matched word for word. I could not load nysenate.gov's own raw HTML directly (it sits behind a bot-challenge), so I am marking this **(b)**, corroborated by two independent, consistent extractions from what is otherwise the correct official text — not (a). The Writer should pull the statute on Westlaw/Lexis for the final citation check before filing, but I have high confidence the text below is accurate.

The operative right-to-counsel language:

> "Upon receipt of the board's recommendation, the sentencing court shall determine whether the sex offender was previously found to be eligible for assigned counsel in the underlying case. Where such a finding was previously made, the court shall assign counsel to represent the offender, pursuant to article eighteen-B of the county law."

And, separately, if no such prior finding exists, the offender may apply and — "If the sex offender applies for assignment of counsel and the court finds that the offender is financially unable to retain counsel, the court shall assign counsel to represent the sex offender pursuant to article eighteen-B of the county law."

Critically, § 168-n(3) **already contains its own adjournment clause**, which appears to have gone unused in the existing draft memo and is directly useful:

> "Where there is a dispute between the parties concerning the determinations, the court shall adjourn the hearing as necessary to permit the sex offender or the district attorney to obtain materials relevant to the determinations from the state board of examiners of sex offenders or any state or local facility, hospital, institution, office, agency, department or division. Such materials may be obtained by subpoena if not voluntarily provided to the requesting party."

This is mandatory ("shall adjourn"), not discretionary, once a genuine dispute over the determinations exists and there is a need to obtain relevant materials. It is not squarely "newly appointed counsel needs general prep time" — it is framed around obtaining *materials* — but it is a real statutory hook: if newly appointed counsel identifies specific materials needed to test the Board's recommendation (e.g., verification of the registration-maintenance history, the underlying PSR pages the Case Summary paraphrases but never pin-cites, or the 2012 Board Case Summary it references), this clause supplies an argument that adjournment is not merely discretionary courtesy but a statutory entitlement once a genuine dispute is identified.

**B. The general adjournment-discretion standard, and the one directly on-point SORA case.**

*People v. Eldridge*, 2024 NY Slip Op 05117 (App. Div., 2d Dept., decided Oct. 16, 2024) — **(b)**, reconstructed from three independent search/fetch extractions that were internally consistent on the operative facts and holding, but I was not able to load the raw opinion text (nycourts.gov's reporter pages returned a bot-block to every fetch method I tried, including direct curl and the web-fetch tool). This is the most directly useful case found for this issue. Facts as reconstructed: at a SORA hearing, the trial court gave defense counsel only fifteen minutes to confer with the defendant immediately beforehand; counsel then asked for a brief adjournment to review documents (relating to treatment-program participation) the defendant had just told him about that "could affect the defendant's designation." The trial court denied the adjournment, citing the defendant's release date two days later. The Second Department reversed, holding the trial court "improvidently exercised its discretion when it denied the defendant's request for an adjournment," reasoning that the documents "may be relevant to support an application for a downward departure" and that "[t]he defendant's open release date two days after the hearing was not a sufficient reason to deny the defendant's request for an adjournment."

The standard the Second Department applied, per the same reconstruction, traces to *Matter of Shepard*, 286 A.D.2d 336 (2001) — **(b), lower confidence**: multiple search-engine syntheses converged on the same formulation ("it is an improvident exercise of discretion to deny such a request where evidence is material, and the application is properly made and is not made for purposes of delay, and where the need for an adjournment does not result from the failure to exercise due diligence"), and independently I was able to confirm via a courtlistener volume-index page that 286 A.D.2d 336 is captioned *In re the Estate of Shepard* (decided Aug. 6, 2001) — which is a Surrogate's-Court-lineage case, not obviously a SORA or even criminal case. That is not disqualifying — New York courts frequently borrow the general adjournment-discretion formula from wherever it was first well articulated, regardless of subject matter — but I want to flag plainly that I have **not** independently read *Shepard* itself, and the department that decided it is not confirmed from what I could access. **This citation needs a direct pull before it goes in any filing.**

Bottom line on the standard: New York does **not** apply a heightened, SORA-specific, or Sixth-Amendment-derived test to adjournment requests. It applies the ordinary civil/criminal-adjacent discretion standard — materiality, good faith, diligence — the same standard used across New York practice generally. *Eldridge* is useful precisely because it applies that ordinary standard *in the SORA context* and finds an abuse of discretion on facts that overlap meaningfully with a same-day-appointed-counsel scenario: counsel had a compressed, last-minute opportunity to learn what was actually at stake, identified specific, potentially departure-relevant material, and was denied time to review it based on scheduling pressure that the Second Department held did not outweigh the request.

**C. Does New York import Sixth-Amendment-style ineffective-assistance protection into the § 168-n(3) right to counsel? No — and one Court of Appeals case says so almost in as many words.**

*People v. Watts*, 42 N.Y.3d 60 (2024) — **(b)**, reconstructed from consistent search/fetch extractions from multiple independent case-summary sources (I was not able to load the raw opinion). Facts as reconstructed: a defendant found not competent to stand trial under CPL Article 730 argued that due process required his SORA classification hearing to be adjourned until he regained competency, so that he could meaningfully participate. The Court of Appeals disagreed, holding that due process does **not** require a competency determination before a SORA hearing, because "the numerous safeguards already provided under SORA, including the rights to notice, counsel, disclosure of relevant information, and an opportunity to object and present evidence at a hearing, adequately balance the interests involved."

This is double-edged and should be presented to the Court candidly, not just as favorable authority. On one hand, *Watts* affirmatively lists "counsel" as one of the safeguards the Court of Appeals treats as constitutionally sufficient — meaning the *presence* of counsel, standing alone, is doing real work in the Court's due-process calculus. If counsel is present in name only, with literally no time to review the file, that is at least a colorable argument that the safeguard *Watts* relied on has not actually been provided in substance. On the other hand, *Watts* is also the clearest evidence I found that New York treats SORA's statutory package as *self-sufficient* — it does not go on to import a *Strickland*-style effective-assistance overlay, and nothing in *Watts* (or in anything else I found) suggests New York courts ask whether appointed SORA counsel's performance was constitutionally adequate the way they would on a criminal appeal. **I found no case — none — addressing the specific scenario of counsel receiving a SORA file the day of the hearing and being asked whether that implicates a right to effective assistance in this civil context.** That silence is itself worth noting to the Court: this is not an argument that has been tried and rejected; it is an argument nobody has yet had occasion to make on these facts. The honest framing is: *Watts* supports treating the presence-of-counsel safeguard seriously enough that stripping it of any practical content is a due-process-adjacent problem, but the vehicle for relief in New York practice is the ordinary adjournment-discretion analysis (Point I.B above), not a freestanding ineffective-assistance claim.

**D. Prejudice-balancing where the respondent claims no practical harm to public notification.**

I found **no case** — SORA or otherwise — that balances an adjournment request specifically against a respondent's claim that he has voluntarily, currently maintained his own registration (including while incarcerated, when not required to do so), such that a short delay causes no practical gap in public notification. This appears to be a genuinely open point, not one that has been raised and lost. It is a factually clean argument to make from first principles under the *Eldridge*/*Shepard* materiality-and-diligence framework: the People's interest that ordinarily justifies denying an adjournment in a SORA proceeding is the public-safety/notification interest identified in the statute's own text (Correction Law § 168-n(3)'s notice language: "Failure to appear may result in a longer period of registration or a higher level of community notification because you are not present to offer evidence"). If the respondent is demonstrably already registered and current, that specific interest is not implicated by a short delay in the same way it would be for someone not yet registered. **This is a good argument to make affirmatively — but see the mandatory flag below: it is only as strong as the factual record behind it, and right now there is no record behind it at all.**

**Flagging the underlying factual claim.** The client's claim that he has voluntarily maintained current registration, including while incarcerated when not legally required to, is exactly the kind of factual premise this memo's format requires flagging as unconfirmed. I have not seen a DCJS registry printout, a registration confirmation letter, or any other document establishing this. Before this argument is put in front of the Court — whether in an adjournment motion or at the hearing itself — it needs to be verified against an actual record. If true, it is a strong, low-cost fact to lead with; if it cannot be documented, the adjournment argument should not be built on it as a stated fact, only (at most) as something counsel represents needs to be confirmed.

### Point II — The Sotomayor Quote

**Confirmed case:** *United States v. Falso*, 544 F.3d 110 (2d Cir. 2008). Panel: Jacobs, Livingston, and **Sotomayor**, Circuit Judge — with Judge Sotomayor authoring the majority opinion on the probable-cause issue. This is confirmed by multiple independent sources (a Quimbee case brief summary picked up in search results, and two independently-run web-fetches of the FindLaw case-law-database page for this opinion, one of which explicitly transcribed the "SOTOMAYOR, Circuit Judge:" attribution line). **Tier (b)** — I was not able to load the raw opinion text myself. Every court-reporter mirror I tried (CourtListener, Justia's cases.justia.com, Leagle, OpenJurist, nycourts.gov-equivalent federal sources) either 403'd on direct fetch or returned a blank/JS-only shell to the web-fetch tool. FindLaw's caselaw database (not an attorney-marketing page — a case-law reference database, which the sourcing rules in the standing brief permit, unlike an attorney's own marketing site) is the only source that yielded readable text, and I ran that fetch twice with different, narrowly-drawn prompts (one of which explicitly warned the tool not to blend in any other case) and got the same quote both times.

**The confirmed quote**, as transcribed by two independent fetches of the same source:

> "It is an inferential fallacy of ancient standing to conclude that, because members of group A" (those who collect child pornography) "are likely to be members of group B" (those attracted to children), "then group B is entirely, or even largely composed of, members of group A."

Both extractions attached the same internal citation immediately after the sentence: *See Martin*, 426 F.3d at 82 (Pooler, J., dissenting) — i.e., Judge Sotomayor's sentence in *Falso* cites, as supporting authority, Judge Pooler's dissent in *United States v. Martin*, 426 F.3d 68 (2d Cir. 2005) (a Second Circuit child-pornography search-warrant case in which Judge Pooler dissented from a probable-cause holding, on facts also involving inferring criminal conduct from list/group membership). I confirmed *Martin*'s citation and Judge Pooler's dissent independently through a separate search that was not seeded by the *Falso* search results. I was **not** able to independently pull the *Martin* dissent's own text to confirm word-for-word whether Sotomayor's *Falso* sentence is itself original or is quoting Pooler more directly than the "See" signal suggests — the "See" signal itself indicates it is Sotomayor's own sentence, supported by (not quoting) Pooler's dissent, but I flag this as a nuance the Writer should not overstate without a direct read of *Martin*.

**Context within *Falso*:** the sentence appears in the Court's analysis of whether the defendant's 2000 conviction for child sexual abuse could support an inference that he had accessed a child-pornography website — i.e., **the fallacy being named is inferring that because members of a broader class (those attracted to children / who had committed a prior sex offense) sometimes belong to a narrower class (those who accessed the specific website at issue), an individual member of the broader class must therefore belong to the narrower class.** I was not able to confirm a page-specific pincite for the quote (F.3d reporter page-break markers do not appear in the source I could access); the Writer should get the exact pincite from Westlaw/Lexis before filing. I found no indication in my searches that *Falso* has been overruled or negatively treated, but I did not have citator access and this should be independently Shepardized/KeyCited before reliance.

**Assessment of use in this SORA matter.** This is a genuinely apt rhetorical and conceptual analogy, but it must be presented as exactly that — persuasive analogy, not controlling authority, and from a wholly different doctrinal context (Fourth Amendment probable cause for a search warrant, not SORA risk classification). The structural fit is real: the Board's Case Summary and Position Statement reasoning about CP offenders as a class (and, more pointedly, the override mechanism challenged in Point II of the existing draft memo) run on exactly the inferential structure Sotomayor's sentence names — some members of the class of CP offenders (group A) go on to commit contact offenses (group B), therefore it is fair to treat a given CP offender as if he belongs to group B. The *Gillotti* "scientific uncertainty" passage (discussed in the existing draft and expanded in Point III below) makes essentially the same point in SORA's own doctrinal vocabulary, without needing the *Falso* quote to carry the argument. My honest recommendation: use the *Falso* language, if at all, as a *rhetorical frame or heading epigraph* — a vivid, non-SORA way of naming the fallacy for the Court before turning to *Gillotti*'s controlling, on-point language — rather than as a citation the argument's legal force depends on. It should never be presented as SORA authority, and the memo should make the cross-context, persuasive-only nature of the citation explicit in the sentence that introduces it, not just implicit from context.

C., Hanson, R. K., & Babchishin, K. M. (2011). Contact sexual offending by men arrested for child pornography offenses. *Sexual Abuse: A Journal of Research and Treatment*, 23, 124-145," which is discussed further below.

**C. Federal Sentencing Commission data — read directly from the primary government report.**

I downloaded and extracted (via direct PDF text extraction, not a summarizing tool) Chapter 11, "Recidivism by Child Pornography Offenders," from the U.S. Sentencing Commission's 2012 report *Federal Child Pornography Offenses* — **tier (a)**, genuine primary-source read of a federal government report. Key findings, with exact figures:

- Of 610 federal non-production CP offenders tracked for an average 8.5-year follow-up period, the **overall sexual recidivism rate was 7.4% (45 of 610)**. Of that group, only **22 offenders (3.6% of the full 610) were arrested for or convicted of a contact sexual offense** (e.g., rape or sexual assault); 14 (2.3%) were arrested/convicted of a new CP offense; the remainder were non-contact offenses involving obscenity or commercial sex.
- Using a comparable ~3-year follow-up window (rather than the full 8.5-year window), the CP-offender new-contact-offense rate was **2.6%**, compared to **5.3%** for a 2003 BJS study of 9,691 sex offenders released from state prison who had *actual prior contact-offense convictions* (rape, statutory rape, sexual assault). The report itself cautions that this comparison is imperfect because the BJS cohort's entire population had known contact-offense histories, while only about one-third of the CP-offender cohort had any known history of "criminal sexually dangerous behavior" (CSDB) — and explicitly states: **"social science research indicates that an offender's history of CSDB (in particular, sexual contact offenses) is a risk factor for sexual recidivism."**

**This last sentence is important adverse-cutting context for this specific client, and should not be left out.** Bernecky's own disclosed 2005/2007 proximity-arousal incidents, even though never charged, are the kind of history the report's own authors flag as a risk factor for sexual recidivism generally — a fact the Board could reasonably invoke if this line of research is put before the Court. The favorable framing (CP offenders as a class have low contact-recidivism) and the unfavorable framing (this particular CP offender has some self-disclosed CSDB-adjacent history) both come from the same body of research, and a candid brief should anticipate the Board using the second half against the first.

**D. Seto, Hanson & Babchishin (2011) — the study the Board's own Position Statement cites.** **Tier (b)** — I was not able to load the original journal article; this is reconstructed from search-engine synthesis of secondary discussions of the study (not from the article itself). **E. New York City Bar Association reports — a useful, candidly-labeled secondary source.**

I read in full the NYC Bar Association's "Updating the Guidelines of the Sex Offender Risk Assessment Instrument" report (initially issued 2013, reissued February 2022) — **tier (a)** as to what the report itself says; **tier (b)** as to every specific study or figure it cites that I have not independently verified (the report is transparent about its own sourcing, and I preserve that same distinction here rather than laundering its citations into my own). This is a bar-association committee report advocating legislative reform, not an attorney's marketing page, and is appropriate to cite as a secondary source under the standing brief's rules. Points of use:

- It quotes a specific New York trial judge (Justice Daniel Conviser, Supreme Court, New York County) criticizing the RAI's methodology generally: "the instrument mixes and matches purportedly objective factors related to the risk of re-offense, with numerical value judgments about the degree of harm an offender's conduct causes and policy considerations... it is not clear on what basis points are assessed" (quoted by the Bar report from what appears to be Justice Conviser's decision in *People v. McFarland*, 2010 NY Slip Op 51705(U) (Sup. Ct., N.Y. County, Oct. 4, 2010) — **I have not independently read *McFarland* itself; this is (b)-tier, one level removed.** *McFarland* is an unreported trial-level decision and would carry limited weight even if directly confirmed, but is worth a look if the Writer wants a New York judge's own words critiquing the RAI's scientific footing generally.
- It states, citing the Board's own Position Statement, that the Board's own scoring practice for CP cases is inconsistent in application: "While the Board generally follows the Position Statement by not scoring in these categories... the courts read Factors 3 and 7 mechanically, increasing points in these categories in virtually every case, resulting in a Level Two adjudication for all people convicted of possessing child pornography who fail to articulate grounds for downward departure." This supports the departure argument's premise that mechanical application is the norm the Court needs to be persuaded to depart from.
- It references the Child Pornography Risk Tool ("CPORT"), a 2015 empirically-validated instrument built specifically on CP-offender data, as an alternative to the RAI for this offender population — useful as background showing that the scientific and policy community, not just the defense bar, recognizes CP offenders as needing a different assessment approach than contact offenders. I have not independently verified the CPORT citation or its validation data.

**F. Adverse authority — actively surfaced, not just favorable results.**

1. ***Gillotti* itself is partly adverse, and this should not be hidden.** The same opinion that supplies the "scientific uncertainty" language also states, in rejecting the defendants' argument that hands-off conduct should categorically limit their risk classification:

   > "Given that child pornography offenders substantially harm the mental health of abused children and, via the consumption of child pornography, encourage others to commit the hands-on sexual abuse needed to produce that material, it is difficult to credit defendants' claims that, due to their failure to personally physically abuse [children,]..." [sentence continues; I have the full clause but am presenting the load-bearing portion I am confident in verbatim]

   And, more directly damaging to a categorical argument: **"there is nothing inherent in child pornography offenses that convinces us to completely ignore the plain language of the guidelines which authorize the scoring of points under factors 3 and 7 against child pornography offenders."** *Gillotti*, 23 N.Y.3d at 858. This confirms that *Gillotti* is a departure case, not a categorical-exemption case — the existing draft memo already frames the argument correctly as a departure argument for this reason, and this passage is the reason why that framing is necessary rather than optional.

2. ***People v. Fortis*, Crim. Ct., Bronx County, Docket No. CR-007821-22BX (decided Nov. 15, 2022)* — **tier (b)**, reconstructed from a web-fetch of a case-law database page; I was not able to load the raw decision. Reconstructed facts: a CP-possession defendant assessed at Level II (80 points) sought downward departure to Level I based partly on possessing a comparatively small quantity of material (138 images/videos, as against cases involving "thousands"). The court denied departure, reasoning the defendant "could not articulate how possessing fewer items would lower recidivism risk," and treated the images' content (including images of 8-to-10-year-old female children) as weighing against departure. This is useful, genuinely adverse authority: a New York criminal court, applying *Gillotti*, was unpersuaded by a "my collection was smaller/less severe" argument, and it should be anticipated as the kind of reasoning the Board or the People might invoke here — particularly given the 2012 conviction's much larger volume (~62,000 files, ~8,811 videos).

3. ***People v. Gunter*, 217 A.D.3d 788 (2d Dept 2023)* — already discussed at length in the existing draft memo's Point I.C in the override context; I confirm on my own read (Drive PDF, **tier (a)**) that *Gunter* also generally describes downward departures as "the exception... not the rule" and rejected several categories of mitigating evidence (unsupported family/housing evidence, non-exceptional treatment response) as insufficient. This is adverse general-departure authority the Writer should keep in mind is available to the People beyond the override-specific point already addressed in the draft.

4. **The CSDB caveat in the USSC report (§ III.C above)** and **the self-report contact-offense rate in Seto/Hanson/Babchishin (§ III.D above)** are both, independently, adverse social-science findings specific to this client's fact pattern (a CP offender with some disclosed, uncharged proximity/arousal history) and should be treated as live risks to the departure argument, not ignored.

## 5. CONCLUSION

**On adjournment (Point I):** I am reasonably confident — though not fully verified to (a)-tier on the two SORA-specific cases — that New York applies its ordinary discretion-based adjournment standard (materiality, good faith, diligence) to SORA hearings, that this standard has real teeth in the SORA context specifically per *Eldridge*, and that no heightened constitutional standard applies. The strongest, most concrete hook is § 168-n(3)'s own mandatory adjournment clause for cases involving a genuine "dispute... concerning the determinations" where materials need to be obtained — which the Writer should consider invoking by name, not just the general discretion cases. **On the broader CP-offender authority (Point III):** The favorable authority is real and gets stronger with the *Gillotti* 4%-statistic and the Board's own Position Statement than the existing draft currently uses.
