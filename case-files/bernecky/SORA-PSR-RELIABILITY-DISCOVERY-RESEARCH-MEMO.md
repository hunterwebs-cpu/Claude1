## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1 — PSR hearsay reliability.** Does a Board Case Summary that
characterizes a federal PSR's own characterization of a police report (a
probation officer's summary of a summary, not a verbatim transcript) meet the
"reliable hearsay" standard of Correction Law § 168-n(3) as construed in
*People v. Mingo*?

*Summary conclusion:* Under *Mingo* itself, the general rule cuts against this
argument — case summaries and presentence reports are treated as needing "no
foundation" because their "origins and function are well-known to SORA
courts." But *Mingo*'s own test is whether "a reasonable person would deem it
trustworthy" based on the circumstances of the proof's development, and *Mingo*
reversed precisely because a category of document that looked reliable in the
abstract lacked any foundation showing *how* it was actually generated. No
case I found addresses a *federal* PSR specifically, as opposed to the New
York PSR/case-summary process that is genuinely well-known to SORA courts.
That is a real, live distinguishing argument — but it is an extension of
*Mingo*, not an application of settled law, and it should be presented to the
Court as exactly that.

**Issue 2 — Rule 32(i)(3)(B) and later reliance on an unresolved fact.** Does
Rule 32(i)(3)(B) allow a sentencing court to decline to resolve a controverted
PSR fact as non-germane to sentencing, and has any court addressed whether
that unresolved fact can later be treated as established in a separate
proceeding?

*Summary conclusion:* Yes on the first half — Rule 32(i)(3)(B) is exactly that
kind of escape valve, confirmed from its current official text. On the second
half: after a genuinely thorough search, using many search-term variations, I
found no case — federal or New York, SORA or any other civil proceeding —
addressing whether a PSR fact a sentencing court declined to resolve under
this rule (or its prior numbering) can later be relied upon as adjudicated in
a different tribunal. This appears to be a first-impression question. I say
that plainly, not to inflate the argument's novelty for effect, but because
that is what a genuinely thorough search turned up.

**Issue 3 — Verification of *Charmer* and *Mingo*.** Do these two cases, cited
in the client's unsent draft letter, actually hold what that letter says they
hold, and are they still good law?

*Summary conclusion:* Both cases are real, and I read *Charmer* in full from a
primary source in the Drive folder. *Charmer* holds what the letter says it
holds in general terms — a PSR "may not properly be disclosed without
authorization by the court," and a third party seeking disclosure must show
"a compelling need... to meet the ends of justice." 711 F.2d at 1176. But the
letter's application of that holding to this case is contradicted by a
document already in this matter's own Drive folder: Bernecky's 2018 federal
judgment contains an express special condition in which the sentencing judge
himself authorized exactly the disclosure the letter calls unauthorized. This
is discussed in full under Issue 4, because it is the single most important
finding in this memorandum and it cuts against the client's own instinct.
*Mingo* is verified as described under Issue 1.

**Issue 4 — The transmission mechanism.** Is there a colorable objection that
transmission of the federal PSR to the Board was legally unauthorized?

*Summary conclusion:* No — not on this record, and this is an answer that
cuts against the client's own draft letter. Bernecky's 2018 judgment (which I
read in full, a primary source already in the Drive folder) contains a special
condition of supervised release expressly stating: "The probation office is
authorized to release the defendant's presentence report to the New York
State Board of Examiners of Sex Offenders. Further disclosure to the county
court and to the parties involved in the determination of the defendant's
final classification level is also authorized." That is precisely the "prior
authorization by the court" *Charmer* requires. The unsent letter's fallback
argument — that this condition "has not yet commenced" because supervised
release has not begun — is very likely wrong, because SORA classification
proceedings are statutorily designed to occur *before* release (Correction
Law § 168-n(2) requires the determination within 30 days before discharge),
so a judgment provision authorizing pre-release registration coordination
with the state cannot sensibly be read as inoperative until after release.

**Issue 5 — The disputed image-count fact.** Can I determine from case law
alone whether this specific dispute was left unresolved as non-germane under
Rule 32(i)(3)(B)?

*Summary conclusion:* No, and I am not guessing. This is a fact question that
can only be answered by obtaining and reading the actual 2012 and/or 2018
sentencing transcripts and any Rule 32 addenda. That is stated as a
Recommendation below, not resolved here.

**Issue 6 — SORA discovery.** What discovery mechanism, if any, does
Correction Law § 168-n give a SORA respondent, and would it reach the actual
federal sentencing transcripts?

*Summary conclusion:* § 168-n(3) itself contains the mechanism — it is not a
CPLR-style discovery regime, but a built-in disclosure-and-adjournment
provision: the Board's recommendation and reasons must be provided at least 20
days before the hearing, and where a dispute exists, "the court shall adjourn
the hearing as necessary to permit the sex offender or the district attorney
to obtain materials relevant to the determinations from the state board of
examiners of sex offenders or any state or local facility, hospital,
institution, office, agency, department or division," obtainable "by subpoena
if not voluntarily provided." That mechanism, however, is textually limited to
*state or local* records-holders. It does not, on its face, reach a federal
district court's own sealed transcript or a federal probation office's file —
those would have to be sought directly in the federal criminal dockets, not
through the SORA proceeding's own subpoena power.

## 4. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### Issue 1: Does PSR-sourced hearsay, filtered through a state Board's further paraphrase, meet the *Mingo* reliability standard?

**Rule.** Correction Law § 168-n(3) allows a SORA court to "consider reliable
hearsay evidence submitted by either party, provided that it is relevant to
the determinations" — text I confirmed directly against the official NY
Senate legislative text mirror **(b-corroborated: matched independently across
two separate fetches of the statute)**. *People v. Mingo*, 12 N.Y.3d 563
(2009), is the controlling construction of "reliable" in this context. I read
it via two independent web mirrors of the official opinion (FindLaw and
Cornell's Court of Appeals mirror) and the quoted language below matched word
for word across both **(b-corroborated)**:

> "hearsay is reliable for SORA purposes — and, therefore, admissible — if,
> based on the circumstances surrounding the development of the proof, a
> reasonable person would deem it trustworthy."

*Mingo* was actually two consolidated appeals decided in a single opinion —
Case No. 94 (*Mingo*) and Case No. 95 (*People v. Balic*) — and the two came
out oppositely on the facts, which is the real teaching of the case. In
*Mingo*, internal District Attorney's office forms were used to score a
weapon-possession factor, and the Court of Appeals *reversed* because "the
SORA court accepted the forms used by the District Attorney's office without
requiring foundation evidence of any type, the record contains no information
supplying the requisite indicia of reliability" **(b-single-source, from the
Cornell-mirror fetch — the exact string was not independently corroborated
against a second mirror and should be confirmed before quoting in a filing)**.
The Court required the DA to come forward with "how these documents are
generated, the personnel who prepared them, the sources typically relied upon
for the information contained therein, and the function they served" before
such documents could be treated as reliable. In *Balic*, by contrast, the
Court *affirmed* reliance on an unsworn victim statement recounted in a sworn
police complaint, because "the police officer indicates that he is recounting
statements made by the child victim on the same day as the crime" and the
officer had "a duty to accurately record statements made by a complaining
witness" — a duty-based, contemporaneous-recording rationale.

Critically for this issue, *Mingo* separately addressed case summaries and
PSRs as a distinct, generically-reliable category:

> "No foundation is necessary for their consideration at SORA hearings
> because such documents are created under statutory mandates and their
> origins and function are well-known to SORA courts."

That is a direct statement that PSRs and Board case summaries, as a category,
do not need the DA-forms foundation showing *Mingo* itself required. And
*People v. Adams*, 216 A.D.3d 1376 (3d Dep't 2023) — which I read directly
from a Drive PDF, a substantial excerpt of the actual opinion text, category
**(a)** — applied exactly this rule as recently as 2023: an indictment and a
presentence report, describing pornographic images of multiple child victims,
were held to be "reliable hearsay that has been found to constitute clear and
convincing evidence supporting a risk level classification," quoting *People
v. Conrad*, 193 A.D.3d 1187, 1188 (3d Dep't 2021), and citing *Mingo*, 12
N.Y.3d at 573-574, directly.

**Application.** The honest reading is that the general rule runs against the
argument as initially framed. If the Board's Case Summary is simply "a case
summary" and the document it draws from is simply "a presentence report,"
*Mingo*'s own language treats both as needing no foundation at all — and
*Adams* shows New York courts applying that rule to exactly the kind of
content at issue here (image counts, victim ages, admissions) as recently as
2023.

But there is a genuine, non-frivolous distinction available, and it should be
built carefully rather than overstated. *Mingo*'s rationale for waiving
foundation — that these documents' "origins and function are well-known to
SORA courts" — is grounded in New York SORA courts' *routine, repeated*
familiarity with how a New York PSR (prepared under CPL 390 by New York
probation officers, for New York sentencing judges, under procedures New York
courts see in case after case) is actually built. A federal PSR, prepared by
federal probation officers under a materially different rule (Fed. R. Crim.
P. 32 and 18 U.S.C. § 3552), for a federal sentencing judge, following federal
Sentencing Guidelines conventions New York SORA courts do not handle in the
ordinary course, is not self-evidently the same kind of document whose
"origins and function are well-known" to a Monroe County SORA court in the
way *Mingo* meant. Layered on top of that is a second, distinct hearsay
problem the assignment specifically raised: even accepting the PSR itself as
reliable, the document actually before the Board — and now before this
Court — is not the PSR. It is the *Board's own case summary*, which
paraphrases what the Board's examiner understood the PSR (itself already once
a probation officer's summary of a police report, not a verbatim transcript)
to say. That is at minimum a third layer of characterization standing between
this Court and the original source. No case I found — SORA or otherwise —
addresses that specific compound structure (state Board's paraphrase, of a
federal probation officer's summary, of an investigative report) as opposed
to the ordinary single-layer New York case summary *Mingo* and *Adams*
actually confronted.

**Conclusion on Issue 1.** This is a real argument, but it is an *extension*
of *Mingo*, built on a distinction *Mingo* itself does not draw, not an
application of a rule already decided in the respondent's favor. It should be
framed to the Court exactly that way — the general rule is adverse, the
distinguishing argument is genuinely available, and no authority forecloses
or resolves it either way.

### Issue 2: Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(i)(3)(B) and later reliance on an unresolved fact

**Rule.** I confirmed the current text of Rule 32(i)(3) directly against
Cornell's official rules mirror, and it read identically across two separate
fetches **(b-corroborated)**:

> "(3) Court Determinations. At sentencing, the court:
> (A) may accept any undisputed portion of the presentence report as a
> finding of fact;
> (B) must — for any disputed portion of the presentence report or other
> controverted matter — rule on the dispute or determine that a ruling is
> unnecessary either because the matter will not affect sentencing, or
> because the court will not consider the matter in sentencing; and
> (C) must append a copy of the court's determinations under this rule to any
> copy of the presentence report made available to the Bureau of Prisons."

So the rule is exactly what the assignment described: a sentencing court
confronted with a controverted PSR fact has three options — rule on it, find a
ruling unnecessary because it won't affect the sentence, or find a ruling
unnecessary because the court won't consider it at all. Options two and three
leave the underlying factual dispute genuinely unresolved; the court has
made no finding either way.

**Research on later reliance.** I ran this search from several different
angles — collateral estoppel and issue-preclusion framings, deportation and
parole framings, civil-commitment framings, and direct "later relied upon"
framings — specifically because the brief instructs me not to stop at the
first unproductive search term. None of these searches turned up a case, in
any jurisdiction or procedural posture, addressing whether a Rule 32(i)(3)(B)
non-ruling can later be treated as an adjudicated or established fact in a
separate proceeding. I did find general background confirming the structural
point that makes the question worth asking: an *unresolved* objection is
listed in a Rule 32 addendum as unresolved, not resolved, and ordinary
issue-preclusion doctrine requires that an issue have been "actually
litigated and determined" before it carries preclusive weight elsewhere — a
sentencing court's express refusal to rule is, on its face, the opposite of a
determination. I did not find a single case applying that logic to a PSR fact
specifically, which means this connection is my own analytical inference from
general preclusion principles, not something any court has said.

**Conclusion on Issue 2.** This is a first-impression question, stated
plainly and without either inflating or undercutting that novelty. It is
worth making as an affirmative argument (a matter a court expressly declines
to resolve cannot, by ordinary preclusion logic, later be presented to a
different tribunal as an adjudicated fact) precisely because no adverse
authority exists either — the Board cannot cite a case rejecting this theory
any more than the defense can cite one accepting it.

### Issue 3: Verification of *Charmer Industries* and *Mingo*

*Mingo* is addressed fully under Issue 1 and is verified as real and
correctly cited for its general holding in the client's unsent letter (though
the letter's characterization that PSRs are categorically too unreliable for
SORA use overstates *Mingo*, which actually treats PSRs and case summaries as
presumptively reliable, not presumptively unreliable — this is exactly the
kind of client-drafted-document error the brief warns about, and it should
not be repeated in any filed document).

*United States v. Charmer Industries, Inc.*, 711 F.2d 1164 (2d Cir. 1983), is
real, and I read the complete opinion — all sections and footnotes — directly
from a PDF already in the Drive folder's "Cases" subfolder **(a) — full
primary-source read**. Its actual holding, correctly stated: a presentence
report "may not properly be disclosed without authorization by the court,"
and once a third party seeks disclosure of a PSR, that party bears the burden
of showing "a compelling need for disclosure... to meet the ends of justice"
— language the Second Circuit borrowed directly from the grand-jury-secrecy
standard. 711 F.2d at 1176-77. The case arose when a state Attorney General
sought a PSR for a liquor-license revocation proceeding — an administrative
proceeding functionally similar to a SORA classification hearing, which is
presumably why the client's letter reached for it. The court reversed
disclosure there because the Arizona AG had gotten the report through an
irregular, unauthorized channel (a probation-service records clerk, before
any court approval) and never made the required compelling-need showing.

*Charmer* remains good law as far as I can determine without a full citator
run. It is cited approvingly and applied by later courts outside the Second
Circuit — *United States v. Gomez*, 323 F.3d 1305, 1307 (11th Cir. 2003),
which I read in substantial excerpt directly from a Drive PDF **(a,
partial)**, cites *Charmer* for the "general presumption that courts will not
grant third parties access to the presentence reports of other individuals,"
alongside *United States Department of Justice v. Julian*, 486 U.S. 1, 108
S.Ct. 1606 (1988). I did not run a dedicated citator (no Shepard's/KeyCite
access), so this should be independently Shepardized before final reliance,
per the brief's mandatory-validation rule — but nothing in my research
suggested it has been overruled or negatively treated, and it is still being
cited as controlling authority on this exact point two decades after it was
decided.

**The critical application point — stated candidly because it cuts against
the client:** *Charmer*'s actual rule is that a PSR "may not properly be
disclosed without authorization by the court." The client's unsent letter
uses *Charmer* to argue that transmission to the Board here was unauthorized.
But Bernecky's own 2018 federal judgment — a primary source I read in full,
already sitting in this matter's Drive folder — contains exactly the
authorization *Charmer* requires. See Issue 4, immediately below, for the
full discussion. *Charmer*, read correctly and applied to the actual record in
this file, does not support the unauthorized-disclosure theory; if anything,
it shows what would have made the disclosure improper (transmission without
prior judicial authorization) — and that is not what happened here.

### Issue 4: The actual mechanism, and whether transmission was authorized

**The federal disclosure framework.** Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(e) governs
disclosure of the PSR to the defendant, defense counsel, and the government
attorney — I confirmed its current text directly against Cornell's mirror
**(b-corroborated)** — and 18 U.S.C. § 3552(d), also confirmed directly,
requires the same three-party disclosure "at least ten days prior to the date
set for sentencing." Neither provision speaks to disclosure to a state
agency. *Charmer* itself makes this same observation about the prior version
of the rule: Rule 32(c) (now e/i) "is silent as to whether and under what
circumstances such reports may be disclosed to 'third persons,'" 711 F.2d at
1172 — which is precisely why *Charmer*'s judge-made "compelling
need"/"authorization by the court" standard exists to fill that gap.

**Correction Law's side of the mechanism.** Correction Law § 168-k governs
registration determinations for offenders convicted in another
jurisdiction, including federal court — the Board's own cover memo in this
file ("has reviewed your conviction in another jurisdiction") tracks this
provision directly. I confirmed via the official NY Senate legislative text
that § 168-k directs the Board to review "any information obtained" and make
a recommendation, but — and this matters — the statute does not itself
specify *how* the Board is to obtain that information from a federal source.
Correction Law § 168-b, which the assignment specifically asked me to check,
similarly does not address federal-offender information sources at all; it
governs what the Division must collect *from registrants themselves* and how
registry data is shared with other state agencies, not how the Board obtains
federal case records. **In other words, New York's own statute does not
supply the authorization for the PSR's transmission — the authorization, if
it exists, has to come from the federal side.**

**It does — and I found it directly.** Bernecky's 2018 federal judgment
(docket 6:18-CR-06018-DGL-MWP, entered June 12, 2018, Hon. David G. Larimer),
which I read in full from a PDF in the Drive folder **(a) — full
primary-source read of the actual judgment in this defendant's own case**,
contains this special condition of supervised release, quoted verbatim:

> "The defendant shall register with the state sex offender registration
> agency in any state where the defendant resides, is employed, carries on a
> vocation, or is a student, and shall provide proof of registration to the
> probation officer. The probation office is authorized to release the
> defendant's presentence report to the New York State Board of Examiners of
> Sex Offenders. Further disclosure to the county court and to the parties
> involved in the determination of the defendant's final classification level
> is also authorized."

This is not a general SORNA registration boilerplate provision (that appears
separately, as mandatory condition 6, requiring compliance with SORNA "as
directed by the probation officer, the Bureau of Prisons, or any state sex
offender registration agency"). It is a distinct, specific special condition,
imposed by the same federal judge who sentenced Bernecky, expressly
authorizing the exact three-step disclosure chain now at issue: probation
office → Board → county court and "parties involved" (i.e., the DA). This is,
on its face, precisely the "authorization by the court" *Charmer* requires
before third-party PSR disclosure is proper.

**The unsent letter's fallback argument, and why it is very likely wrong.**
`jb_courtpack.pdf` (also an unsent draft in the same Drive folder, part of the
same never-filed TRO package) anticipates this condition and argues around
it: "the Judgment contains a special condition authorizing disclosure to the
Board, that condition is part of a term of Supervised Release which has not
yet commenced. Upon information and belief, under 18 U.S.C. § 3583,
conditions of supervised release do not bind the Defendant or the Probation
Office until the Defendant is released from imprisonment." I evaluated this
argument on its own terms and do not find it persuasive. Correction Law §
168-n(2), which I confirmed directly, requires the SORA determination to occur
"no later than thirty calendar days prior to the sex offender's discharge" —
meaning the entire SORA classification process, by statutory design, is meant
to happen *before* release, while the defendant is still in BOP custody or at
a reentry facility, exactly as happened here. A judgment condition authorizing
the probation office to coordinate that pre-release process with the state
Board cannot sensibly be read as legally inert until after release, because
that reading would make it impossible for the condition to ever serve the
purpose it plainly exists for. I did not find, and was not asked to find,
case law specifically rejecting this "not yet commenced" theory — but the
statutory-timing mismatch it runs into is enough that I do not think it
should be relied upon.

**Conclusion on Issue 4.** There is no colorable objection that transmission
of the 2018 PSR to the Board was unauthorized — the sentencing court's own
judgment authorized it, in terms specific enough to leave little room for
argument. This is an honest answer that cuts against the client's own
instinct and against a theory already partly drafted in this file, and it
should be said to the client plainly rather than left for a hearing to
discover. One open item remains, flagged as unknown above and as a
Recommendation below: I have not seen the 2012 judgment (docket
6:11-CR-06045) and cannot confirm it contains an equivalent authorization for
the PSR content the Case Summary attributes to that earlier case.

### Issue 6: SORA discovery mechanism

**Rule.** Correction Law § 168-n(3), confirmed directly against the official
legislative text **(b-corroborated across two independent fetches, with the
key operative language matching word for word both times)**, provides the
actual discovery-adjacent mechanism in a SORA proceeding:

> "At least twenty days prior to the determination proceeding, the sentencing
> court shall notify the district attorney, the sex offender and the sex
> offender's counsel, in writing, of the date of the determination proceeding
> and shall also provide the district attorney, the sex offender and the sex
> offender's counsel with a copy of the recommendation received from the
> board and any statement of the reasons for the recommendation received from
> the board."

> "Where there is a dispute between the parties concerning the
> determinations, the court shall adjourn the hearing as necessary to permit
> the sex offender or the district attorney to obtain materials relevant to
> the determinations from the state board of examiners of sex offenders or
> any state or local facility, hospital, institution, office, agency,
> department or division. Such materials may be obtained by subpoena if not
> voluntarily provided to the requesting party."

There is no separate CPLR-style discovery regime grafted onto SORA
proceedings — no depositions, no interrogatories, no CPLR Article 31 motion
practice. The statute's own mandatory pre-hearing disclosure (the
recommendation and statement of reasons, 20 days out) plus this
adjournment-and-subpoena provision *is* the mechanism.

**Due process backdrop.** This mechanism exists because of *Doe v. Pataki*, 3
F. Supp. 2d 456 (S.D.N.Y. 1998) **(b-single-source — I was blocked by a 403
error from directly fetching Justia's copy and relied on a secondary summary;
this should be independently confirmed before being quoted in any filing)**,
which found the pre-amendment SORA notice procedures constitutionally
deficient for, among other things, "plac[ing] the burden on him to prove that
the Board's findings were wrong without disclosing to him the bases for those
findings." *People v. David W.*, 95 N.Y.2d 130 (2000), confirmed via Cornell's
official Court of Appeals mirror **(b-corroborated)**, later applied the same
due-process logic to reverse a SORA classification where the defendant "did
not receive notice that his risk level was being determined, was not informed
of what materials would be relied upon in making that determination and was
not afforded an opportunity to present his objections." And *People v.
Baxin*, 26 N.Y.3d 6 (2015) — a real, confirmed case, though I have only a
secondary summary of it and have not independently read the full opinion, so
its holding should be confirmed before being relied on for exact language —
appears to have held that a SORA court's reliance on undisclosed grand jury
minutes violated due process (though the error was found harmless on the
facts of that case), reasoning that in the SORA post-conviction context the
usual secrecy justifications for withholding such material "have much less
force."

**Application to obtaining the federal transcripts.** This is the honest
limitation to flag: § 168-n(3)'s adjournment/subpoena language is written to
reach "any state or local facility, hospital, institution, office, agency,
department or division." On its face, that does not extend to a federal
district court's own sealed transcript or a federal probation office's file.
A SORA subpoena issued under this provision, directed at a federal court or
federal probation office, would be reaching outside the class of
institutions the statute actually names. The practical path to the actual
2012/2018 sentencing transcripts is not through the SORA proceeding's own
discovery mechanism at all — it is a separate application in the federal
criminal dockets themselves (ordering the transcript through the federal
court's own docket, or seeking access to sealed portions of the record under
that court's continuing authority over its own file).

**Conclusion on Issue 6.** § 168-n(3) does give Bernecky a real, usable
mechanism to demand the materials the Board and DA actually intend to rely on
before the hearing, and to force an adjournment and subpoena state-level
records custodians if there is a genuine dispute — this should be used
affirmatively before September 11. But it is not a vehicle for compelling the
federal court to produce the sentencing transcripts; that has to be pursued
directly in the federal dockets.

## 5. CONCLUSION

On Issue 1 (PSR hearsay reliability), the Board currently has the better of
the general rule, but a genuine, unresolved distinguishing argument exists
for federal PSRs filtered through a state Board's further paraphrase. I would
put moderate confidence in this being worth raising, low confidence in it
prevailing without more development.

On Issue 2 (Rule 32(i)(3)(B) later-reliance), this is honestly a novel
argument with no authority on either side. I have moderate confidence the
underlying preclusion logic is sound as a matter of first principles, low
confidence about how a court will receive an argument with zero supporting
precedent.

On Issue 3 (verification), both cases check out as real and are described
correctly at the level of general holding, with the correction that *Mingo*
treats PSRs as presumptively reliable, not presumptively unreliable, which
should not be misstated the way the unsent letter states it.

On Issue 4 (transmission mechanism), I have high confidence there is no
viable unauthorized-transmission argument on the 2018 PSR specifically — the
2018 judgment's own language forecloses it. This is the finding the team most
needs to hear plainly before deciding whether to pursue any version of that
theory.

On Issue 5 (image count), I express no opinion because none is possible from
case law alone.

On Issue 6 (discovery), Bernecky has a real, statute-based discovery tool for
state-held materials, with a genuine gap as to federal records that requires
a separate, federal-docket-side application.
