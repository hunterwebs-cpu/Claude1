**Roadmap and bottom line up front:** The controlling authority is *United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748 (2d Cir. 2023) — a Western District of New York case in which the Second Circuit construed the *identical* special-condition language that appears as Special Condition (a) in Mr. Bernecky's Judgment, together with several CIMP provisions that are verbatim or near-verbatim identical to provisions in Mr. Bernecky's Agreement. *Kunz* upheld the general delegation scheme (as narrowly construed) but struck down one specific CIMP term as exceeding the authority the special condition actually delegated. ## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue A:** Does Special Condition (a) of the Judgment — which conditions Mr. Bernecky's computer/internet use on CIMP participation and authorizes Probation to install monitoring software, notify Probation of "impermissible/suspicious activity," and conduct triggered examinations "consistent with the computer monitoring policy in effect by the probation office" — itself constitute an impermissible delegation of judicial sentencing authority to Probation?

*Summary answer:* No, in all likelihood. The identical language was upheld against this exact challenge in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 765–75, as construed to delegate only "minor details" of implementation, not decisions that make Mr. Bernecky's liberty "contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." A frontal attack on Special Condition (a) itself is very unlikely to succeed on this record.

**Issue B:** Do specific numbered items of the CIMP Participant Agreement Probation required Mr. Bernecky to sign impose obligations beyond what Special Condition (a) (or the general search condition, Special Condition (f)) actually authorizes?

*Summary answer:* Yes, as to several items — most clearly **Item 3** (blanket prior-authorization/"whitelist" requirement for every internet-capable device), **Item 19** (freestanding duty to notify third parties who share access to the device that it is subject to monitoring/search), and **Item 21** (search-and-seizure trigger broadened to "unlawful conduct by any person," extended to "law enforcement" generally, untethered from the "impermissible/suspicious activity" trigger that Special Condition (a) actually specifies). **Item 9** (categorical, non-waivable ban on "encrypted email accounts") presents a related vagueness/overbreadth problem given that ordinary modern email service is, by default, transport-encrypted. The Agreement's **opening catch-all** ("...the instructions of my probation officer") is a weaker, unresolved fifth candidate. Most of the *remaining* items (2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 17, 18, 20) either implement Special Condition (a)'s text directly or track CIMP language the Second Circuit specifically examined and upheld *as narrowly construed* in *Kunz* — they are not independently invalid, but their validity depends on that narrowing construction being applied in practice, not on the Agreement's literal text.

**Issue C:** What relief is available, and through what procedural vehicle?

*Summary answer:* The correct vehicle is not a broad attack on Special Condition (a) but a request that the sentencing court (1) construe Special Condition (a) as not authorizing the specific CIMP provisions that exceed its text, consistent with *Kunz*'s own remedy of declaring one CIMP term unenforceable "as written" while affirming the judgment as construed, and/or (2) modify or clarify the conditions under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2), which the Second Circuit has expressly recognized as available "at any time" for this kind of dispute over the scope of a probation officer's already-delegated authority — a defendant need not first violate a disputed term and litigate it in a revocation proceeding. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 774 n.14 (citing Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's notes; *United States v. Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d 147, 153 (2d Cir. 2020)).

## 5. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### 5.1 Issue A: Is Special Condition (a) itself a valid delegation?

**Rule.** "The power to impose special conditions of supervised release ... is vested exclusively in the district court." A district court may delegate to Probation "authority over certain minor details of supervised release," but may not delegate "decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *United States v. Birkedahl*, 973 F.3d 49, 54 (2d Cir. 2020), as quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 765; accord *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122 (as quoted in *Kunz* and *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 509–10 & n.20). Whether a given detail is "minor" or instead a "significant liberty interest" requiring the court's own particularized findings is a contextual question. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 765–66.

**Application.** Special Condition (a) in Mr. Bernecky's Judgment is, word for word, the special condition *Kunz* construed. Compare Special Condition (a) (as quoted in my assignment instructions) with *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 757–58 (quoting App'x 73) — the two texts are identical in every clause, including the "consistent with the computer monitoring policy in effect by the probation office" language that Kunz specifically challenged as an open-ended delegation. The Second Circuit held that this language does *not* itself constitute an impermissible delegation, but only when construed to authorize Probation to make **future unilateral changes only to the extent those changes are "minor details ... already within Probation's purview,"** and not changes that "cross the threshold from minor detail into major imposition on [the defendant's] liberty." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 772–73 (citing *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122).

**Conclusion on Issue A.** A frontal challenge to Special Condition (a) itself — i.e., an argument that the special-condition language is *facially* an unconstitutional delegation — is squarely foreclosed by *Kunz* as to this identical text. Any relief here has to come from Issue B: identifying which *specific* CIMP terms exceed what that already-validated delegation actually authorizes.

### 5.2 Issue B: Do specific CIMP Agreement items exceed Special Condition (a)'s delegated scope?

**Rule, synthesized from *Kunz*, *Peterson*, *Boles*, and *Carlineo*.** A CIMP or similar implementing-agreement term survives delegation scrutiny only if it can fairly be read as a "minor detail" that implements the sentencing court's own condition — i.e., something "naturally" within the scope of what the special condition's text already authorizes, serving the purpose the special condition itself identifies (chiefly, preventing evasion of monitoring). *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 765–66. A term fails this test, and "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court," when it (1) imposes a restriction the special condition's text does not itself authorize, and (2) burdens a "significant liberty interest" such that it would, if imposed directly, require the court's own particularized findings. *Id.* at 771–73 (striking the "one Internet-capable device" CIMP term on this basis); *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 123 (as quoted in *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 537) (choice between in-patient and out-patient treatment is not a minor detail because it "impose[s] significantly greater restrictions" on liberty). A term separately fails if it is so open-ended that "the ultimate discretion and authority to figure out what the special condition requires is left with the Probation Office" without any defined standard — the vice identified in *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 537–38, and in the "risk" condition struck down in *Boles*, 914 F.3d at 111–12 (quoting *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 86, for the rule that Probation may not be given "unfettered discretion"). Separately, under *Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d at 190, 192–93, any computer-monitoring condition — including its implementing details — must be "narrowly tailored, and not sweep so broadly as to draw a wide swath of extraneous material into its net," and under *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 536, and *United States v. Simmons*, 343 F.3d 72, 81 (2d Cir. 2003) (as quoted in *Carlineo*), a condition must be clear enough that "a reasonable person" is not left "guessing as to its meaning."

I apply this framework item by item below. For the items that are verbatim or near-verbatim identical to CIMP language *Kunz* already construed, I apply *Kunz*'s own construction rather than re-deriving one.

#### 5.2.1 Items that mirror language *Kunz* already construed and upheld — valid, but *only* as narrowly construed

Bernecky Agreement Item 6 ("notify ... of any alterations ... prior to executing change") is materially identical to Kunz CIMP ¶7; Item 14 (evidence-cleaning/defragmentation) mirrors Kunz CIMP ¶15; Item 15 ("obtain permission ... prior to ALL computer repairs and/or alterations") is verbatim identical to Kunz CIMP ¶16 (including the capitalized "ALL"); Item 16 (anti-virus/firewall pre-authorization) mirrors Kunz CIMP ¶17; Item 17 ("shall not use any type of encryption unless pre-approved") is verbatim identical to Kunz CIMP ¶18; Item 20 (no OS alteration without approval) mirrors Kunz CIMP ¶21; and Item 12 (no IP-concealment/spoofing service) is verbatim identical to Kunz CIMP ¶13.

*Kunz* held that these kinds of terms are permissible "minor details" *only when read narrowly*: they reach only conduct Mr. Bernecky himself "actively initiates," not automatic, vendor-driven, or passive changes outside his control (68 F.4th at 763–64); the encryption restriction does not reach ordinary use of standard transport encryption (e.g., "https" websites, standard secure messaging) that does not impede Probation's monitoring capability (*id.* at 764–65); and none of these terms may be read to "demand the impossible" of an ordinary person (*id.* at 764, quoting *Johnson*, 446 F.3d at 281). **This is genuinely useful protection for Mr. Bernecky, but it is a construction, not the Agreement's literal text.** As written, several of these items (e.g., Item 15's unqualified "ALL...alterations," Item 17's unqualified "any type of encryption") would, read literally, sweep in conduct *Kunz* says they cannot reach. The practical risk is that W.D.N.Y. Probation enforces the literal text rather than the *Kunz* gloss. I recommend the Writer ask the court to make the *Kunz* construction explicit on this record (see Recommendations), rather than leaving Mr. Bernecky to rely on Probation's good-faith administration of an unwritten judicial gloss.

#### 5.2.2 Item 3 — device pre-authorization/"whitelist" requirement

> "I shall not use, own, or access any device which allows internet access other than what I have been authorized to use by the Probation Office. This includes, but is not limited to, internet appliances, mobile telephones, tablets, electronic games, gaming consoles, blu ray players, and SMART TV's."

Special Condition (a)'s text requires only that Mr. Bernecky give Probation *advance notification* of devices he will use, so that monitoring software can be installed, and that he participate in CIMP. It does not say that Probation must *authorize* — i.e., pre-approve — every device before use. Item 3 converts a notice-and-install regime into a prior-restraint/gatekeeping regime covering every internet-capable device in the household, expressly including ordinary consumer electronics (smart TVs, gaming consoles, Blu-ray players).

This is directly analogous to, and arguably broader than, the CIMP term the Second Circuit refused to enforce in *Kunz*: a restriction limiting the supervisee to "one Internet-capable device." The Second Circuit held that such a restriction "would pose a significant burden on his liberty, and therefore would need to be imposed by the court and justified by particularized on-the-record findings," and that "any special condition granting Probation discretion to decide whether or not to restrict a supervisee to a single internet-connected device would constitute an impermissible delegation of the court's judicial authority." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 773 (citing *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122–23). *Kunz* reasoned that Special Condition (a)'s plain text — which nowhere restricts device *number* — did not delegate that authority to Probation, so the CIMP term "exceed[ed] the authority actually delegated." *Id.*

Item 3 does something similar but through categorical device-*type* gatekeeping rather than a numerical cap: no device may be used, period, unless Probation has affirmatively authorized it. Special Condition (a)'s text supports notification-plus-monitoring, not case-by-case Probation pre-clearance of every device a person might own (a phone, a smart TV, a gaming console). Under *Kunz*'s own reasoning, this makes Mr. Bernecky's ability to use *any* device "contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion" — precisely what *Matta* forbids, as quoted in *Kunz*, *Browder*, and *Carlineo* alike.

#### 5.2.3 Item 9 — categorical ban on "encrypted email accounts"

> "I understand that I will not use any encrypted email accounts."

Unlike Item 17 (general encryption, "unless pre-approved"), Item 9 offers no pre-approval mechanism — it is an absolute, standalone prohibition. Special Condition (a) says nothing about email encryption specifically. Under *Kunz*'s reasoning about Item 17's cousin provision (CIMP ¶18), the Second Circuit went out of its way to hold that an encryption restriction cannot reasonably be read to reach ordinary transport encryption that a user does not affirmatively choose and that does not impede monitoring — for example, "https" websites or standard secure messaging services. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 764–65. Virtually every mainstream email provider (Gmail, Outlook, and the like) now encrypts mail in transit by default. Read literally, Item 9 would categorically forbid Mr. Bernecky from having *any* modern email account at all — a result that cannot be what Special Condition (a) (which never mentions email or encryption) actually authorizes, and that would fail *Carlineo*'s and *Simmons*'s vagueness/notice standard: a condition must not leave "a reasonable person" "guessing as to its meaning," *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 536, and must give "a person of ordinary intelligence a reasonable opportunity to know what is prohibited." *Simmons*, 343 F.3d at 81, as quoted in *Johnson*, 446 F.3d at 280. This item also sits in tension with Item 17's own built-in pre-approval safety valve for encryption generally, which underscores that its categorical, no-exceptions phrasing looks more like an oversight in drafting than a considered restriction Special Condition (a) actually delegated.

#### 5.2.4 Item 19 — third-party notification duty

> "I will notify all individuals who have access to my computer system/internet accessible device that it is subject to monitoring and/or search/seizure."

Neither Special Condition (a) nor Special Condition (f) says anything about a duty running to third parties (family members, roommates, coworkers who might share a household device). This is a freestanding obligation that Probation appears to have added on its own. The closest governing authority is *Peterson*, which held that a condition requiring the defendant to notify third parties (there, employers and schools) of risk information was, to the extent it required notification never authorized by the offense of conviction, "not authorized by statute," and that — separately — "the court must determine, rather than leaving to the discretion of the probation officer, whether such notification is required ... [and] may not simply leave the issues of ... notification to the probation officer's unfettered discretion." 248 F.3d at 85–86. *Boles* applied the same principle to vacate a "risk" condition that let Probation decide, standardless, when third-party notification was required. 914 F.3d at 111–12. Item 19 was not imposed by the court at all — it appears nowhere in Special Condition (a) or (f) — and it was inserted by Probation into its own agreement. That is a stronger delegation problem than *Peterson*'s and *Boles*'s, where at least the *category* of third-party notification had been ordered by the court and the dispute was about how much discretion Probation retained over its scope.

#### 5.2.5 Item 21 — broadened search-and-seizure trigger

> "I shall permit the seizure and removal of hardware and data storage media for further analysis by law enforcement or the probation officer based upon reasonable suspicion that a violation of a condition of supervision or unlawful conduct by any person has or is about to occur."

Special Condition (a)'s own search/seizure clause is narrower and more specific: it triggers examination "[a]s triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity" (i.e., activity the CIMP monitoring itself flags), covers "any computer equipment owned or used by the defendant," and is silent on any actor beyond "the U.S. Probation Office." Special Condition (f), the Judgment's general search condition, is broader in subject matter (person/property/vehicle/residence) but still keyed to "reasonable suspicion" concerning the defendant, based on his own conduct — not "unlawful conduct by any person."

Item 21 does three things neither special condition's text supports: (1) it decouples the seizure trigger from CIMP-monitoring-flagged "impermissible/suspicious activity" and substitutes a freestanding "reasonable suspicion of a violation ... or unlawful conduct by any person" standard; (2) it extends that trigger to conduct by *any person*, not just Mr. Bernecky; and (3) it adds "law enforcement" as an independent actor authorized to seize and analyze the equipment, not just Probation. This is a materially different — and broader — search-and-seizure regime than either special condition actually states. Under the same reasoning *Kunz* applied to the device-limitation term, a provision that is not supported by the special condition's actual text, and that affects a core liberty/privacy interest (seizure of a person's computer equipment based on a standard the court never wrote), "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 773.

#### 5.2.6 The Agreement's opening catch-all clause — weaker, unresolved candidate

> "I agree to comply with all program rules set forth in this agreement, and the instructions of my probation officer."

This open-ended catch-all — binding Mr. Bernecky to whatever "instructions" his probation officer gives, with no defined subject-matter limit — resembles the kind of standardless discretion *Boles* found problematic in the "risk" condition (914 F.3d at 111–12) and that *Carlineo* found problematic generally (998 F.3d at 537–38: Probation may not be left to "fill in too many blanks" with "open-ended discretion"). However, a nearly identical clause appeared in the treatment agreement at issue in *Browder* ("failure to comply with its provisions or the instructions of my officer will be considered a violation"), and the Second Circuit did not need to reach a delegation problem with that specific language because Browder's actual complaint concerned a different, conflicting term, and because Browder had "waived any delegation challenge." 866 F.3d at 509–10. I did not find a case squarely resolving whether this kind of general "follow my instructions" catch-all is itself a delegation problem when nothing more specific is at issue. I flag this as the weakest of the five candidates — worth raising, but resting on extension of the doctrine rather than a case matching it as closely as *Kunz* matches Items 3 and the encryption/alteration items.

### 5.3 What about the rest of the Agreement (Items 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 13, 18)?

These items generally implement Special Condition (a)'s actual text (advance notification of devices, Item 2; consent to install monitoring software, Item 1) or serve the clearly-related purpose of preventing evasion of the monitoring the special condition itself authorizes (no tampering with monitoring, Item 7; no booting an unmonitored OS, Item 8; no misleading profiles or use of another's account, Items 10–11; no in-private browsing or proxying internet access for another, Items 5, 13; tamper-resistant port tape, Item 18). Under *Kunz*'s framework, these read as the kind of "minor detail" implementation of an already-validated monitoring condition that Probation may permissibly administer — I did not identify a strong delegation argument against these on the current record, though I have not stress-tested each one against Mr. Bernecky's actual, individual circumstances (which I do not have).

### 5.4 Ripeness

*Kunz* and *Villafane-Lozada* draw a sharp line: a challenge to authority Probation has *already been delegated* — i.e., to the text of a standing condition or agreement — is ripe now, even before Probation has invoked it in a specific instance, because "it is the probation officer's already-granted authority" that is being challenged, not "some hypothetical decision that this delegation might allow in the future." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 769–70 (quoting *Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d at 151). By contrast, a challenge to *how* Probation might exercise retained, permissibly-delegated discretion in some future, unspecified instance is not yet ripe. *Id.* at 770–71 (citing *Balon*, 384 F.3d at 46–47; *Traficante*, 966 F.3d at 106). Items 3, 9, 19, and 21 are standing textual obligations in a signed agreement, not hypothetical future exercises of otherwise-valid discretion — under *Kunz*'s own ripeness analysis, a challenge to these terms as written is ripe now, and Mr. Bernecky need not wait to be charged with violating one of them. This is reinforced by *Kunz*'s own citation to the Rule 32.1 advisory committee's notes: a probationer should be able to "obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 774 n.14.

### 5.5 § 3583(e)(2) as the procedural vehicle — and its limits

Section 3583(e)(2) authorizes the court to "modify, reduce, or enlarge the conditions of supervised release, at any time prior to the expiration or termination of the term of supervised release." *Kunz* expressly identifies this as the avenue for a dispute of exactly this kind, alongside recourse to the sentencing court under Rule 32.1. 68 F.4th at 774 n.14. There is a documented limit worth flagging honestly: *Kunz*'s own footnote observes that, "generally, the illegality of a condition of supervised release is not a proper ground for modification under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2) ... [t]here is an exception to this rule for new or unforeseen circumstances, which include, among other things, technological changes." *Id.* (citing *Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d at 152 & n.2) (internal quotation marks omitted; this is a direct quotation from *Kunz*'s own opinion text, which I read in full, even though the underlying rule traces back to *Villafane-Lozada*, which I did not read directly). This caveat is squarely about *Section 3583(e)(2) modification* as a vehicle for re-litigating an already-and-finally-decided condition's *legality*. It is less clearly a bar here, because the theory advanced above is not that Special Condition (a) itself was illegally imposed (which *Kunz* forecloses in any event), but that specific CIMP *items* were never authorized by Special Condition (a) *in the first place* — a scope/construction dispute, which *Kunz* itself resolved for one term via direct construction of the judgment on appeal, not via a § 3583(e)(2) modification motion. This distinction should be made explicitly in the motion: the request is that the court **construe** the existing judgment (confirming what was and was not delegated) and, only secondarily and to the extent necessary, **modify or clarify** the conditions under § 3583(e)(2) so the record reflects that construction. Framing it as pure "modification of an illegal condition" risks the *Villafane-Lozada* limitation the Writer should be aware of and work around.

## 6. CONCLUSION

On the current record, I believe Mr. Bernecky has the better of the argument on Issue B as to at least three of the CIMP Agreement's 21 items — **Item 3** (device pre-authorization), **Item 19** (third-party notification duty), and **Item 21** (broadened search/seizure trigger) — each of which lacks any textual anchor in Special Condition (a) or (f) and each of which, under *Kunz*'s own reasoning applied to materially identical special-condition text from this same district, "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court." My confidence in this conclusion is **moderate-to-high** for Items 3 and 21 (close structural analogy to the specific CIMP term *Kunz* itself struck down) and **moderate** for Item 19 (strong analogy to *Peterson*'s third-party-notification holding, but no case addressing a probation-drafted, court-silent third-party duty of exactly this kind). I have **lower-moderate** confidence in Item 9 (real vagueness/overbreadth problem, but arguably curable by the same narrowing-construction technique *Kunz* applied to the general encryption provision, rather than requiring the term's outright invalidation) and **low** confidence in the opening catch-all clause (a real concern, but resting on extension rather than direct authority).

I have **low confidence** that a broad challenge to Special Condition (a) itself, or to the "policy in effect" delegation generally, would succeed — *Kunz* forecloses that theory as to this identical text, and the government's strongest response to the whole motion will likely be to point to *Kunz*'s general blessing of the CIMP scheme and try to characterize every disputed item as just another "minor detail." The Writer's strongest position is therefore not "the CIMP Agreement is unlawful" but "*Kunz* itself supplies the test, and applying that test to *these specific, previously-unexamined items* — not the ones *Kunz* already blessed — produces the same result *Kunz* reached for the one term it did strike down."
