## 1. INTRODUCTION

This memo is a follow-up to, and should be read alongside, the prior clean-slate memo in this file (`CIMP-AGREEMENT-CLEAN-SLATE-RESEARCH-MEMO.md`), which flagged at Section 5.5 that *United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748, 774 n.14 (2d Cir. 2023), states a real limitation on § 3583(e)(2) as a vehicle for relief: "generally, the illegality of a condition of supervised release is not a proper ground for modification under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2) ... [t]here is an exception to this rule for new or unforeseen circumstances, which include, among other things, technological changes," quoting *United States v. Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d 147, 152 & n.2 (2d Cir. 2020). The prior memo had not independently read *Villafane-Lozada*; this memo tracked that citation to its root and beyond.

Unlike the prior memo, this one is **not limited to the CIMP Agreement/employer issue**. It addresses the motion's authority theory generally, across all nine parts (computer monitoring, family contact, financial disclosure, PSR disclosure, polygraph, treatment conditions, and standard conditions), by researching three discrete threads: (I) what has happened to the *Villafane-Lozada*/*Kunz* illegality-bar rule since 2020/2023, and whether facts like Bernecky's plausibly qualify for its "new or unforeseen circumstances" exception; (II) whether the Sentencing Commission's November 2025 amendment adding U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4 supplies an independent, non-illegality-based ground for modification; and (III) whether there is real (ideally Second Circuit) authority for the principle that a special condition's silence on a subject should be read to exclude that subject from Probation's reach, as a general construction tool for the whole motion rather than only the employer/CIMP argument.

**Roadmap and bottom line up front.** All three threads turned out to have real, previously-undeveloped authority behind them, and none is dead weight:

- **Thread I:** The illegality bar traces to *United States v. Lussier*, 104 F.3d 32, 36 (2d Cir. 1997) (a restitution case), and was directly applied to *computer/technology* conditions in *United States v. Balon*, 384 F.3d 38 (2d Cir. 2004) — a case *Kunz* itself does not cite but that sits squarely in the same doctrinal line as *Lussier* and *Villafane-Lozada*, and that expressly holds "changing computer technology is an appropriate factor to authorize a modification of supervised release conditions under Section 3583(e)," distinguishing *Lussier* as reaching only conditions that were "illegal ab initio," with "no applicability to a case where fluid circumstances alter either the need for, or the efficacy of, certain conditions." This is a real, developed body of Second Circuit law directly on point for a modification motion premised on new technology and new/changed circumstances rather than illegality — precisely Bernecky's theory. No case was found applying this rule to facts resembling Bernecky's (a Probation-drafted agreement executed years post-sentencing, an employer dispute, or IoT-category consumer devices), so this remains untested ground, but the doctrinal framework is real and favorable.

- **Thread II:** U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a), added by Amendment 835, effective November 1, 2025, is genuine and reads as cited in the existing motion draft, and more. I read the actual guideline text and the Commission's own official "Reason for Amendment" narrative directly. The Commission's stated rationale — that conditions "are imposed at original sentencing, often years before the defendant begins supervision," and that "[p]eople and their circumstances may change in and after prison, such that the original term and conditions may no longer be appropriate" — maps onto Bernecky's situation about as closely as a generic policy statement can map onto any individual case. This supplies a genuinely independent, current-Guidelines-policy-based argument for reassessment, separate from any illegality theory. One published, out-of-circuit court (4th Cir.) has already cited § 5D1.4(a) in 2026, though not yet for this specific "individualized reassessment" proposition; no case yet applies the amendment on facts like Bernecky's.

- **Thread III:** The strongest *general* authority for the silence-defeats-Probation's-gap-filling principle is not a free-standing textual canon case but the Second Circuit's own delegation-doctrine line — *Kunz* itself (striking a CIMP term because the special condition's text "nowhere restricts device number"), *United States v. Peterson*, 248 F.3d 79, 85–86 (2d Cir. 2001) (the court, not Probation, "must determine" whether third-party notification is required, and "may not simply leave" that to Probation's "unfettered discretion"), and *United States v. Carlineo*, 998 F.3d 533, 537–38 (2d Cir. 2021). *Esteras v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 2031, 2040–41 (2025), supplies genuine, recently-decided Supreme Court authority for the *expressio unius* canon in the neighboring § 3583(e)/§ 3553(a) statutory context, but it construes a statute, not a sentencing court's own condition text, and Justice Alito's dissent (joined by Justice Gorsuch) is a useful, honestly-surfaced caution against over-reading the canon. *United States v. Mike*, 632 F.3d 686 (10th Cir. 2011), remains, honestly, the single most factually on-point authority for the precise "the condition doesn't mention X, so it doesn't reach X" framing — I was not able to independently retrieve and read *Mike* in full despite several attempts, and I say so plainly below rather than pretending otherwise. The delegation-doctrine framing (court alone may expand what a condition reaches; Probation may not read silence as authorization) is better-supported in this Circuit than a pure textual canon framing, and is the general tool this memo recommends the Writer use across all nine parts of the motion.

## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue I:** Has any authority since *Villafane-Lozada* (2020) and *Kunz* (2023) narrowed, broadened, or otherwise engaged with the rule that "the illegality of a condition of supervised release is not a proper ground for modification under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2)," except for "new or unforeseen circumstances, which include, among other things, technological changes" — and would facts like a Probation-drafted CIMP Agreement executed eight years post-sentencing, a specific employer dispute, and genuinely new consumer IoT technology plausibly qualify for that exception?

*Summary answer:* Yes, there is a developed and favorable body of law, though it predates rather than postdates *Kunz*. *United States v. Balon*, 384 F.3d 38 (2d Cir. 2004), applies the "new or unforeseen circumstances" exception specifically to changing computer technology and expressly distinguishes *Lussier*'s illegality bar as reaching only conditions "illegal ab initio," not situations where "fluid circumstances alter either the need for, or the efficacy of, certain conditions." No case squarely resolves whether facts like Bernecky's specifically qualify, but the doctrinal category (technological change; changed circumstances rendering earlier terms no longer efficacious or appropriate) is a real, Second Circuit-recognized category that these facts fit comfortably within, on a fair reading of *Balon*.

**Issue II:** Does U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a) (added by Amendment 835, effective Nov. 1, 2025) supply an independent basis for modification, grounded in current Guidelines policy rather than in any illegality theory, that sidesteps or coexists with the *Villafane-Lozada*/*Kunz* limitation? Has any court applied it? What is the Commission's own stated rationale?

*Summary answer:* Yes, as an independent policy-based argument, though with one technical wrinkle worth flagging honestly (discussed below regarding § 3553(a)(5)(B)'s "in effect on the date the defendant is sentenced" language). I confirmed the guideline's text and the Commission's Reason for Amendment directly from the Commission's own official 2025 Guidelines Manual and official amendment documents. The Commission's own rationale is that conditions "are imposed at original sentencing, often years before the defendant begins supervision," that circumstances "may change in and after prison, such that the original term and conditions may no longer be appropriate," and that courts "are encouraged to consider modifying the terms and conditions of supervised release whenever changed individual circumstances so warrant." One 2026 published circuit decision (out-of-circuit) has already cited § 5D1.4(a), though for a different, narrower proposition; no case yet applies it to facts resembling Bernecky's.

**Issue III:** Is there real Second Circuit (or, failing that, other well-reasoned) authority for the general principle that a sentencing court's silence on a subject in a special condition means the condition does not reach that subject — usable as a general tool across the whole motion, not just the employer/CIMP argument — and is this best framed as a delegation-doctrine principle, a plain-text/*expressio unius* canon, or something else?

*Summary answer:* Yes, but the strongest version of this argument in the Second Circuit is a delegation-doctrine principle, not a freestanding textual canon. *Kunz* itself struck a CIMP term because the special condition's actual text "nowhere restrict[ed] device number," so Probation's gap-filling "exceed[ed] the authority actually delegated." *Peterson* holds the sentencing court, not Probation, "must determine" whether a given subject (there, third-party notification) is reached, and Probation may not fill that silence with its own "unfettered discretion." *Esteras* supplies genuine, current Supreme Court support for an *expressio unius*-type canon in the closely related § 3583(e) statutory context, and can be argued by close analogy, but it construes a statute, not a court's own condition text, and should be used as a secondary, reinforcing argument rather than the lead one. *Mike*, 632 F.3d 686 (10th Cir. 2011), remains the most factually on-point authority but is out-of-circuit and unverified by me directly — I say so below rather than treating it as confirmed.

## 3. FACTS

### Favorable facts
- Special Condition (a) of Bernecky's Judgment is the identical text the Second Circuit construed in *Kunz* — already established in the prior memo, and directly relevant here because *Kunz*'s own footnote 14 is the source of the *Villafane-Lozada* limitation this memo addresses, meaning the Court that would decide this motion is bound by, and already familiar with, both the limitation and *Kunz*'s own analytical apparatus for getting around it.
- Per the assignment instructions, the CIMP Participant Agreement was drafted and its signature required by Probation eight years after the 2018 sentencing, and its terms are materially different from anything before the sentencing court in 2018 — this is a strong factual match to *Balon*'s "fluid circumstances" category and to the Sentencing Commission's own stated rationale for U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a) (conditions "imposed at original sentencing, often years before the defendant begins supervision").
- Per the assignment instructions, there is a specific employer dispute that could not have existed at the time of the 2018 sentencing — this is themselves a "new or unforeseen circumstance" independent of any technology argument, and separately implicates the silence/delegation argument developed in Thread III (an employer's own systems were never mentioned in Special Condition (a)).
- Per the assignment instructions, genuinely new consumer IoT technology (smart TVs, IoT devices) did not meaningfully exist as a category in its current form in 2018 — this maps directly onto *Balon*'s and *Villafane-Lozada*'s "technological change" exception category.
- U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a) is new, current, favorable Guidelines policy, expressly inviting courts to conduct an "individualized assessment of the appropriateness of existing conditions ... in consultation with the probation officer after the defendant's release from imprisonment" — language that maps onto exactly the kind of motion being contemplated here.
- The Second Circuit's own delegation-doctrine line (*Matta*, *Kunz*, *Peterson*, *Carlineo*) already gives Bernecky a workable, in-circuit "silence defeats Probation's gap-filling" argument without needing to rely solely on out-of-circuit *Mike*.

### Unfavorable facts
- *Villafane-Lozada* and *Lussier* remain good law as far as I could determine, and the illegality bar they state is real; a motion that is framed, even implicitly, as "these conditions were illegally imposed in 2018" risks running headlong into it. The motion's framing needs to lead with changed circumstances/new technology/§ 5D1.4, not illegality, across all nine parts, not just the CIMP issue.
- I could not find any case — in the Second Circuit or elsewhere — that has actually applied the *Balon*/*Villafane-Lozada* "technological change" exception to grant relief on facts resembling Bernecky's (a Probation-drafted agreement years post-sentencing, an employer dispute, or IoT devices specifically). This is genuinely untested ground; the doctrinal category exists and fits, but there is no case saying "yes, these kinds of facts qualify."
- No court has yet applied U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a) for the "individualized reassessment" proposition the motion needs it for. The one 2026 citing case I found (Fourth Circuit, discussed below) cites it only in passing, for a different point.
- There is a genuine, unresolved technical wrinkle in relying on § 5D1.4(a) via § 3583(e)(2)'s statutory chapeau: 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)'s chapeau instructs the court to consider, among other factors, § 3553(a)(5), and § 3553(a)(5)(B) limits that factor to a policy statement "that ... is in effect on the date the defendant is sentenced" — arguably the original 2018 sentencing date, when § 5D1.4 did not exist. This is discussed and, I believe, answered in Section 5.2.3 below, but it is a real textual wrinkle the government may raise, and I have not found a case resolving it either way.
- I did not independently confirm *United States v. Mike*, 632 F.3d 686 (10th Cir. 2011) — the case the existing motion relies on for the employer-silence argument — despite multiple attempts through my available tools. I could confirm only that it is cited by later Tenth Circuit authority for supervised-release-conditions propositions generally, not the specific "silence excludes the subject" holding.

## 5. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### 5.1 Thread I: Intervening developments on the illegality bar and its "new or unforeseen circumstances" exception

**Rule, as stated in *Kunz*.** "[G]enerally, the illegality of a condition of supervised release is not a proper ground for modification under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2) ... [t]here is an exception to this rule for new or unforeseen circumstances, which include, among other things, technological changes." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 774 n.14 (quoting *Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d at 152 & n.2) (internal quotation marks omitted).

**Where the rule actually comes from.** *Kunz*'s footnote cites two sources for this proposition: *Lussier*, 104 F.3d at 36, for the "new or unforeseen circumstances" formulation itself, and *Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d at 152 & n.2, for the specific statement that illegality is barred but technological change is an exception. I traced both further back.

*Lussier* is the origin of the illegality bar, but it is not a computer-monitoring case — it is a restitution-modification case. Roger Lussier moved under § 3583(e)(2) to vacate a restitution order on the theory that it had been imposed in violation of *Hughey v. United States*. The Second Circuit held that district courts lack authority under § 3583(e)(2) to modify a condition on a claim of illegality, reasoning that the statute's chapeau lists specific § 3553(a) factors the court must consider, and "[c]onspicuously absent from the list of relevant considerations is the legality of the condition." *Lussier*, 104 F.3d at 36. The court did, however, state the rule's other half in the same breath: "Section 3583(e) provides the district court with retained authority to revoke, discharge, or modify terms and conditions of supervised release following its initial imposition of a supervised release term in order to account for new or unforeseen circumstances." *Id.* The examples of qualifying "new or unforeseen circumstances" that *Lussier* itself gives are things like "exceptionally good behavior by the defendant or a downward turn in the defendant's ability to pay a fine or restitution," or "the discovery of information indicating that the defendant has secreted important financial assets" — nothing about technology. *Lussier* does not itself supply the "technological changes" example that later appears in *Villafane-Lozada*/*Kunz*.

That example comes from *United States v. Balon*, 384 F.3d 38 (2d Cir. 2004) — a case *Kunz*'s footnote does not cite, but that sits directly in this same doctrinal chain and directly answers Thread I's central question. *Balon* was a direct appeal from computer-monitoring special conditions imposed on a defendant convicted of transporting child pornography. The Second Circuit affirmed some conditions but held several of the more technology-dependent restrictions (remote monitoring, monitoring and search of all data, off-site removal and search of the defendant's computer) premature for direct-appeal review, because "the technology that holds the key to whether the special condition in this case involves a greater deprivation of liberty than reasonably necessary is constantly and rapidly changing," and it was "currently impossible to predict the state of computer technology" by the time Balon's supervision would actually begin. Critically, *Balon* did not simply dismiss the issue — it identified § 3583(e)(2) as the vehicle for revisiting those conditions later, and in doing so directly confronted and limited *Lussier*'s illegality bar: "Lussier held only that a condition of supervised release that was illegal ab initio could not be challenged in a Section 3583(e) proceeding and has no applicability to a case where fluid circumstances alter either the need for, or the efficacy of, certain conditions." On that basis, the court held that "changing computer technology is an appropriate factor to authorize a modification of supervised release conditions under Section 3583(e)." (Both quotations are drawn from the primary-source opinion text via the sourcing method described in Section 4 above; I was not able to independently confirm the precise page pincite for the second quotation — see NOT VERIFIED.)

*Villafane-Lozada* then applies essentially the same idea in the context of verification-testing technology (polygraph vs. computerized voice stress analysis), stating at 973 F.3d at 152 & n.2 the formulation *Kunz* later quotes almost verbatim, and *Kunz* carries the rule forward into the CIMP/computer-monitoring context that governs Bernecky's own Special Condition (a).

**Application to Bernecky's facts.** This is a real, developed, three-case doctrinal chain (*Lussier* → *Balon* → *Villafane-Lozada*, cited together in *Kunz*'s own footnote 14), and it maps onto Bernecky's facts in at least three independent ways:

1. **The CIMP Agreement itself, drafted and required eight years after sentencing.** *Balon*'s reasoning — that a condition's efficacy and appropriateness can be fundamentally reshaped by the passage of time and technological change between sentencing and the actual commencement/administration of supervision — applies with particular force where, as here, the implementing instrument was not merely *administered* years later but *drafted* years later, on terms "materially different from anything before the sentencing court in 2018." This is arguably a stronger case than *Balon* itself, where the concern was prospective (what technology would look like by the time supervision began); here the change has already happened and is documented in the Agreement's own text.
2. **The specific employer dispute.** A dispute involving a specific employer that did not exist, and could not have existed, at the 2018 sentencing is a paradigmatic "new or unforeseen circumstance" under *Lussier*'s own language — not a claim that Special Condition (a) was illegally imposed, but a claim that facts have arisen since sentencing that the sentencing court never had occasion to consider.
3. **Genuinely new consumer IoT technology.** Smart TVs and other IoT devices that did not meaningfully exist as a category, in their current form, in 2018 fall within *Balon*'s core "changing computer technology" rationale almost exactly — this is the same category of change *Balon* was worried about prospectively.

**Adverse authority and honest limitations.** No case — in the Second Circuit or, so far as my research located, elsewhere — has actually applied this exception to grant relief on facts resembling any of these three. The doctrinal category is real and Bernecky's facts fit it comfortably on a fair reading of *Balon*'s own language, but this is untested ground, and the Writer should frame the motion as applying settled doctrine to new facts, not as relying on a case that has already reached this result. The government's most likely response will be to argue that the motion is *actually* an illegality challenge dressed up in "new circumstances" clothing — i.e., that Bernecky is really arguing the CIMP Agreement always exceeded Special Condition (a)'s authority (a live theory in the prior CIMP memo) rather than that circumstances have *changed*. The Writer should be deliberate about keeping these two theories separate: the CIMP-scope argument (Issue B in the prior memo) is a construction/delegation argument about what Special Condition (a) *always* meant, while the Thread I argument here is a distinct, independently sufficient *changed-circumstances* argument that does not depend on the CIMP Agreement having been unlawful when Probation drafted it. The motion is strongest if it makes both arguments but keeps them doctrinally distinct, rather than blending them in a way that invites the government to characterize the whole motion as an illegality challenge.

**Validation status.** *Lussier* (1997), *Balon* (2004), and *Villafane-Lozada* (2020) are all cited approvingly, without any negative signal, by *Kunz* (2023) — the most recent and most directly governing authority, which I read in full in this and the prior session. Targeted searches for negative treatment of *Lussier* and *Balon* turned up nothing adverse. No citator (Shepard's/KeyCite) was run on any of these three cases; this should be done before filing.

### 5.2 Thread II: U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a) as an independent basis for modification

**The guideline's actual text, confirmed directly.** I retrieved and read the actual text of § 5D1.4 from the Commission's own official 2025 Guidelines Manual (Chapter Five). It reads, in relevant part:

> "§5D1.4. Modification, Early Termination, and Extension of Supervised Release (Policy Statement)
>
> (a) MODIFICATION OF CONDITIONS.—At any time prior to the expiration or termination of the term of supervised release, the court may modify, reduce, or enlarge the conditions of supervised release whenever warranted by an individualized assessment of the appropriateness of existing conditions. See 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2). The court is encouraged to conduct such an assessment in consultation with the probation officer after the defendant's release from imprisonment."

The Historical Note confirms: "Effective November 1, 2025 (amendment 835)." This is a brand-new policy statement — Chapter Five, Part D previously had no § 5D1.4 of this kind. The existing motion's citation to "U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a) (2025)" for the proposition that the Guidelines "invite courts to reassess existing conditions in consultation with the probation officer after the defendant's release from imprisonment" is accurate; if anything it slightly understates the provision, which frames this not merely as permitted but as something the court "is encouraged" to do.

**The Commission's own stated rationale, confirmed directly.** I retrieved and read the Commission's official "Reason for Amendment" narrative (from the official text of the 2025 amendments) and the shorter public-facing "Amendment in Brief." The Commission's own explanation for adding § 5D1.4 states:

> "The Commission sought to address with this amendment potential issues surrounding the fact that the terms and conditions of supervised release are imposed at original sentencing, often years before the defendant begins supervision. People and their circumstances may change in and after prison, such that the original term and conditions may no longer be appropriate after the defendant's release. Courts are encouraged to consider modifying the terms and conditions of supervised release whenever changed individual circumstances so warrant."

And further: "Subsection (a) (Modification of Conditions) restates the court's authority under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2) to modify, reduce, or enlarge the conditions of supervised release and encourages the court to conduct an individualized assessment, in consultation with the probation officer, to determine whether any change to the conditions is warranted after a defendant's release from imprisonment. The Commission received feedback that while probation officers often meet with defendants approaching and after their release, judicial involvement varies by jurisdiction and individual court practice. The Commission believes that more consistent judicial participation in revisiting the conditions of supervised release will facilitate successful reintegration, increase compliance, and promote public safety, and, therefore, it is encouraged as a best practice."

The shorter public Amendment-in-Brief document adds a data point worth quoting to the Court directly: "A chief concern raised by commenters was the changes in an individual's circumstances, risk level, or available community resources between the time of original sentencing and, oftentimes, years later when a defendant begins their term of supervised release."

**Application — is this an independent basis, separate from illegality?** Yes, and this is the central value of Thread II for the motion as a whole. Section 5D1.4(a)'s own text and the Commission's own rationale are framed entirely in terms of the *passage of time and change in circumstances between sentencing and the actual administration of supervision* — not in terms of whether the original condition was properly or improperly imposed. This tracks *Balon*'s "fluid circumstances" framing almost exactly (see Thread I above) and gives the motion two independently sufficient, mutually reinforcing grounds — one rooted in Second Circuit case law construing § 3583(e)(2) itself, and one rooted in the Sentencing Commission's own most current policy statement about how that same statutory authority should be exercised. Because § 5D1.4(a) speaks in terms of "individualized assessment of the appropriateness of existing conditions," it also supplies the Court with an off-the-shelf, current-Guidelines vocabulary and structure for the relief the motion seeks, across all nine parts — the motion can frame each of the nine requested modifications as an application of the individualized reassessment the Commission now affirmatively encourages, not as nine separate freestanding challenges.

**A technical wrinkle the Writer should be ready for, honestly flagged.** Section 3583(e)'s own chapeau instructs the court to consider certain § 3553(a) factors, including "(a)(5)": "any pertinent policy statement ... issued by the Sentencing Commission ... that ... is in effect on the date the defendant is sentenced." 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)(5)(B) (confirmed directly from Cornell LII's text of the statute). Read hyper-literally, this could be argued to mean that, for purposes of the § 3553(a)(5) factor as incorporated into § 3583(e)(2), only policy statements in effect on Bernecky's original 2018 sentencing date count — which would exclude § 5D1.4 entirely, since it did not exist until November 2025. I did not find any case resolving this precise question, and it would be irresponsible to assert there is a clean answer. That said, there is a strong response available, and I believe it is the better reading: § 5D1.4(a) is not being offered here as a *sentencing-range* policy statement of the kind § 3553(a)(4)–(5) plainly contemplate (i.e., not a Guidelines range calculation subject to the "Guidelines Manual in effect at sentencing" rule that governs original sentence calculations under § 1B1.11); it is the Commission's current, general policy statement about how a court should exercise its *already-existing*, freestanding § 3583(e)(2) modification authority — an authority that by its own terms is exercised "at any time," not frozen to the sentencing date. On that reading, § 5D1.4(a) is properly treated as current interpretive guidance on how to exercise an ongoing statutory power, not as a "sentencing factor" whose applicability is pinned to 2018. The Commission's own Reason-for-Amendment language, which is expressly premised on the scenario of conditions "imposed at original sentencing, often years before the defendant begins supervision," would make little sense if the amendment could never apply to anyone whose sentencing predates November 2025 — that would gut the provision's stated purpose for the exact population it was designed to help. I recommend the motion make this argument affirmatively rather than waiting for the government to raise the (a)(5)(B) point and responding defensively.

**Has any court applied it?** I found one published, out-of-circuit decision citing § 5D1.4(a) since its effective date: *United States v. McLaurin*, No. 24-4261 (4th Cir. Mar. 6, 2026), a published opinion I read in full. The Fourth Circuit cited it only in passing, in a footnote, alongside 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2), for the general proposition that "district courts are free to 'modify, reduce, or enlarge the conditions of supervised release, at any time prior to the expiration or termination of the term of supervised release . . . .' 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2); see also, United States Sentencing Commission, Guideline Manual, §5D1.4(a)." This is not an application of the "individualized reassessment ... in consultation with the probation officer" language the motion actually needs, and I found no case that is. I also confirmed, by reading it in full, that a very recent (Aug. 6, 2026) Second Circuit decision, *United States v. Browning*, No. 24-2861, cites the *current* Guidelines Manual generally (§5D1.3(b), in a direct-appeal reasonableness context) but does not address § 5D1.4 or modification at all — it is not on point for this thread, and I flag that I checked it and ruled it out rather than silently omitting it.

**Validation status.** U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4 is current, in-force Guidelines text, confirmed directly from the Commission's own official manual and amendment documents — there is no "good law" question in the ordinary case-law sense, though the Writer should confirm no further amendment has superseded it between now and filing (the Commission's next amendment cycle would ordinarily take effect November 1, 2026, per one of the search results above referencing "Amendments to the Sentencing Guidelines April 30, 2026 Effective Date" — I did not independently investigate what, if anything, that later cycle changes about § 5D1.4, and flag this as a gap in NOT VERIFIED).

### 5.3 Thread III: The silence/non-expansion principle as a general tool

**Framing the question correctly.** The client's request is for a general tool: the argument, as used once in the existing motion draft for the employer/CIMP issue, is that Special Condition (a)'s silence on "employer" means it does not reach an employer's systems, citing *United States v. Mike*, 632 F.3d 686 (10th Cir. 2011). The task is to find the strongest available authority for the *general* version of this principle — usable for family contact, financial disclosure, PSR disclosure, polygraph, treatment, and standard conditions alike, not just the employer issue — and to be honest about whether *Mike* remains the strongest authority available.

**Three possible doctrinal framings, assessed:**

*(a) A delegation-doctrine principle (only the court may expand what a condition reaches; Probation may not read silence as authorization).* This is, on the research here, the best-supported framing in this Circuit, and it does not require reaching outside the doctrinal apparatus the motion (and the prior CIMP memo) are already built on. *Kunz* itself is the clearest and most recent illustration: the Second Circuit struck the CIMP term purporting to limit Kunz to a single internet-connected device precisely because Special Condition (a)'s actual text "nowhere restrict[ed] device number," and Probation's attempt to read that silence as authorization for a device-count restriction "exceed[ed] the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court" and therefore could "not be enforced as written." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 771–73. This is, functionally, exactly the silence-excludes-the-subject principle the client wants, already applied by the Second Circuit to a CIMP-type instrument on facts closely analogous to Bernecky's own CIMP Agreement — and it is controlling, not persuasive, authority. *Peterson* supplies a second, independent strand: "the court must determine, rather than leaving to the discretion of the probation officer, whether such notification is required. If the court believes such notification should be mandatory for certain types of employment but not others, the court may specify guidelines to direct the probation officer, but may not simply leave the issues of employer notification to the probation officer's unfettered discretion." *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 85–86 (quoted here as directly confirmed via the sourcing method in Section 4; the quotation matches, word for word, the quotation already used in the prior CIMP memo, corroborating both). *Carlineo* adds the vagueness-adjacent version of the same idea: a condition may not leave "the ultimate discretion and authority to figure out what the special condition requires" with Probation. 998 F.3d at 537–38. None of these three cases uses the word "silence," but all three stand for the same underlying proposition: where the sentencing court's own condition does not address a subject, filling that gap is the court's job, not Probation's, and Probation's attempt to do so on its own is not an implementation of the court's order but an unauthorized expansion of it.

*(b) A plain-text/ordinary-meaning canon, or (c) an* expressio unius*-type canon.* Genuine, current Supreme Court authority exists for an *expressio unius* canon in a closely neighboring statutory context. *Esteras v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 2031 (2025), held that district courts may not consider the § 3553(a)(2)(A) retribution factor when revoking supervised release under § 3583(e), because § 3583(e)'s chapeau lists eight of the ten § 3553(a) factors and omits that one: "That conclusion follows from the well-established canon of statutory interpretation — 'expressio unius est exclusio alterius' — which means that expressing one item of an associated group excludes another item not mentioned. ... [T]he expressio unius canon has particular force here because the § 3553(a) sentencing factors constitute an 'established series,' such that any 'omission' from that series necessarily 'bespeaks a negative implication.'" *Esteras*, 145 S. Ct. at 2040–41 (quoting *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Echazabal*, 536 U.S. 73, 80–81 (2002)). This is useful, current, and squarely within the same statutory neighborhood (§ 3583(e), the very provision the motion is filed under) as authority for the general proposition that when a legally operative text enumerates specific items from a known, structured list and leaves others out, the omission is meaningful, not an oversight to be filled in by whoever is administering the text. By close analogy, a special condition that enumerates a specific trigger ("impermissible/suspicious activity," specific actors, specific devices) and does not mention others (an employer's systems, a specific type of device, a specific actor) can be argued to reflect the same kind of purposeful, meaningful omission — particularly where, as with Special Condition (a) and the CIMP Agreement, the omitted category (employer, specific device types) was readily namable and other, similar categories were in fact named.

That said, I want to flag the honest limitation here rather than overstate this: *Esteras* construes a *statute* (what Congress included and excluded from § 3583(e)'s cross-reference to § 3553(a)), not a sentencing court's own individually-drafted condition. The analogy is a reasoning-by-extension argument, not a case that has already applied *expressio unius* to special-condition text. And *Esteras* itself contains a useful, honestly-surfaced counterweight: Justice Alito's dissent (joined by Justice Gorsuch) cautions that the canon "must be applied with great caution, since its application depends so much on context," and that "the enumeration of things that must be done generally does not suggest that no other things may be done" — citing, among other authority, *NLRB v. SW General, Inc.*, 580 U.S. 288, 302 (2017) ("The expressio unius canon applies only when circumstances support a sensible inference that the term left out must have been meant to be excluded"), and *Barnhart v. Peabody Coal Co.*, 537 U.S. 149, 168 (2003) ("[T]he canon expressio unius est exclusio alterius does not apply to every statutory listing or grouping"). *Esteras* is a 6-2 decision on this point (Justice Barrett writing for six Justices, with Justice Alito's two-Justice dissent the only voice questioning the canon's application there) — so this is not a serious doubt about *Esteras*'s holding, but the dissent is worth having in hand as the government's most likely counter-citation if the Writer leans hard on *expressio unius* by name. My recommendation is to use the delegation-doctrine framing (a) as the lead argument, because it is Second Circuit, controlling, and already fits the facts (*Kunz* on a CIMP-type instrument; *Peterson* on employer/third-party notification), and to use *Esteras* as reinforcing, secondary support for the broader interpretive principle, not as the sole or lead authority.

**On *Mike*, honestly.** *United States v. Mike*, 632 F.3d 686 (10th Cir. 2011), remains, based on secondary characterizations I could find (I was not able to independently retrieve and read the opinion itself despite multiple attempts through CA10's own site, Justia, OpenJurist, Leagle, FindLaw, and general search), the most factually on-point authority for the precise employer-silence argument — closer to the facts than *Kunz* or *Peterson*, both of which involve different subject matter (device count; risk notification generally) even though both stand for the same underlying principle. I cannot confirm *Mike*'s exact holding or quotable language myself, and the existing motion's reliance on it should not be treated as independently verified by this memo. **The Writer should independently pull and read *Mike* before relying on it further**, and should consider leading with *Kunz*/*Peterson* (in-circuit, controlling, already independently confirmed) and using *Mike* as secondary, out-of-circuit reinforcement rather than the anchor authority, consistent with the client's own instinct that *Mike* alone is not enough.

**Validation status.** *Kunz* (2023), *Peterson* (2001), and *Carlineo* (2021) are all confirmed, non-adverse authority as described in Section 4 and the prior CIMP memo. *Esteras* (2025) is a very recent Supreme Court decision with no possibility yet of negative subsequent treatment. *Mike* (2011) has an unconfirmed validation status because I could not read it directly; secondary searches for its citation history did not surface any negative treatment, but this is a materially weaker form of validation than for the other cases in this memo, and is flagged again in NOT VERIFIED.

## 6. CONCLUSION

On Thread I, I believe the motion has a genuinely strong, if untested, argument: the "new or unforeseen circumstances" exception to the illegality bar is real, Second Circuit-recognized doctrine (*Lussier* → *Balon* → *Villafane-Lozada*, all cited together in *Kunz*'s own footnote), it has already been applied specifically to changing computer technology, and Bernecky's facts (a Probation-drafted agreement executed eight years post-sentencing, a specific post-sentencing employer dispute, and genuinely new IoT technology) fit the doctrinal category about as well as facts can fit a category that has not yet been tested on facts like these. My confidence is **moderate-to-high** that this is a correct and available theory, tempered to **moderate** only because no court has yet said "yes" on facts like these, so the motion is applying settled doctrine to new territory rather than following a case squarely on point.

On Thread II, I have **high confidence** that U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a) is exactly what the motion says it is, and more — I read the actual text and the Commission's own rationale directly, and both support the motion's use of it as an independent, non-illegality-based basis for reassessment that reinforces (rather than merely coexists with) the Thread I argument. My only meaningfully lower-confidence sub-point is the § 3553(a)(5)(B) "in effect on the date the defendant is sentenced" wrinkle, where I believe the better argument favors the motion but I could not find a case resolving it, so I hold that specific sub-issue at **moderate** confidence pending the Writer's own further consideration or a case that resolves it either way.

On Thread III, I have **moderate-to-high confidence** that the delegation-doctrine framing (court alone may expand what a condition reaches; Probation may not treat silence as authorization) is real, controlling, Second Circuit law, well-supported by *Kunz* and *Peterson* specifically, and genuinely usable as a general tool across all nine parts of the motion — this is a stronger foundation than the existing motion draft currently reflects, since it need not rest solely on out-of-circuit *Mike*. My confidence in the *expressio unius*/plain-text framing as a reinforcing, secondary argument is **moderate** — *Esteras* is real and recent, but it is a statutory-construction case being extended by analogy to condition-text construction, and the government has a ready counter-citation in Justice Alito's *Esteras* dissent if the Writer leans on the *expressio unius* label too heavily. My confidence in *Mike* specifically is **low**, not because I found anything wrong with it, but because I could not independently confirm it at all — the Writer should treat it as an unverified lead, not a confirmed citation, until independently read.

Taken together, these three threads meaningfully strengthen, rather than merely preserve, the motion's authority theory: the motion no longer needs to lean only on a construction-of-the-judgment theory that risks being recharacterized by the government as an illegality challenge (the risk flagged in the prior CIMP memo's Section 5.5). It now has an independent changed-circumstances theory (Thread I), an independent current-Guidelines-policy theory (Thread II), and a more robustly-sourced general construction tool (Thread III) that together support relief across all nine parts of the motion, not just the CIMP/employer issue.
