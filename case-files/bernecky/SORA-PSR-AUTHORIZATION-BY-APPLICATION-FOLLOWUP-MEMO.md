## 2. ISSUE AND SUMMARY CONCLUSION

**Issue.** *Charmer Industries* holds that a presentence report "may not
properly be disclosed without authorization by the court," and that a third
party seeking disclosure must show "a compelling need for disclosure ... to
meet the ends of justice." 711 F.2d at 1175–76. Bernecky's 2018 judgment
contains a special condition of supervised release, imposed at sentencing as
part of the standard conditions package, expressly authorizing PSR disclosure
to the Board. Does that standing, advance, non-individualized condition
satisfy *Charmer*'s "authorization by the court," or does *Charmer* require
something procedurally closer to what actually happened in *Charmer* itself —
a specific disclosure application, evaluated against a specific requester's
specific need, at or near the time of the actual disclosure?

**Summary conclusion.** This is a genuine, close question, and no court
appears to have decided it either way. The client's distinction is not
frivolous — *Charmer*'s own operative language ("the court should not release
a presentence report to a third person unless **that person** has shown a
compelling need," 711 F.2d at 1176 (emphasis added)) is built around an
identified requester making an individualized showing, and every fact pattern
*Charmer* itself discusses (its own case, and the string of authority it
cites) involves a live, contested, ad hoc request — never an advance,
categorical grant made at sentencing. But the government has a real
counterargument that is not frivolous either: the Administrative Office of the
U.S. Courts' own current governing policy on PSR disclosure (Guide to
Judiciary Policy, Vol. 8D, Ch. 5, § 530(a)), read and confirmed directly in
this session, treats "the respective sentencing court has granted
authorization to disclose such information at its discretion" as an
*independent, sufficient* basis for proper disclosure — textually separate
from, not a subset of, "the court determines that a compelling need for
disclosure has been demonstrated." That is the exact structure of Bernecky's
2018 condition, and it is a structure the very agency responsible for
administering PSR confidentiality treats as adequate on its own. On balance, I
would call this a close question that leans — modestly, not decisively —
toward the government, for the reasons developed below. It is not a question
this memorandum can resolve with confidence, because no court has resolved it.

## 3. FACTS

**Favorable to the client's distinction:**
- The 2018 judgment's special condition is, on its face, generic and advance:
  it was entered as one of many standard special conditions at sentencing, not
  in response to any request by the Board, and (so far as anyone has found)
  without any recitation of a compelling need, an ends-of-justice finding, or
  any individualized analysis of what the Board specifically needed and why.
- *Charmer*'s own operative holding language is requester-specific and
  showing-specific: "the court should not release a presentence report to a
  third person unless **that person** has shown a compelling need for
  disclosure to meet the ends of justice." 711 F.2d at 1176.
- Every disclosure scenario *Charmer* actually discusses — its own facts (the
  Arizona AG's request, the Probation Service's March 14 memorandum seeking
  "the court's approval to release the Peerless Report to the Arizona AG in
  connection with the license application proceedings," approval granted
  March 16, 1983), and the string of cases it cites (*Hancock Brothers*,
  *Figurski*, *Krause*, *Bernstein*) — involves a live, identified requester
  and an ad hoc request. *Charmer* itself states: "No reported case, and only
  one unreported case, has been called to our attention in which a court has
  ordered the disclosure of a presentence report to a third party." 711 F.2d
  at 1173–74 & n.10. The concept of a standing, advance authorization
  embedded in an original sentencing judgment is not something the *Charmer*
  panel had before it, discussed, or purported to resolve.
- *Charmer*'s reasoning is explicitly individualized and fact-specific: the
  panel's own application of the standard turned on parsing exactly which
  parts of the Peerless Report were and were not independently available to
  the Arizona AG, weighing the specific hardship of disclosure against the
  specific showing made. 711 F.2d at 1177–78. That kind of weighing is not
  something that can occur in the abstract, before any specific recipient's
  specific need for specific information is even before the court.

**Unfavorable to the client's distinction:**
- The Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts' own current governing policy
  — Guide to Judiciary Policy, Vol. 8D, Ch. 5, § 530(a), read directly and in
  full in this session — states that "[p]resentence information is
  confidential and may be disclosed to third parties or other agencies only
  if: (1) the respective sentencing court has granted authorization to
  disclose such information at its discretion; (2) the court determines that
  a compelling need for disclosure has been demonstrated; or (3) there exists
  explicit authority to disclose such information." This is phrased
  disjunctively ("only if" followed by an "or" list) — meaning the agency
  charged with administering PSR confidentiality across the federal system
  treats a court's advance, discretionary authorization as an *independently*
  sufficient basis, not merely as a preliminary step still requiring a
  compelling-need showing at the time of actual disclosure.
- Bernecky's 2018 special condition went through the individualized review
  18 U.S.C. § 3583(d) requires for every special condition: it had to be
  "reasonably related" to the § 3553(a) sentencing factors, involve "no
  greater deprivation of liberty ... than is reasonably necessary," and be
  consistent with Sentencing Commission policy statements — and per the same
  Guide (Vol. 8D, Ch. 3, § 340.20.30(i)), "[a]n individualized assessment of
  the need for and appropriateness of each recommended special condition
  should be included in the sentencing recommendation." That is real judicial
  process specific to Bernecky, even though it was not framed as a
  disclosure-specific "compelling need" finding.
- The condition is narrow and recipient-specific — it names the New York
  State Board of Examiners of Sex Offenders by name, in a judgment for a
  defendant SORNA already obligates to register with that exact agency — not
  a blanket grant to disclose to any future requester. That is a meaningfully
  different animal from *Charmer*'s facts, where an out-of-jurisdiction state
  agency with no built-in relationship to the case obtained the report
  through an irregular channel before any court involvement at all.
- No case anywhere — after a genuinely thorough search across multiple search
  strategies (below) — has ever held that this kind of standing special
  condition fails to satisfy *Charmer*, despite this general type of
  condition (PSR disclosure to a state sex-offender registration agency as a
  special condition of supervised release) being common, longstanding federal
  sentencing practice. The absence of any successful (or even attempted)
  challenge is weak evidence on its own, but it is evidence.

**Unknown — flagged, not assumed:**
- Whether the sentencing judge in Bernecky's case made any oral findings at
  the 2018 sentencing hearing itself specifically addressing why PSR
  disclosure to the Board was appropriate, beyond what appears in the written
  judgment. No sentencing transcript has been reviewed (see prior memo's
  Recommendation 3, still outstanding).
- Whether the AO's own § 530(a) disjunctive framework has itself ever been
  cited, relied upon, or rejected by any court construing *Charmer* — it was
  not found cited in any judicial opinion in this session's searches, and
  should be treated as agency practice guidance, not as an authoritative
  judicial gloss on what *Charmer* actually requires. See Section 4 below.
- Whether any circuit other than the Second has addressed a standing special
  condition specifically (as opposed to the general "presumption against
  disclosure" background principle, which many circuits recite).

## 4. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### A. What procedural posture actually produced "authorization by the court" in *Charmer* — and what the opinion's own language does and does not foreclose

*Charmer* arose from a specific, non-representative sequence: the Probation
Service released the Peerless Report to the Arizona Assistant Attorney General
*before* any court involvement at all (an irregular phone request to a
records-room clerk); only afterward did the Probation Service send Judge
Sifton a memorandum "asking the court's approval to release the Peerless
Report to the Arizona AG in connection with the license application
proceedings," which the judge granted on March 16, 1983 — an approval the
Second Circuit later found had been "entered on the basis of inaccurate and
incomplete representations to the court" and therefore could not be relied on.
711 F.2d at 1168–69 & n.11. Peerless then sought an injunction, and the
district court, having (in the Second Circuit's view) wrongly treated the
matter as an ordinary preliminary-injunction motion rather than recognizing
that the burden remained on the Arizona AG to justify disclosure, denied
relief. Id. at 1176–77. The Second Circuit reversed, conducting its own
review "as if the unauthorized release of the Report had not occurred and as
if the district court had simply granted a motion to disclose," id. at 1177,
and then performed a granular, fact-specific weighing of exactly what
information the Arizona AG needed, whether it was available elsewhere, and
whether disclosure served "the ends of justice." Id. at 1177–78.

Every part of that sequence — the request, the after-the-fact court
memorandum, the district court's review, and the Second Circuit's own
substitute analysis — is built around a live, identified third-party
requester and a specific proceeding that requester was pursuing. The panel's
own summary of the governing rule reflects that structure directly: "we
conclude that the court should not release a presentence report to a third
person unless **that person** has shown a compelling need for disclosure to
meet the ends of justice." 711 F.2d at 1176 (emphasis added). "That person"
and "has shown" are both singular, retrospective, and tied to an actual
applicant — the sentence does not read as "unless the court has determined in
advance that disclosure to a category of future recipients would be
appropriate." The panel's later application confirms this individualized
structure: "[a] central element in the showing required of a third person
seeking disclosure is the degree to which the information in the presentence
report cannot be obtained from other sources," 711 F.2d at 1177 — a factual
question that, by definition, can only be answered once a specific requester
and a specific need for specific information actually exist.

**Does the opinion's own language leave room for prospective, judgment-level
authorization without a separate disclosure-time application?** Honestly:
*Charmer* neither embraces nor forecloses that scenario, because it was never
presented with it. The panel expressly noted that "[n]o reported case, and
only one unreported case, has been called to our attention in which a court
has ordered the disclosure of a presentence report to a third party," 711
F.2d at 1173–74 & n.10 — every authority *Charmer* discusses, going back
through *Hancock Brothers*, *Figurski*, *Krause*, and *Bernstein*, involves an
ad hoc request, not a standing condition. The closest the opinion comes to
addressing anything like advance, delegated authorization is a footnote
quoting the AO's own presentence monograph: "In some instances the court
delegates to the probation office the responsibility for determining what
information from the report may be disclosed. In most instances, if release
of the information is otherwise deemed proper, it is best to obtain the
defendant's written consent for release of information." 711 F.2d at 1174 n.9
(quoting AO Presentence Monograph at 3). The panel quotes this only as
background on Probation Service practice — it does not adopt, endorse, or
even discuss whether such court-to-probation-office delegation satisfies its
own "authorization by the court" / "compelling need" standard. That silence
cuts both ways: it means *Charmer* does not condemn advance delegation, but it
also means *Charmer* provides no affirmative holding that advance delegation
suffices. This is genuinely open ground, not settled law in either direction.

### B. Whether any court has decided the precise question — a standing special condition of supervised release as *Charmer* "authorization"

I searched this from several angles, consistent with the standing brief's
instruction not to stop at one unproductive term: direct citation searches
("Charmer" plus "special condition," "supervised release," "sex offender
registration"); functional searches (PSR disclosure to treatment providers,
employers, and state agencies under a standing condition); a full citator-
style search of every opinion citing *Charmer* on CourtListener (approximately
twenty citing opinions returned, spanning 1st, 2d, 5th, 6th, and 9th Circuit
and multiple SDNY/EDNY/NDNY district decisions from *Kravetz* and *Iqbal*
through *Gotti*, *Sattar*, *Kushner*, and others); and a specific look at the
one Second Circuit case in that citing list that applies *Charmer* to a
presentence-report request in the same district, *United States v. Molina*,
356 F.3d 269 (2d Cir. 2004) **(b-single-source — confirmed only through a
search-engine summary of the opinion, not a direct read of the full text; the
Justia and CourtListener copies both failed to load in this session and
should be independently confirmed before quoting)**. *Molina* applies
*Charmer* to a co-defendant's request for another co-defendant's presentence
report (an impeachment/exculpatory-material theory), holding that the
district court should examine the report in camera and that any exculpatory
or impeachment material should not be released absent "a compelling need for
disclosure to meet the ends of justice" — again a live, individualized,
disclosure-time application, not a standing condition. It does not address
the question this memorandum was assigned.

**No case — in the Second Circuit or any other jurisdiction — was found
addressing whether a standard special condition of supervised release,
authorizing PSR disclosure to a specific listed recipient and imposed at
sentencing as part of the ordinary conditions-of-supervision package,
satisfies *Charmer*'s "authorization by the court" standard.** This mirrors
the prior memorandum's finding on the separate Rule 32(i)(3)(B) question
(Issue 2) — both appear to be genuinely first-impression questions in this
specific procedural configuration. I say that plainly, not to inflate the
novelty of the point, but because a search using many term variations turned
up nothing closer than general background on PSR confidentiality
("presumption against disclosure" language from *Iqbal*, *Huckaby*, *Siler*,
*Cianscewski*, *Corbitt*, *McKnight*, and *Santarelli*, all confirmed via the
Guide to Judiciary Policy's own citations, Vol. 8D, Ch. 5, § 510(d)) and
*Molina*'s individualized-application scenario, neither of which reaches the
standing-condition question.

### C. The government's most likely counterargument: does § 3583(d) individualized review of the special condition itself satisfy *Charmer*, even without a separate disclosure-specific application?

This is the strongest version of the government's position, and it deserves a
direct answer rather than a dismissal. Two distinct threads support it:

**First — the AO's own governing policy treats advance court authorization as
independently sufficient.** The current Guide to Judiciary Policy, Vol. 8D,
Ch. 5, § 530(a), read directly in this session, states: "Presentence
information is confidential and may be disclosed to third parties or other
agencies only if: (1) the respective sentencing court has granted
authorization to disclose such information at its discretion; (2) the court
determines that a compelling need for disclosure has been demonstrated; or
(3) there exists explicit authority to disclose such information." This is
the Administrative Office's current, in-force interpretation of how
*Charmer*'s general rule operates in practice — three independent bases, not
one, and "the sentencing court has granted authorization ... at its
discretion" is listed separately from, not as a component of, "the court
determines that a compelling need ... has been demonstrated." Under this
framework, Bernecky's 2018 special condition is squarely basis (1): a
discretionary grant of authorization by the sentencing court itself, in the
judgment. If a court gave this document controlling weight, the client's
distinction would not hold — advance judgment-level authorization would be
treated as its own category, full stop, with no separate compelling-need
showing required at the time of actual disclosure.

I want to be precise about what this source is and is not. It is not a
judicial opinion construing *Charmer* — it was not found cited in any case in
this session's searches, and no court has been shown to have adopted its
three-basis framework as an accurate restatement of *Charmer*'s holding. It is
agency practice guidance, issued by the body that administers presentence
reports across the federal system, describing how the confidentiality rule
is applied in ordinary practice. It carries real persuasive weight — it shows
what federal courts and probation offices actually do, at scale, treating
advance conditions like Bernecky's as adequate — but it is not binding
precedent on a New York SORA court, and it is fair for the client to argue
that the AO's disjunctive restatement is *broader* than what the Second
Circuit's own opinion actually says, for the reasons in Section A above.

**Second — § 3583(d) itself supplies real, individualized judicial process.**
Every special condition, including this one, had to satisfy 18 U.S.C. §
3583(d): reasonably related to the § 3553(a) factors (the nature and
circumstances of the offense, the defendant's history and characteristics,
and the applicable statutory sentencing purposes), no greater deprivation of
liberty or property than reasonably necessary, and consistent with Sentencing
Commission policy statements. The AO's own current guidance (Guide, Vol. 8D,
Ch. 3, § 340.20.30(g)–(i)) directs officers to avoid "presumptions or the use
of set packages of conditions for groups of defendants," to tailor special
conditions "to the defendant's individual needs" and "the case's specific
facts and circumstances," and states that "[a]n individualized assessment of
the need for and appropriateness of each recommended special condition should
be included in the sentencing recommendation." That is a real, defendant-
specific process — not a rubber stamp — even though it is not the same
inquiry as *Charmer*'s ends-of-justice/compelling-need weighing, and even
though it does not itself require the court to make findings about the
Board's specific need for specific information (because, at the time of
sentencing, the Board has made no request and has no case-specific need to
evaluate yet).

**My honest assessment of this counterargument:** it is a real argument, but
it does not fully answer the client's point, because § 3583(d) review asks a
different question than *Charmer* asks. Section 3583(d) asks whether *this
condition, imposed on this defendant*, is a reasonable and proportionate
restriction — it is fundamentally about the *defendant's* liberty interest in
not having unnecessary conditions imposed on him. *Charmer*'s compelling-need
standard asks a different question: whether *this specific third party's
specific need* for *this specific information* outweighs the confidentiality
interest the report otherwise carries — a question about the *report's*
interest in confidentiality vis-à-vis a particular outside recipient, not
about the defendant's liberty interest in his conditions of supervision. A
court can conduct rigorous, genuinely individualized § 3583(d) review of
whether it is reasonable to *require Bernecky to register and permit his PSR
to reach the state registration agency* without ever separately asking
whether the Board's eventual, still-hypothetical use of that report meets an
ends-of-justice standard — because that second question, by its nature, has
no concrete content until an actual disclosure event exists. Whether that
distinction matters as a matter of law is exactly the unresolved question
this memorandum cannot close, because no court has closed it.

### D. Weighing it honestly

Putting the two sides together: the client's textual argument from *Charmer*
itself is genuine and not answered by anything in *Charmer*'s four corners —
the opinion's operative language is built for individualized, requester-
specific showings, and every fact pattern it actually discusses fits that
mold. But the government's practical and administrative argument is also
genuine — the AO's own current, in-force policy treats advance judicial
authorization as an independent, sufficient basis, § 3583(d) supplies real
(if differently-focused) individualized process, and this exact category of
condition (PSR disclosure to a named state sex-offender registration agency)
is longstanding, common federal sentencing practice that — so far as this
search could determine — has never been successfully challenged, or even
litigated, on a *Charmer* theory.

I do not think this cuts as decisively in the government's favor as the prior
memorandum's Issue 4 conclusion suggested. That memorandum treated the 2018
condition as "precisely the 'prior authorization by the court' *Charmer*
requires" without confronting the procedural-posture distinction the client
has now raised, and having gone back and reread *Charmer* specifically for
that distinction, I think the client identified a real gap in that reasoning.
At the same time, I do not think the client's argument affirmatively wins
either — the AO's own governing framework, read directly, is a serious
obstacle that the prior memorandum did not have before it and that a court
(especially a court weighing this against the backdrop of ordinary federal
sentencing practice) is likely to find persuasive. This is a genuinely close,
first-impression question. Anyone presenting it to the SORA court should say
so, rather than either overclaiming that *Charmer* plainly requires an
application, or overclaiming that the 2018 condition plainly satisfies it.

## 5. CONCLUSION

The client's distinction is real, not frivolous, and the prior memorandum's
Issue 4 conclusion — stated there with "high confidence" — should be revised
downward. *Charmer*'s own text is genuinely more consistent with an
individualized, disclosure-time showing than the prior memorandum
acknowledged. But the client's position does not clearly win either: the
Administrative Office's own current policy, read directly, treats standing
judicial authorization as independently sufficient, § 3583(d) supplies real
individualized process specific to this defendant, and no court anywhere has
ever held a condition like this one insufficient under *Charmer* despite the
condition type being common practice. My honest confidence level: this is
close to a genuine toss-up as a matter of unresolved law, with a modest lean
toward the government given the weight of current administrative practice and
the total absence of any contrary authority. I would not advise the Writer to
present the "unauthorized transmission" theory to the SORA court as a clean
winner, but I also would not advise treating it as foreclosed. It is an
argument with real support and real, undecided risk on both sides — precisely
the kind of question that should be flagged to the court as unresolved, not
asserted as settled in either direction.

This does not disturb the client's own separate, independent instruction that
the *Mingo*/reliability theory remains the primary focus regardless of how
this question comes out.
