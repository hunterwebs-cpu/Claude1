## Headline Finding

The CIMP Agreement is not a form that implements Special Condition (a) — it is a second, unwritten sentencing order. At least six of its clauses (a total encryption ban, a total "in-private" browsing ban, a total security-software ban, an "ALL repairs" pre-clearance requirement, an "any person"/prospective seizure-and-search authorization, and a hardware tape-seal mandate) impose restrictions that appear nowhere in the Judgment's actual text. *United States v. Browder*, 866 F.3d 504, 513–14 (2d Cir. 2017), already holds — in this same district, on this same category of condition — that "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose" a rule that "would contradict, rather than execute" the sentence actually pronounced. And *United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748, 767–68 (2d Cir. 2023) — construing this *identical* CIMP program, on appeal from this same district — goes further: it held that a specific CIMP clause purporting to let Probation restrict a supervisee to a single internet-capable device "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court" and ordered that "Probation may not enforce" it, precisely because the CIMP form went beyond what the sentencing court's own special-condition language actually authorized. That is this memo's Finding 1 fact pattern, already decided, in this exact program. Every clause flagged below is a variant of the same defect this motion already attacks in Parts I and V: Probation reading its own silence-filling discretion as license to write conditions the sentencing judge was never asked to approve.

## Prioritized Findings

### 1. Item 21 — Seizure by "law enforcement," on suspicion of "any person's" conduct, "about to occur"

> "I shall permit the seizure and removal of hardware and data storage media for further analysis **by law enforcement or the probation officer** based upon reasonable suspicion that a violation of a condition of supervision or **unlawful conduct by any person** has or **is about to occur**."

**Theory:** Ultra vires / Fourth Amendment overbreadth (combined). Three separate expansions, each independently unsupported:

- *Actor.* The Judgment's CIMP condition authorizes only "**the U.S. Probation Office**" to install applications and conduct examinations; its general search condition, Special Condition (f), says nothing about "law enforcement" at all — it authorizes a search of the defendant's own person/property and "permit[s] confiscation of any evidence or contraband discovered," full stop. Congress *does* know how to write a search condition that names "law enforcement" alongside "probation officer" — it did so in 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(23), the discretionary condition authorizing search of "computer, other electronic communication or data storage devices" by "any law enforcement or probation officer with reasonable suspicion." (Verified: exact statutory text confirmed via Cornell LII, primary source.) That provision exists, was available to the sentencing judge, and he did not select it — he wrote Special Condition (f) and Special Condition (a) using narrower language limited to "the U.S. Probation Office." A probation-drafted "Agreement" cannot import the broader statutory condition Congress made available but the sentencing judge did not choose. That is the identical defect *Browder* condemns — and the identical *methodology* *Kunz* used to strike the single-device clause: compare the CIMP form's text against the court's actual special-condition text, word for word, and refuse to enforce anything the CIMP form adds that the court's own language does not support. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767–68 (device restriction "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court" because the special condition's own text, using "(s)," did not support a single-device limit); *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 514 ("contradict[ing], rather than execut[ing]" the sentence).
- *Whose conduct.* The statute and every ordinary search condition tie reasonable suspicion to conduct by *the defendant*. Item 21 triggers on "unlawful conduct by **any person**" — meaning a roommate's, a visitor's, or a family member's suspected unrelated crime becomes a basis to seize Bernecky's hardware. Nothing in Special Condition (a)'s "as triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity" language, and nothing in Special Condition (f), extends the special-needs rationale that far. (Note: U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(7)'s model search condition, quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 758–59, authorizes search "upon reasonable suspicion concerning a violation of a condition of supervised release or unlawful conduct **by the defendant**" — the Commission's own model ties the trigger to the defendant, not "any person," reinforcing that Item 21's broader trigger is Probation's own addition, not derived from the Guidelines model the Judgment presumably drew on.)
- *When.* "Has or **is about to occur**" is anticipatory — it authorizes seizure before any violation exists, on a prediction. The special-needs doctrine that permits suspicionless or reduced-suspicion supervision searches is grounded in monitoring *actual* compliance, not preempting a predicted future act. *See United States v. Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d 173 (2d Cir. 2004) (special needs justify computer monitoring, but the monitoring/search authority must bear a "close and substantial relation" to the government's actual supervisory interest and cannot be open-ended); *United States v. Knights*, 534 U.S. 112, 121 (2001) (upholding a warrantless probation search *supported by reasonable suspicion* of an already-occurring or completed violation). **Caveat, now confirmed from the primary Kunz text:** Kunz's own counsel expressly disavowed any Fourth Amendment *Lifshitz* challenge at oral argument, and any First Amendment challenge, so the *Kunz* panel never reached either question. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 754 n.1. Lifshitz and Knights remain independently good law, but they are not authority *Kunz* applied or endorsed — do not cite *Kunz* as having resolved the Fourth Amendment question, because it did not. Lifshitz's exact pincite for the "close and substantial relation" language has not yet been independently pulled from a primary source (see NOT VERIFIED).

**Recommendation:** Seek an order striking "by law enforcement" (leaving "the probation officer," consistent with Special Condition (a)'s actual grant of authority); striking "or unlawful conduct by any person" (limiting the trigger to the defendant's own conduct, consistent with U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(7)'s own model language); and striking "or is about to occur" (limiting the trigger to a violation or unlawful conduct that has actually occurred). Cite *Kunz*'s single-device holding as the closest on-point precedent for the general proposition that a CIMP-form clause exceeding the court's own special-condition text will not be enforced.

### 2. Items 9 & 17 — Encryption ban

> Item 9: "I understand that I will not use any encrypted email accounts."
> Item 17: "I shall not use any type of encryption unless pre-approved by my probation officer."

**Theory:** Narrowing construction under *Kunz*, plus a real residual gap in Item 9 that *Kunz* did not resolve.

**What *Kunz* actually held (confirmed by direct review of the reporter text, not a secondary summary):** Kunz's own CIMP terms included paragraph 18 — "I shall not use any type of encryption unless pre-approved by my probation officer" — the same clause as Bernecky's Item 17, and paragraph 10 — "I understand that I will not use any encrypted email accounts" — **word-for-word identical to Bernecky's Item 9.** *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 757. Kunz's expert argued a literal reading of paragraph 18 would require pre-approval before visiting any "https" website. The panel agreed a literal reading would be absurd, but it did **not** strike the clause — it construed it narrowly and **affirmed the condition as construed**: "so long as no encryption is initiated by or at the behest of Kunz, and so long as any encryption initiated by external forces does not meaningfully impair Probation's ability to monitor Kunz's behavior..., we construe the encryption restrictions to allow the reasonable use of services and resources that happen to employ encryption in some way." *Id.* at 764. The panel's textual hook for that narrowing was that the clause is "phrased in terms of what 'I' (Kunz, the supervisee) will or will not actively do" — it personalizes the obligation to Kunz's own affirmative conduct, not "conduct... effected by the vendor, invisible to the user." *Id.* at 762–64 & n.5.

**Why this changes the recommended framing:** Item 9 uses the identical operative construction — "I will not **use** any encrypted email accounts" — so a court applying *Kunz*'s own reasoning would very likely read it the same way it read paragraph 18: barring Bernecky from himself affirmatively choosing an end-to-end-encrypted service (PGP/GPG, S/MIME, ProtonMail, Tutanota) to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, not from holding an ordinary Gmail/Outlook/Yahoo account that the provider encrypts in transit and at rest, invisibly to him. **Do not frame Item 9 as "impossible to comply with and therefore void."** *Kunz* forecloses exactly that framing for the sibling clause — the panel rejected the premise that the maximalist literal reading is the correct one, and affirmed rather than struck. Presenting an "impossible, must be struck" argument to a court already holding *Kunz* invites the court to cite *Kunz* right back and deny the request on the same reasoning that defeated Kunz's own expert.

**What *Kunz* does *not* resolve, and where the actual opening is:**
1. *Kunz*'s substantive discussion never mentions paragraph 10 (the email clause) by name — only paragraphs 7, 16, and 18. That silence means no court has yet squarely applied the narrowing construction to the email-specific clause; there is a live, first-instance argument that the *same* construction should govern it, but honesty requires disclosing this as an extension by direct analogy, not an on-point holding.
2. **Item 9 has no pre-approval mechanism at all — unlike Item 17.** Item 17 says "unless pre-approved by my probation officer"; Item 9 has no comparable escape valve. Even under the *Kunz*-style narrowed reading, Item 9 as written gives Bernecky no way to ever seek approval for a legitimate use of an end-to-end encrypted service — e.g., if a future employer's IT department required PGP-signed correspondence, or counsel/the court communicated through an encrypted portal. *Kunz*'s own paragraph 10 shares this same gap and the panel never had occasion to address it. This is a real, unresolved asymmetry worth a targeted, modest ask.
3. **Supporting technical facts** (from a computer/internet-security review, used here as background technical/legislative fact supporting the *Kunz* narrowing construction — not as freestanding grounds to void the clause): Gmail, Outlook/Microsoft 365, Yahoo Mail, and iCloud Mail all encrypt mail in transit (TLS/STARTTLS) and at rest by default, per RFC 3207 (2002), RFC 8314 (2018, "Cleartext Considered Obsolete"), and RFC 8461 (2018, MTA-STS, which lets a receiving domain force sending servers to use verified TLS or have mail refused outright). Google's own "Safer Email" transparency initiative (launched June 2014) exists because, at that time, "approximately 40 to 50 percent of emails sent between Gmail and other email providers aren't encrypted" — the ecosystem has moved one-directionally toward near-universal TLS since. Webmail login itself has been HTTPS-only since Chrome began flagging all plain-HTTP pages as "Not Secure" in July 2018. None of this is user-configurable; whether a message is encrypted in transit depends on the *receiving* provider's infrastructure, not any choice the account-holder makes. These facts support, factually, exactly the premise *Kunz*'s construction already assumes — that mainstream email's encryption is provider-side and invisible to the user, not something the user "uses" in the sense the clause targets — and would be offered as judicially-noticeable technical background (the same posture *Kunz* itself accepted the "https" premise, without disputed expert testimony), not as legal authority. They are not caselaw and should never appear in a Table of Authorities.

**Recommendation:** Ask the Court to do what *Kunz* has already shown it is willing to do with this exact boilerplate — enter a written order construing both Item 9 and Item 17 to reach only encryption tools or services Bernecky himself affirmatively selects to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, expressly excluding the ordinary, provider-side transport and at-rest encryption built into mainstream commercial email and web services. Separately, ask that Item 9 be amended to add the same pre-approval mechanism Item 17 already has, so the narrowly-construed prohibition on user-selected end-to-end encryption is not an absolute, unwaivable bar with no path to a legitimate use. This is more modest than "strike outright," it is the ask *Kunz*'s own reasoning actually supports, and it is consistent with this motion's overall practice of asking for written clarification rather than asking the court to invalidate conditions wholesale.

### 3. Item 16 — Ban on antivirus/firewall/security software without "advanced authorization"

> "I shall not purchase, download, possess and/or install any anti-virus, anti-spyware, firewall, or internet security application without advanced authorization by the U.S. Probation Office and my probation officer."

**Theory:** Overbreadth/reductio + ultra vires + vagueness. This clause, read literally, criminalizes leaving a computer's *factory-default* security software running: Windows Defender (antivirus + firewall) and macOS's built-in firewall ship active out of the box on every modern operating system. A defendant cannot "not install" what the manufacturer already installed, and disabling it to comply would leave the monitored device *more* vulnerable to compromise — directly undermining the government's own interest in reliable, tamper-free monitoring. Nothing in Special Condition (a) mentions security software at all; the closest textual hook is the anti-tampering concern for the *monitoring* software specifically, not third-party security products generally. Note: *Kunz*'s own CIMP paragraph 17 is the same clause, but the panel's opinion does not separately analyze it — its narrowing-construction discussion is confined to paragraphs 7, 16 (renumbered here as Item 15/16), and 18. Treat this finding as independent of *Kunz*, not as something *Kunz* already resolved.

**Recommendation:** Strike or narrow to require notice only (not dual pre-authorization) before installing *additional, non-default* security software, with an explicit carve-out for the operating system's own built-in, factory-default protections.

### 4. Item 5 — Total ban on "in-private" browsing

> "While using my monitored device, I shall not use any form of 'in-private' browsing at any time."

**Theory:** Ultra vires (pure *Browder* problem) + vagueness. Special Condition (a) authorizes Probation to "install any application as necessary to surveil all activity" — meaning the *premise* of the monitoring software is that it captures activity regardless of browser mode. If that premise is accurate, Item 5 is either surplusage (private-browsing mode does nothing to defeat monitoring that already captures everything) or it is doing independent work Probation never got court authorization to do: banning a mode of browsing, as opposed to monitoring the activity within it. Nothing in the Judgment authorizes a categorical browsing-mode prohibition. This is *Browder*'s "would contradict, rather than execute" problem in its cleanest form. 866 F.3d at 514. This clause does not appear among *Kunz*'s challenged CIMP paragraphs at all — it stands on *Browder* alone.

**Recommendation:** Strike, or require Probation to explain on the record why the monitoring software it already deploys under Special Condition (a) cannot capture private-browsing activity — if it can, the clause is unnecessary; if it cannot, that is a fact about Probation's chosen monitoring method (addressed in MOTION-v2.md Part I.B), not a basis for an independent behavioral prohibition never authorized by the Court.

### 5. Item 15 — Pre-clearance for "ALL" computer repairs and alterations

> "I agree to obtain permission and coordinate with my probation officer prior to ALL computer repairs and/or alterations to my computer hardware and/or software."

**Theory:** This is Bernecky's version of *Kunz*'s paragraph 16, and *Kunz* already construed it narrowly rather than striking it — cite that construction affirmatively rather than arguing overbreadth from scratch. *Kunz* held that an identical "obtain permission... prior to ALL computer repairs and/or alterations" clause does **not** require pre-clearance for automatic, vendor-triggered updates (Windows Update, macOS auto-updates) or other changes "entirely outside of [the defendant's] control and of which he himself has no advance notice" — it reaches only alterations Kunz himself actively initiates. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 762–64. The panel reasoned that because the clause personalizes the obligation ("I agree to obtain permission... prior to... alterations"), it does not, and could not sensibly, "compel him to seek prior approval for processes entirely outside of his control." *Id.* at 763.

**Recommendation:** Do not ask the Court to strike "ALL" or rewrite the clause from scratch — ask the Court to adopt the *Kunz* construction in writing: Item 15 binds only alterations Bernecky himself actively and intentionally initiates, not automatic OS/vendor updates or other changes outside his knowledge or control. This is a stronger, better-supported, more easily granted ask than a facial overbreadth challenge, because it asks the Court to do exactly what it has already done once in this same program.

### 6. Vagueness/delegation cluster — undefined "approval," "pre-approved," "authorization," "advanced authorization" (Items 6, 13, 14, 16, 20)

**Theory:** Vagueness / improper delegation of standardless discretion — the same theory MOTION-v2.md Part VIII already applies to Standard Conditions 5 and 8. At least five separate CIMP clauses condition Bernecky's rights on an undefined approval process, with no criteria stated anywhere for what earns it, how it's requested, or how long it takes: notifying PO of "any alterations... prior to executing change" (Item 6); having someone else access the internet "unless pre-approved" (Item 13); using evidence-cleaning/defrag utilities "unless pre-approved" (Item 14); installing security software "without advanced authorization by the U.S. Probation Office and my probation officer" (Item 16); and altering the OS "without the approval of the probation officer" (Item 20). *United States v. Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77, 80–81 (2d Cir. 2010): "[a] condition is too vague if it requires a reasonable person to speculate as to its meaning."

**Citation correction:** An earlier draft of this memo attributed the quotation "may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion" to *Kunz* quoting *Matta*. Direct review of the primary text shows that quotation is *Kunz* quoting ***Birkedahl***, not *Matta*: "while a district court may delegate to Probation authority 'over certain minor details of supervised release,' it may not delegate 'decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion.'" *United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748, 765 (2d Cir. 2023) (quoting *United States v. Birkedahl*, 973 F.3d 49, 54 (2d Cir. 2020)). *Matta* is *Kunz*'s companion authority for the related but distinct principle that "[t]he power to impose special conditions of supervised release... is vested exclusively in the district court," while "the extensive supervision mission of federal probation officers includes executing the sentence but not imposing it," and that delegating the choice between inpatient and outpatient drug treatment was, on that basis, an impermissible delegation. *Matta*, 777 F.3d 116, 122–23 (2d Cir. 2015), as quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 765.

**Recommendation — recalibrated:** *Kunz* resolved the identical vagueness/delegation concern for its own approval-gated clauses (paragraphs 7 and 16, i.e., Bernecky's Items 6 and 15) not by ordering Probation to draft a new written standard or checklist, but by *construing the clauses narrowly* — confining the probation officer's approval authority to changes the defendant himself actively and intentionally initiates, and holding that authority void wherever it would cross from a "minor detail" into a "major imposition" on liberty (in which case it reverts to the court). *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 762–66. The Kunz-consistent, more-likely-to-be-granted ask is a written order adopting that same construction for Items 6, 13, 14, 16, and 20 — not a demand that Probation produce an entirely new checklist, which goes beyond what *Kunz* itself required. Fold this into the existing Part VIII vagueness argument as a citation to *Kunz*'s specific method, alongside *Reeves*'s general vagueness standard.

### 7. Item 14 — Ban on defragmentation and "system restore" absent pre-approval

> "I agree not to employ any evidence cleaning utility, or defragmentation technology unless pre-approved by probation officer. This includes, but is not limited to, utilizing the 'system restore' or similar function."

**Theory:** Overbreadth/reductio, related to but not resolved by *Kunz* (whose paragraph 15 is the same clause, but whose narrowing discussion does not separately address it by name — the panel's discussion of paragraphs 7/16 is the closest analog and supports the same approach here). Modern Windows runs scheduled disk optimization automatically in the background by default, and "System Restore" points are created automatically by the OS during routine updates. A defendant cannot meaningfully "not employ" a feature the operating system triggers on its own schedule without his input. The clause's evident purpose — stopping deliberate evidence destruction — is legitimate and derivable from the Judgment's anti-tampering concern (Item 7), but its text sweeps in automated OS housekeeping that has nothing to do with evidence and everything to do with how the machine functions by design.

**Recommendation:** Extend the same *Kunz*-style construction requested for Item 15 (Finding 5) to this clause: it binds only deliberate, user-initiated use of these features for the purpose of defeating monitoring or destroying evidence, not automated, OS-scheduled background maintenance.

### 8. The Agreement as undifferentiated boilerplate — parsimony / individualized-assessment problem

**Theory:** 18 U.S.C. § 3583(d)'s parsimony requirement ("no greater deprivation of liberty than is reasonably necessary," confirmed at 18 U.S.C. § 3583(d)(2) via Cornell LII primary-source text, and quoted by *Kunz* itself at 68 F.4th at 758) and USSG § 5D1.3(b)(1)'s individualized-assessment mandate, already relied on in MOTION-v2.md Part IV, apply with equal force here. The Agreement's opening paragraph is a fill-in-the-blank form — Probation's standardized template applied to every CIMP participant regardless of offense type. Bernecky was convicted of *possessing* images, with no allegation anywhere in the record that he used encryption, VPNs, security software, or private-browsing modes to commit or conceal his offense. A blanket, one-size-fits-all encryption ban, security-software ban, and private-browsing ban — none tied to anything in *his* record — is exactly the kind of condition the Sentencing Commission's 2025 amendments were written to catch: conditions "imposed at original sentencing, often years before the defendant begins supervision," that were never individually tailored and should be reassessed "at the start of supervision." USSG § 5D1.4(a) (2025); § 5D1.3(b)(1) (2025).

**Recommendation:** Use this as the framing argument for the whole CIMP-Agreement challenge, not a standalone numbered item — it is the umbrella under which Findings 2–7 sit, and it is the same move MOTION-v2.md Part IV already makes with Special Condition (i).

### 9. Cost-shifting — "at my own expense" / unquantified fee obligation (weak; hold in reserve)

> Opening paragraph: "I agree to install, or allow to be installed, **at my own expense**, equipment or software to monitor my approved computer or internet ready device."
> Cf. Judgment, Special Condition (a): "**The defendant will be required to pay the cost of monitoring services.**"

**Theory:** An unquantified, open-ended cost obligation, with no dollar figure, cap, or ability-to-pay procedure specified anywhere in the Judgment or the Agreement. **This theory is now confirmed weaker still by direct review of *Kunz*, which rejected the identical challenge.** Kunz's 2021 special conditions added, for the first time, the exact clause in Bernecky's own Judgment — "[t]he defendant will be required to pay the cost of monitoring services" — and Kunz challenged it on appeal. The panel rejected the challenge because Kunz "does not even attempt to articulate any impact on his liberty," and "the record is silent as to what, if any, costs [he] will actually be forced to bear"; absent such a showing, "the self-evident rationale — shifting reasonable monitoring costs to the person whose crime made the monitoring necessary — provides sufficient justification." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 761–62. No Second Circuit case was located squarely holding an unquantified CIMP/monitoring-cost condition an unconstitutional delegation; the out-of-circuit Fifth Circuit authority located during this research (treating ability-to-pay delegations for treatment/monitoring costs as more permissible than delegations over the *type* of treatment) points the same direction. The "*United States v. Leone*" case referenced in this matter's own prior research (conditions-list.md) as addressing WDNY CIMP cost practice could not be independently located or verified (see NOT VERIFIED) — **note, however, that the real *Kunz* opinion itself cites a real, different case named *United States v. Leone*, 813 F. App'x 665 (2d Cir. 2020) (summary order), at 68 F.4th at 770 n.11, in an unrelated context (affirming a "substantially identical" CIMP special condition where the delegation argument raised was limited to vagueness of computer restrictions).** This confirms *a* case named Leone exists and is real, but it is not shown to say what this case's prior internal research claimed about CIMP cost/ability-to-pay practice specifically — that citation should still be independently pulled and checked before use for that proposition.

**Recommendation:** Do not lead with this theory or promise any win. Do not pursue it at all absent an actual, documented showing of financial burden on Bernecky specifically — the file's existing `BERNECKY-FINANCIAL-DECLARATION.md` may supply this predicate; without it, *Kunz* is directly adverse authority on this exact clause from this exact Judgment template.

## Closing Recommendation — Where This Goes

**Fold into MOTION-v2.md as a new subpart of Part I, not a separate motion.** Part I already frames Special Condition (a) as the operative text and already argues three sub-problems (occupational reach, unspecified monitoring method, "operated/used" overbreadth). This CIMP-Agreement analysis is a fourth sub-problem of the *same* condition — call it **Part I.D** — because it shares the identical predicate fact (the Agreement is the document Probation actually handed Bernecky to sign) and the identical theory (Probation reading silence as license). Concretely:

1. Add **Part I.D** addressing the CIMP Agreement specifically. Lead with **Finding 1** (the seizure-authority overreach) — it is the strongest and most direct ultra vires argument, and it is now backed by *Kunz*'s single-device holding as directly on-point, binding, same-program precedent, not just *Browder*'s general principle. Follow with **Finding 2** (encryption), framed as a request for a written *Kunz*-style narrowing construction plus a pre-approval mechanism for Item 9 — not a request to strike the clause outright. Then **Findings 5 and 7** (the "ALL repairs"/defrag clauses), each framed as a request that the Court adopt the construction *Kunz* already gave to the identical clauses. Finding 4 (private browsing) and Finding 3 (antivirus ban) stand independently on *Browder* and ordinary overbreadth analysis, since *Kunz* did not address either.
2. Fold Finding 6 (the vagueness/delegation cluster) into the **existing Part VIII** request (Standard Conditions 5 and 8) as an explicit extension, citing *Kunz*'s specific construction method (not a demand for a new checklist) alongside *Reeves*.
