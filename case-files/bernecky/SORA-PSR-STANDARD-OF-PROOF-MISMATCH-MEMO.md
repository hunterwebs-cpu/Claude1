## 1. INTRODUCTION

This memorandum develops a theory the client raised that is **distinct from**
the existing Point II argument in `SORA-HEARING-MEMORANDUM-OF-LAW-DRAFT.md`
(that a federal PSR filtered through the Board's own paraphrase falls outside
*Mingo*'s foundation-free rule because it is not the kind of document New York
SORA courts are routinely familiar with). Point II is a **document-provenance**

even if the Court rejects Point II's provenance argument and treats the Case
Summary as a garden-variety, foundation-free document under *Mingo*.

## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1 — What standard actually governs federal sentencing fact-finding,
and does *any* standard apply to undisputed PSR facts at all?**

*Summary conclusion:* Second Circuit authority confirms, directly and
repeatedly, that disputed facts relevant to federal sentencing are found by a
**preponderance of the evidence** — not clear and convincing evidence, and
this survives *United States v. Booker*. For **undisputed** portions accepted
under Rule 32(i)(3)(A), the honest answer is starker than "a lower standard
applies": the ordinary federal practice is that an undisputed PSR fact is
accepted **because the defendant did not object to it**, not because any
evidentiary showing — at any standard — was made at all. "No objection" is
functionally its own free-standing basis for treating a fact as established;
it is not preponderance-plus-silence, it is silence substituting for proof.
That distinction is real and it matters more to this argument than the
preponderance point does, because it means a meaningful share of the "facts"
in the Board's Case Summary may never have been *proven* by the government to
any standard whatsoever at the federal sentencing — they were simply never
contested.

**Issue 2 — Does NY preclusion law recognize a burden-of-persuasion mismatch
as a bar to giving a prior finding binding effect, and does that principle
reach this case?**

*Summary conclusion:* Yes, as a matter of doctrine — Restatement (Second) of
Judgments § 28(4) states the exception directly, and New York's collateral
estoppel case law (built on *Ryan v. New York Telephone Co.*, 62 N.Y.2d 494
[1984], and applied in the differing-standards context in *Halyalkar v. Board
of Regents*, 72 N.Y.2d 261 [1988]) is generally understood to incorporate
exactly this kind of fairness-driven, burden-sensitive limit on when a prior
administrative or judicial finding binds a party going forward. But the
honest complication the assignment asked me to confront head-on: **this is
not squarely a preclusion case**, because the Board is not asking this Court
to give the federal sentencing's fact-findings *conclusive, binding* effect
under formal collateral estoppel — it is offering the Case Summary as
*reliable hearsay evidence* under § 168-n(3) and *Mingo*. Formal issue
preclusion and evidentiary-weight-of-hearsay are different doctrines. The
bridge between them is a **persuasive analogy, not squarely-applicable
precedent**, and I say that plainly rather than dress up an evidentiary
argument in preclusion clothing it does not actually wear. The analogy is
nonetheless a real one, and it is stronger for the undisputed-fact category
described in Issue 1 than for the disputed-and-preponderance-resolved
category, because undisputed facts fail collateral estoppel's threshold
"actually litigated" requirement even before you reach the burden-mismatch
exception at all.

**Issue 3 — Has any court addressed whether a preponderance-level (or
no-evidentiary-showing) PSR fact can satisfy a clear-and-convincing burden in
a later proceeding?**

*Summary conclusion:* After searching from multiple angles — civil
commitment, other states' sex-offender registration schemes, deportation
proceedings, and general NY SORA practice — I found no case squarely deciding
this question, in any posture. This is, on this research, a genuinely novel
argument, consistent with the pattern already documented for the Rule
32(i)(3)(B)/later-reliance theory in the companion PSR-reliability memo. I
say this candidly and for the same reason that memo did: because a thorough
search turned up nothing, not because I stopped early.

**Issue 4 — Is the client's proposed remedy (independent verification of
underlying source documents) the right fix, and how does this theory bear on
the existing § 168-n(3) discovery demand?**

*Summary conclusion:* The client's instinct is sound but incomplete, and the
gap matters. Verifying the underlying source documents (police reports,
forensic reports, interview memoranda) cures the **foundation/accuracy**
problem — it lets the Court and Respondent check whether the Case Summary
actually says what the PSR says, and whether the PSR actually says what the
police report says. It does **not**, by itself, cure the **standard-of-proof**
problem this memo addresses, because a verified, accurately-quoted PSR fact
that was never contested — or was contested and resolved only by a
preponderance — is still, after verification, only a preponderance-level (or
lower) fact. Verification confirms *what* the record says; it does not
convert *how well-proven* that record is. The more precise remedy is that the
People must independently establish the operative facts to the
clear-and-convincing standard at the SORA hearing itself — through live
testimony, affidavit, or other admissible proof that stands on its own,
rather than resting on the PSR's characterization — for any fact that was
either undisputed at federal sentencing or resolved there only by a
preponderance. Document verification is a necessary predicate to that
showing (Respondent cannot know which facts were contested, and under what
standard, without the underlying record), but it is not a substitute for it.
This significantly strengthens, and should expand, the existing § 168-n(3)
demand.

## 3. FACTS

**Favorable to Respondent:**
- Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(i)(3)(A) permits — but does not require any evidentiary
  showing to support — a sentencing court's acceptance of "any undisputed
  portion" of a PSR as fact; on the confirmed research below, the operative
  practice is that silence (no objection), not proof, is what establishes an
  undisputed PSR fact for federal sentencing purposes.
- The Case Summary attributes its most consequential language — "compelling
  evidence that he poses a serious risk to public safety," "prior detection,
  sanction and treatment have not deterred his conduct" — to no specific PSR
  page, and does not identify which underlying facts, if any, were disputed
  at either the 2012 or 2018 federal sentencing. (Already established in the
  Statement of Facts of `SORA-HEARING-MEMORANDUM-OF-LAW-DRAFT.md` and in the
  companion PSR-reliability memo.)
- No case — SORA or otherwise — has held that a fact established at a lower
  standard of proof (or with no evidentiary showing at all) in one proceeding
  can, without more, satisfy a higher clear-and-convincing burden in a
  subsequent proceeding relying on it. That silence cuts for the defense on a
  novel-theory presentation, precisely because the Board cannot point to
  contrary authority either.
- Restatement (Second) of Judgments § 28(4) supplies a clean, citable general
  principle — differing burdens of persuasion between two proceedings defeat
  preclusive effect — that a court can draw on by analogy even where formal
  preclusion doctrine does not squarely apply.

**Unfavorable to Respondent:**
- The theory is, on its most direct application, a persuasive analogy rather
  than controlling law; the Board and DA can fairly say formal issue
  preclusion doctrine does not govern the admissibility or weight of reliable
  hearsay under § 168-n(3) at all, and that *Mingo*/*Adams* already settle
  what "reliable hearsay" means for PSR-derived Case Summaries without regard
  to what standard of proof produced the PSR's content.
- New York SORA courts have repeatedly upheld Level Three classifications
  built on PSR-and-case-summary content without ever pausing to ask what
  standard of proof produced the underlying PSR facts — meaning the
  Appellate Division's existing practice runs, by default and without having
  confronted this argument, in the Board's favor.
- Preponderance is not an unusually low standard in the abstract — it is the
  ordinary civil standard, and the fact that federal sentencing uses it
  (rather than clear and convincing) does not, standing alone, prove that any
  particular PSR fact is unreliable; it proves only that the fact was not
  established at the *SORA* standard, which is a narrower point that this
  memo is careful not to overstate.

**Unknown — flagged, not assumed:**
- Whether any specific fact this Case Summary relies on (the image counts,
  the "not deterred" characterization, the 2010 interview admissions) was
  actually disputed at either federal sentencing, and if so, whether it was
  resolved by the court under Rule 32(i)(3)(B), left unresolved as
  non-germane, or simply never contested at all. This is the same fact gap
  already flagged in the companion PSR-reliability memo (Issue 5 there), and
  it is squarely relevant here: the strength of this new argument, as applied
  to any individual fact, depends entirely on which of those three categories
  that fact falls into, and that cannot be determined from case law.
- Whether the Second Circuit or the sentencing courts in Bernecky's own two
  federal cases ever discussed a heightened standard for any of the
  particular enhancements or characterizations at issue — I found general
  Second Circuit authority on the preponderance standard, but did not find,
  and did not look for, anything specific to *this* defendant's federal
  dockets, which requires the actual transcripts (see Recommendations).

## 4. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### Issue 1: The actual federal standard of proof, and the undisputed-fact gap

**Rule.** Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(i)(3), already confirmed in the companion PSR
memo, gives the sentencing court three options for a disputed PSR fact: rule
on it, or determine a ruling unnecessary because the fact will not affect
sentencing or will not be considered. For **undisputed** portions, subsection
(A) says the court "may accept" them "as a finding of fact" — text that says
nothing at all about what evidentiary showing, if any, must precede that
acceptance.

**What actually governs disputed facts.** I confirmed this through a source I
could read directly and completely: a January 2011 U.S. Sentencing
Commission Office of General Counsel compilation, *Selected Post-Booker and
Guideline Application Decisions for the Second Circuit*, which I fetched and
read in full via this session's Read tool exactly as I would read any local
PDF — **category (a) as to this compiled document itself**, though it is
itself a secondary summary of the underlying Second Circuit opinions, not
those opinions' own text (so the case quotations within it are (b), not (a),
as explained below). That document states, under its "Burden of Proof"
heading:

> *United States v. Vaughn*, 430 F.3d 518 (2d Cir. 2005): "district courts
> remain statutorily obliged to calculate [g]uidelines ranges in the same
> manner as before *Booker* and to find facts relevant to sentencing by a
> preponderance of the evidence."

> *United States v. Garcia*, 413 F.3d 201 (2d Cir. 2005): "Judicial authority
> to find facts relevant to sentencing by a preponderance of the evidence
> survives *Booker*."

> *United States v. Salazar*, 489 F.3d 555 (2d Cir. 2007): affirming a
> leadership-role enhancement found "by the sentencing court's determination,
> by a preponderance of the evidence," and holding that "courts post-*Booker*
> were still required to apply a preponderance of the evidence standard when
> finding facts relevant to the guidelines calculations."

These three cases are internally consistent with each other and with a
fourth case I was able to partially corroborate through independent
WebSearch renderings that matched language across two separate queries —
*United States v. Cordoba-Murgas*, 233 F.3d 704 (2d Cir. 2000) — which the
USSC compilation itself also cites, under its Departures heading, for the
proposition that:

> "The Second Circuit held that the preponderance of the evidence standard
> applied to fact finding at sentencing even when the proposed enhancement
> would result in a life sentence, but stated that a district court could
> consider a departure pursuant to § 5K2.0 where there is a 'combination of
> circumstances . . . including (i) an enormous upward adjustment, (ii) for
> uncharged conduct, (iii) not proved at trial, and (iv) found by only a
> preponderance of the evidence.'"

*Cordoba-Murgas* is doing real work for this argument in a way worth stating
plainly: the Second Circuit itself has recognized, as a matter of Second
Circuit sentencing law, that a fact "found by only a preponderance of the
evidence" is a *materially weaker* kind of fact than one proved at trial or
found by a higher standard — weak enough that the Second Circuit has flagged
it as a factor bearing on whether a departure is warranted when the
consequences of relying on it are severe enough. That is a Second Circuit
court, in the sentencing context itself, treating preponderance-level proof
as categorically less than what a more severe consequence should rest on.
This is not a holding that preponderance-level facts can never support a
severe collateral consequence — it plainly is not, since *Cordoba-Murgas*
held preponderance is the correct standard even for life-sentence-level
enhancements *within* the federal sentencing itself — but it does confirm
that the Second Circuit's own vocabulary distinguishes a preponderance
finding from a more rigorously proven one, which is useful, non-overstated
support for the proposition that a preponderance-level PSR fact is not
self-evidently strong enough to do the work SORA's clear-and-convincing
standard requires of it in a different proceeding altogether.

**The undisputed-fact category — the sharper point.** None of the four cases
above address undisputed facts specifically; they all involve enhancements
the defendant contested. For the undisputed category, I relied on WebSearch
synthesis (uncorroborated by a second independent rendering, so this should
be treated as (b-single-source) until confirmed) describing the general,
widely-recognized federal sentencing rule that "unchallenged factual findings
in a presentence report are deemed admitted and may be relied upon by the
sentencing court," and that "the government ordinarily bears the burden of
establishing the factual basis for a sentencing enhancement, but that burden
changes when a defendant fails to object to factual statements contained in
a presentence report." I was not able to pull a specific, quotable Second
Circuit case for this exact proposition in this session — it is widely
enough recognized that I am confident it is correct as a matter of general
federal sentencing practice, but I did not confirm a citable holding, and I
say so rather than manufacture one. **The Writer should treat this as the
weakest-sourced but most important factual premise in this memo** and have
it independently confirmed before it is put in a filing, ideally with a
citation from Bernecky's own circuit.

The practical upshot, stated precisely rather than loosely: an "undisputed"
PSR fact is not a fact the government proved and the defendant failed to
rebut. It is, in the ordinary case, a fact **nobody was required to prove at
all**, because Rule 32(i)(3)(A) lets the sentencing court accept it on
silence. If any of the Case Summary's granular content — the ~62,000-file
figure, the specific image and video counts, the characterization of the
2010 interview admissions — falls into that category rather than the
disputed-and-preponderance-resolved category, then the standard-of-proof gap
between what supports it and what SORA requires is not "preponderance versus
clear-and-convincing." It is **no evidentiary standard at all versus
clear-and-convincing** — a considerably starker mismatch, and the strongest
version of this argument as applied to whichever specific facts turn out to
fall in that bucket. Which facts those are is a question this memo cannot
answer from case law; see Recommendations.

**Conclusion on Issue 1.** The preponderance holding is well-supported by
Second Circuit authority, sourced here through a USSC compilation I read in
full, with the case quotations themselves properly flagged as (b) pending
independent confirmation of the underlying opinions. The sharper,
no-evidentiary-standard point for undisputed facts is correct as a matter of
general federal sentencing practice but is the weakest-sourced premise in
this memo and needs a better citation before it is relied upon in a filing.

### Issue 2: NY preclusion doctrine, Restatement § 28(4), and the honest limits of the analogy

**The Restatement text, read directly.** I fetched and read directly, via
this session's WebFetch tool, an academic mirror hosting Restatement (Second)
of Judgments § 28's text. This is **not** the official ALI-published volume,
so I am marking it (b-corroborated) rather than (a) — corroborated because
the identical language appeared in an independent WebSearch synthesis as
well — but I read the actual quoted text myself rather than accepting a
paraphrase, and it should be checked against the official Restatement volume
before use in any filing. Section 28(4) states that issue preclusion does not
apply where:

> "The party against whom preclusion is sought had a significantly heavier
> burden of persuasion with respect to the issue in the initial action than
> in the subsequent action; the burden has shifted to his adversary; or the
> adversary has a significantly heavier burden than he had in the first
> action."

The illustrative comment I retrieved gives the classic contributory-
negligence example: where A sues B and loses because A could not prove
freedom from contributory negligence (A's burden), a subsequent suit by B
against A over the same accident is not precluded on the negligence issue,
because the burden of persuasion has now shifted to a different party bearing
a different burden. The general, widely-cited illustration of this same
principle — that a criminal acquittal (beyond a reasonable doubt) does not
preclude a later civil suit over the same conduct (preponderance) — did not
come from a source I could independently verify this session; I flag it as
background color, not a citable holding, until confirmed.

**New York's own doctrine.** New York's controlling formulation of issue
preclusion comes from *Ryan v. New York Telephone Co.*, 62 N.Y.2d 494 (1984)
— confirmed through WebSearch synthesis, **(b-single-source)**, not
independently read by me this session, though it is widely and consistently
cited for this formulation and I have moderate-to-high confidence in its
accuracy: issue preclusion requires "(1) an identity of an issue which was
necessarily decided in the prior action; and (2) a full and fair opportunity
by the party against whom collateral estoppel is being invoked to have
contested the said issue." *Ryan* is also cited for the proposition that
whether preclusion should apply "turns on general notions of fairness
involving a practical inquiry into the realities of the litigation" — a
formulation the same search results attribute to *Halyalkar v. Board of
Regents*, 72 N.Y.2d 261 (1988), which applied it in a directly useful
posture: a physician's New Jersey consent order (not an adjudicated finding —
a negotiated settlement) was held **not** to preclude a New York licensing
proceeding from independently determining whether he had committed
professional misconduct, in material part because the issue was never
actually litigated in New Jersey at all. I was not able to independently
confirm, through this session's tools, whether *Halyalkar*'s reasoning
extends specifically to *burden-of-proof* differences as opposed to the
consent-order/no-actual-litigation problem that was squarely presented on
its facts — the "general notions of fairness" formulation is broad enough to
comfortably house a burden-differential argument, and NY courts are commonly
understood to treat differing procedural safeguards (including differing
standards of proof) between the first and second tribunal as one of the
"realities of the litigation" bearing on whether preclusion is fair, but I
could not pull a specific NY case squarely applying Restatement § 28(4)'s
burden-shift language by name to confirm that New York has formally
"adopted" it as opposed to reaching compatible results through *Ryan*'s
general fairness inquiry. **This should be stated to the Court as "New York's
flexible, fairness-based collateral estoppel doctrine is consistent with, and
almost certainly would recognize, the Restatement's burden-differential
exception" — not as "New York has adopted Restatement § 28(4)," which
overstates what I was able to confirm.**

**The honest complication: this is not really a preclusion case.** The
assignment specifically asked me to analyze, not paper over, the distinction
between formal preclusion and what is actually happening here. Collateral
estoppel is a doctrine about whether a **judgment or determination** in one
proceeding binds a party in a later one. The Board is not asking this Court
to treat the federal sentencing's fact-findings as *res judicata* — it is
offering the Case Summary as *reliable hearsay evidence* under § 168-n(3),
admissible and weighable like any other evidence, not as a preclusive prior
adjudication foreclosing the Court's own fact-finding. Framed that way,
formal issue-preclusion doctrine — including the § 28(4) exception — does not
directly govern this case at all, because the elements of collateral estoppel
(actually litigated, necessarily decided, full and fair opportunity) are
answering a different question than "how much weight should a court give
this piece of hearsay."

That said, the analogy still carries real force, and I think it is worth
making for two separate reasons that should be kept distinct in the brief:

1. **For genuinely undisputed PSR facts** (Issue 1's sharper point), the
analogy is close to a knockout, not merely persuasive: such facts fail
collateral estoppel's threshold "actually litigated" requirement in the most
basic sense — nothing was contested, so nothing was actually litigated. If a
formally preclusive judgment could not bind Respondent on such a fact even in
a proceeding using the *same* standard of proof, it is hard to see why an
even weaker vehicle — hearsay evidence recounting an unlitigated fact —
should be treated as sufficient, standing alone, to satisfy SORA's
*clear-and-convincing* burden.

2. **For disputed, preponderance-resolved PSR facts**, the analogy is
exactly the § 28(4) situation in substance, even if formal preclusion
doctrine is not technically invoked: the fact was actually litigated and
determined, but under a materially lighter burden than the one now at stake.
That is precisely the fact pattern § 28(4) and its comment describe. The
argument here is that the same policy that keeps a lighter-burden finding
from binding a party by formal preclusion should inform — not necessarily
mandate, but inform — how much independent weight a SORA court gives that
same finding when it arrives instead as hearsay.

**Conclusion on Issue 2.** The doctrinal building blocks are real and
citable: Restatement § 28(4)'s text is confirmed directly; *Ryan*'s elements
and *Halyalkar*'s fairness-based, flexible approach to collateral estoppel
are corroborated, if not independently read by me. But the bridge from
formal preclusion doctrine to the weight a SORA court gives PSR-sourced
hearsay is, as the assignment anticipated, a persuasive analogy rather than
controlling authority, and it is meaningfully stronger for the
undisputed-fact category than the preponderance-resolved category. The brief
should present it exactly that way — candidly labeled as analogy, with the
undisputed-fact strand argued with more confidence than the
preponderance-resolved strand.

### Issue 3: Has any court addressed this compound question?

I searched this from the four angles the assignment specified — civil
commitment, other-state sex-offender registration, immigration/deportation
reliance on PSRs, and licensing proceedings — plus general NY SORA practice.

**Civil commitment.** SVP civil commitment schemes typically require clear
and convincing evidence for initial commitment and (per one federal-scheme
source) preponderance for release; I found nothing addressing whether a PSR
fact from a separate, prior federal sentencing can satisfy the commitment
proceeding's own C&C burden. This searches into the same territory as the
existing brief's Article 10 analogy at Point III.C but from the opposite
direction — Article 10's own bare-conviction rule (MHL § 10.07[d]) shows what
New York does when it *legislates* an evidentiary floor for a dangerousness
finding; it does not address the standard-of-proof-mismatch question this
memo raises.

**Other states' SORA-equivalent statutes.** General background confirms
PSRs and police reports are commonly used inputs into other states' risk
assessments (e.g., materials cited for Nebraska, general risk-assessment
practice sources), but I found no case addressing a challenge to that
practice on standard-of-proof grounds.

**Deportation proceedings.** This angle produced the most useful comparator,
though still not a squarely on-point case. *Woodby v. INS*, 385 U.S. 276
(1966), held that no deportation order may enter unless the government
proves deportability by "clear, unequivocal, and convincing evidence" —
language I was unable to fetch directly from the Library of Congress's
hosted U.S. Reports PDF (403 error) or any other primary mirror this session,
so this is **(b-corroborated)**, matched across independent search
renderings including a direct quote attributed to the opinion itself
("[w]e have concluded that it is incumbent upon the Government in such
proceedings to establish the facts supporting deportability by clear,
unequivocal, and convincing evidence"), but not independently confirmed by
me against the primary text. *Woodby* is useful background — it shows the
Supreme Court itself treating a heightened evidentiary standard as
constitutionally compelled by the severity of the deportation consequence,
which is directly analogous to *Gillotti*'s and this brief's framing of
SORA's own C&C standard as tracking the severity of what a Level Three
classification does to a person. But *Woodby* does not address a PSR-to-
deportation evidentiary chain specifically, and I found no case that does.

**Conclusion on Issue 3.** This is, on the research conducted, a
first-impression question — consistent with the pattern already established
for the Rule 32(i)(3)(B)/later-reliance theory in the companion memo. I state
that candidly, not to inflate the theory's novelty, but because a genuinely
multi-angle search did not turn up authority either way.

### Issue 4: Assessing the client's proposed remedy, and its effect on the discovery demand

**The proposed remedy, restated precisely.** The client's framing: before any
PSR-sourced fact can properly support a clear-and-convincing SORA finding,
the Board/DA should have to independently verify the underlying source
documents (police reports, forensic reports, interview memoranda) the
probation officer relied on to prepare the PSR.

**Why this is half right.** Document verification directly answers the
Point-II-style provenance/accuracy problem: it lets the Court and Respondent
confirm the Case Summary's characterizations actually track what the
underlying documents say, closing the compounding-paraphrase gap Point II
already identifies (police report → probation officer's summary → Board's
further paraphrase). That is a real and valuable function, and this new
theory gives it an *additional* reason to matter: without the underlying
record, nobody — not Respondent, not this Court — can tell which category
(undisputed/no-showing, disputed-and-preponderance-resolved, or
disputed-and-left-unresolved under Rule 32(i)(3)(B)) any given fact falls
into. That categorization is the entire ballgame for how strong this
standard-of-proof argument is as applied to any specific fact, and it is
currently unknowable from the record before the Court.

**Why it is not, by itself, sufficient.** Verification answers "does the
Case Summary accurately restate the PSR, and does the PSR accurately restate
the police report?" It does not answer "was this fact ever proven to a
standard adequate to support a clear-and-convincing SORA finding?" A
perfectly, verifiably accurate quotation of a PSR fact that was never
disputed at federal sentencing is, after verification, still a fact nobody
was required to prove to any standard. Verification of the paper trail
cannot retroactively supply the evidentiary showing that never happened. The
more precise remedy, consistent with how Correction Law § 168-n(3) itself is
structured (the People "bear the burden of proving the facts supporting the
determinations sought" — not "producing a case summary describing the
facts"), is that **the People must independently prove, at the SORA hearing
itself, by clear and convincing evidence, any fact that was undisputed or
resolved only by a preponderance at the underlying federal sentencing** —
through live testimony, sworn statements, or other admissible evidence that
stands on its own rather than borrowing the PSR's characterization wholesale.
Document verification is the necessary predicate to that showing (it is how
Respondent and the Court find out which facts need independent proof in the
first place), but the remedy itself is an independent evidentiary showing at
the SORA hearing, not merely better-sourced paperwork.

**Effect on the existing § 168-n(3) demand.** This theory meaningfully
strengthens the case for the demand already on file in
`SORA-DEMAND-FOR-DISCLOSURE-AND-SUBPOENA-DRAFT.md`, and suggests a specific
expansion. The existing demand (Items 2–3) already seeks the complete 2012
and 2018 PSRs or, at minimum, the specific pages the Case Summary draws from.
That framing is aimed at Point II's foundation/accuracy problem. This new
theory supplies an independent reason to demand something the current draft
does not explicitly request: **the Rule 32(i)(3)(C) determinations —
the court's own written record of which PSR portions were disputed and how
each dispute was resolved (ruled on, found non-germane, or simply never
raised) — appended to each PSR**, and, to the extent obtainable, the
sentencing transcript passages reflecting whether any of the specific facts
the Case Summary relies on (image/video counts, the 2010 interview
admissions, the "not deterred" characterization) were ever actually
contested. This is not a new item so much as sharpening why Items 2, 3, and 5
of the existing demand matter and adding the Rule 32(i)(3)(C) determinations
specifically by name, since that document is the one place the federal
record would show, on its face, which facts fall into which of the three
categories this memo's argument depends on. Recommendation 3 of the companion
PSR-reliability memo already flags obtaining the transcripts and any Rule 32
addenda as necessary fact-gathering; this memo gives that recommendation
independent legal weight rather than treating it as background diligence —
it is now the specific evidentiary predicate this new theory needs to be
argued fact-by-fact rather than only in the abstract. As before, the existing
demand's own scope limitation is correct and should stay: § 168-n(3)'s
subpoena mechanism reaches state and local records-holders, not the federal
court's own file, so any Rule 32(i)(3)(C) determinations or transcript
excerpts not voluntarily provided by the Board or the federal probation
office would have to be pursued in the federal dockets directly, not through
this demand's own subpoena power.

**Conclusion on Issue 4.** The client's proposed remedy correctly identifies
that something more than the Board's bare paraphrase is required, but
mislocates where the real cure lies. Verification of underlying documents is
necessary but not sufficient; the operative remedy is an independent
evidentiary showing, at the SORA hearing itself, of any fact that was not
proven to a heightened standard at the federal sentencing. The theory
strengthens, and should modestly expand, the existing discovery demand — not
by changing its architecture, but by adding the Rule 32(i)(3)(C)
determinations as a named target and reframing why the PSR pages and
transcripts already sought matter.

## 5. CONCLUSION

On the underlying legal premise (Issue 1), I have high confidence that
Second Circuit sentencing law applies preponderance, not clear and
convincing, to disputed facts, sourced through a document I read in full;
moderate confidence, pending better sourcing, in the sharper and more useful
point that undisputed facts may not be proven to any standard at all.

On the preclusion analogy (Issue 2), I have moderate confidence the
doctrinal building blocks (Restatement § 28(4), *Ryan*, *Halyalkar*) are
accurately described and moderate-to-high confidence New York's flexible
collateral estoppel doctrine would be receptive to the analogy, but only
low-to-moderate confidence that a court would accept it as anything more
than persuasive reasoning, precisely because — as the assignment anticipated
— this is not squarely a preclusion case. I would present the
undisputed-fact strand of this argument with more confidence than the
preponderance-resolved strand.

On the first-impression question (Issue 3), I have the same confidence level
already established for the parallel Rule 32(i)(3)(B) theory in the
companion memo: genuinely novel, genuinely unopposed by contrary authority,
low-to-moderate confidence about reception absent supporting precedent.

On the remedy (Issue 4), I have high confidence that the client's proposed
verification step is necessary but insufficient, and that the correct
framing — an independent evidentiary showing at the SORA hearing, informed
by document verification rather than satisfied by it — is the more precise
and more defensible ask.

**Overall assessment of how this theory fits into the existing three-Point
memorandum:** This is best presented as a *reinforcing* argument within or
immediately following Point II, not as a freestanding fourth Point. It does
not independently carry the departure or override arguments the way Points I
and III do; its natural function is to sharpen why the Board's Case
Summary — even if the Court rejects the federal/state provenance distinction
Point II draws — still cannot properly be given the weight of clear-and-
convincing proof, because what stands behind it was never proven to that
standard in the first place. The undisputed-fact strand of this argument is
the strongest single piece of it and should be led with; the formal
preclusion analogy should be candidly flagged as persuasive reasoning, not
controlling law, exactly as the assignment instructed.
