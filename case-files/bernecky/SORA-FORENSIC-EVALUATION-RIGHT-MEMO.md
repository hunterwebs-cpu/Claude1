## 1. INTRODUCTION

This memorandum was commissioned as a follow-on research assignment to reinforce Point III of the draft *Memorandum of Law in Support of Respondent's Application for a Downward Departure* (the "Departure Memo"), specifically the § 168-l(6) conjunctive-statute argument and its Article 10 evidentiary analogy in Point III.C. It does not revisit Points I or II, and it does not re-derive the existing Boreali or Gunter/Moss analysis; it assumes familiarity with the Departure Memo and builds on it.

The assignment asked five sequenced questions: (1) whether SORA (Correction Law Article 6-C) itself gives a respondent any right to a forensic evaluation; (2) whether Mental Hygiene Law ("MHL") Article 10 gives that right, and whether it is funded for an indigent respondent; (3) whether any New York statute or case law extends a funded-expert right to SORA specifically, including County Law § 722-c and a direct *Mathews v. Eldridge* due-process argument; (4) what *Ake v. Oklahoma* actually holds and how far it can honestly be stretched toward a SORA context; and (5) what Level 3's specific added practical consequences are, confirmed against the statute itself.

**Research method and limitations, stated plainly up front.** I do not have access to Westlaw, Lexis, or a paid citator (Shepard's/KeyCite) in this environment. My primary-law research was conducted by (a) direct retrieval of New York statutory text from the New York Senate's official codification site (nysenate.gov) and cross-checked against Justia and FindLaw's republications of the same Consolidated Laws sections; and (b) direct reading of full opinion PDFs where I could obtain them (this was possible for *Harvey v. Commonwealth* via vacourts.gov, and for the New York State Defenders Association's practice guide on County Law § 722-c). Several primary sources I attempted to reach were blocked by Cloudflare bot-challenges (nycourts.gov's Reporter, law.justia.com's case pages, courtlistener.com) or required an authenticated login (Google Scholar). Where I could not read a case's full opinion myself, I say so explicitly below and do not present it as independently verified. **No citator check was possible for any case cited in this memorandum** — that is a gap, not an omission, and is repeated in the mandatory NOT VERIFIED section at the end. Every case cited here needs a Westlaw/Lexis validity check before the Writer relies on it in a filing.

**Bottom line, stated at the outset:** No provision of SORA itself, and no New York case, gives an indigent SORA respondent a right to a funded forensic evaluation to contest the Board's "threat to public safety" finding. That gap is real and, on this record, appears to be a genuine and so-far-untested one. County Law § 722-c — the general New York indigent-expert-funding statute — does extend by its own text to Article 6-C (SORA) proceedings, and at least one Second Department case (found only in a secondary source, not independently verified — see Section 8) suggests a SORA respondent may obtain a § 722-c expert on a proper showing of necessity. That is a real, useable tool for this case regardless of what happens with the Article 10 analogy, and should be flagged to the Writer as a separate, affirmative avenue: Respondent may be able to *ask* for a funded expert under § 722-c now, rather than only arguing after the fact that SORA denies him one.

## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1:** Does Correction Law Article 6-C (SORA), §§ 168-a through 168-w, anywhere give a respondent a right to request or obtain a forensic psychological/psychiatric evaluation — funded or otherwise — to contest a risk-level determination or the "threat to public safety" finding?

**Conclusion:** No. I reviewed the full list of Article 6-C's sections and read §§ 168-l and 168-n (the sections governing the Board's classification standard and the judicial hearing procedure) directly. Neither contains any such provision. The Board itself "may consider" psychological or psychiatric profiles in developing its Guidelines, but that describes an input into the Board's own guideline-drafting process — not a right the *offender* holds to obtain an evaluation of his own.

**Issue 2:** Does MHL Article 10 give a respondent a right to an expert/psychiatric examination, and is any part of that funded for an indigent respondent?

**Conclusion:** Yes, both. MHL § 10.06(e) gives the respondent the right to request court-ordered evaluation by a psychiatric examiner, and provides explicitly: "If the respondent is financially unable to obtain an examiner, the court shall appoint an examiner of the respondent's choice to be paid within the limits prescribed by law." This is a real, funded, indigent-specific right built directly into the statute — not merely a general due-process gloss. It sharpens the existing Article 10 analogy in Point III.C considerably: Article 10 does not just require clinical proof before a dangerousness finding (already argued in the Departure Memo); it hands the respondent the tools to contest that proof. SORA does neither.

**Issue 3:** Is there any New York statutory or case-law right to funded expert assistance for an indigent respondent in a SORA proceeding specifically?

**Conclusion:** Partially yes, and this is the most actionable finding in this memorandum. County Law § 722-c — New York's general indigent-expert-funding statute — by its own text extends to "the defendant or other person described in section two hundred forty-nine or section two hundred sixty-two of the family court act, article six-c of the correction law or section four hundred seven of the surrogate's court procedure act." Article 6-C is SORA. This is not an analogy or an extension by a court; the Legislature wrote SORA respondents directly into the funding statute's own text. A secondary source (a New York State Defenders Association practice guide, not independently verified against the primary opinion — see Section 8) cites *People v. Linton*, 94 A.D.3d 962 (2d Dep't 2012), for the proposition that a SORA respondent may be entitled to a § 722-c expert on a showing of necessity, though necessity was not established on that record. On the separate question of a freestanding *Mathews v. Eldridge* due-process claim (independent of § 722-c): New York courts have applied *Mathews* to SORA proceedings generally — *People v. David W.*, 95 N.Y.2d 130, 138 (2000), holds that "[t]he bedrock of due process is notice and opportunity to be heard," citing *Mathews*, 424 U.S. at 333 — but I found no New York case applying that framework specifically to a claimed right to expert assistance in SORA. That appears to be untested territory, not foreclosed territory.

**Issue 4:** What does *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68 (1985), actually hold, and how far can it honestly be stretched toward SORA?

**Conclusion:** *Ake* holds that when an indigent criminal defendant shows his sanity is likely to be a significant factor at trial, due process requires the State to provide access to a competent psychiatrist to assist in evaluation, preparation, and presentation of the defense — and separately, that a capital defendant facing the State's psychiatric evidence of future dangerousness is entitled to responsive psychiatric assistance at sentencing. It is a criminal-trial/capital-sentencing doctrine, decided under a presumption of innocence, and I found no case — in New York or any other state — extending it to a sex-offender registration/classification proceeding. The single closest analog I located and read in full, *Harvey v. Commonwealth* (Va. 2019), is a Virginia Supreme Court case declining, 4–3, to extend *Ake* to a *civil* proceeding (an SVP conditional-release revocation hearing) — and even that case is more useful to Respondent than it first appears, because Virginia's own SVP statute affirmatively entitles an indigent respondent to a court-appointed expert at the *initial* commitment hearing; only the narrower, later, expedited revocation hearing was held not to independently require one. Bernecky's SORA hearing is analytically closer to that initial hearing than to the narrow revocation hearing *Harvey* actually addressed. The distance between *Ake* and SORA is real and should be presented candidly as a persuasive analogy, not controlling authority — exactly as the Departure Memo already does with Boreali and Article 10.

**Issue 5:** What are Level 3's specific added practical consequences beyond Level 2's, confirmed against the statute?

**Conclusion:** Confirmed and more concrete than the assignment's framing assumed. The starkest, most citable differences are: (a) Level 3 offenders must personally verify their address with local law enforcement every 90 calendar days, Correction Law § 168-h, on top of an annual in-person photo appearance under § 168-f — Level 2 offenders appear in person only once every three years; (b) Level 2 offenders who are not predicate sex offenders, sexual predators, or sexually violent offenders may petition for full relief from all registration duties after 30 years, Correction Law § 168-o(1) — Level 3 offenders have no equivalent relief provision at all, ever, only a narrower petition to modify the *level of notification*; and (c) the subdirectory/notification provisions for Level 2 and Level 3 are substantially similar in content and audience (both post name, exact address, and photograph to the public subdirectory and to "entit[ies] with vulnerable populations"), but Level 3's disclosure additionally includes the offender's place of employment address, Correction Law § 168-l(6)(c). **Important candid caveat:** Bernecky is independently designated a "Predicate Sex Offender" (Departure Memo, Statement of Facts, citing Designation Form Ex. A at 2-3), which — on the statutory text I read — excludes him from the § 168-o(1) thirty-year relief provision *regardless of risk level*. The lifetime-registration contrast between Level 2 and Level 3 is real as a matter of the statute's general design, and supports the "meaningfully more severe" argument in the abstract, but it is not, on this specific record, a consequence that would change for Bernecky personally between Level 2 and Level 3. That should be stated accurately if used.

## 4. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### Issue 1: SORA itself contains no evaluation right

**Rule and analysis.** I obtained the complete list of Article 6-C's 24 sections (§ 168 through § 168-w) directly from the New York Senate's codification site. None is titled, or on inspection contains, any provision addressing a respondent's right to a forensic evaluation. I read § 168-l (the Board of Examiners section, including subsection 6's risk-level standards) and § 168-n (the judicial-determination/hearing section, including its right-to-counsel and reliable-hearsay provisions) directly. Both confirm what the Departure Memo already argues about § 168-l(6)'s conjunctive "high risk of repeat offense AND threat to public safety" standard, and neither contains any provision permitting the respondent to request or obtain a psychological or psychiatric evaluation, funded or otherwise. Section 168-l(5)(e) permits the *Board* to consider "psychological or psychiatric profiles" in developing its own Guidelines — an input into rulemaking, not a right held by the individual offender to be evaluated or to rebut the Board's finding with his own expert. Section 168-n(3) provides for assignment of *counsel* to an indigent respondent (already the subject of the companion Application for Assigned Counsel) but is silent on experts entirely.

**Validation status.** Read by me directly from the New York Senate's official codification (nysenate.gov), via an automated fetch-and-summarize tool rather than raw HTML I paginated through myself — this is the best access available in this environment, but it is worth flagging that I did not personally scroll through every line of statutory text; I relied on the fetch tool's extraction being accurate, and cross-checked the § 168-l(6) risk-level language against the language already quoted (and presumably independently confirmed) in the Departure Memo, which it matches exactly.

**Significance to Point III.** This confirms, rather than surprises: it is the silence Point III.C's Article 10 comparison is built on. It is worth stating in the brief itself, plainly, that this silence is total — Article 6-C, unlike Article 10 (see Issue 2), contains no procedural mechanism of any kind by which a respondent can generate his own clinical evidence to rebut the Board's "threat to public safety" finding, whether he can afford one or not.

### Issue 2: MHL Article 10 gives the respondent a funded right to a psychiatric examiner

**Rule.** MHL § 10.06(d) permits the Attorney General to request that the court order the respondent to submit to a psychiatric examination; if the respondent is then unrepresented, the court must appoint counsel. Separately and centrally, MHL § 10.06(e) gives the *respondent* the mirror-image right: "At any time after the filing of a sex offender civil management petition, and prior to trial, the respondent may request the court in which the petition is pending to order that he or she be evaluated by a psychiatric examiner." The critical sentence for this case: **"If the respondent is financially unable to obtain an examiner, the court shall appoint an examiner of the respondent's choice to be paid within the limits prescribed by law."** The examiner then reports findings in writing to the respondent or counsel, to the Attorney General, and to the court.

**Validation status.** I obtained this exact language independently from three separate sources — Justia's, FindLaw's, and the New York Senate's own codification of MHL § 10.06 — and all three produced matching text for subsections (d) and (e), including the identical "shall appoint an examiner ... to be paid within the limits prescribed by law" language. This cross-validation across independent republications of the same statute gives me high confidence the quoted language is accurate, though — as with Issue 1 — I obtained it through an automated fetch-and-extract tool rather than reading the raw statutory text character-by-character myself, and it should still be spot-checked against a McKinney's or Westlaw copy before the exact quotation is filed with the court.

**Significance to Point III.C.** This is the sharpening the assignment asked for. The Departure Memo's existing Article 10 analogy (Point III.C) argues that Article 10 requires an actual clinical finding before a dangerousness determination — MHL § 10.07(d)'s bar on finding mental abnormality "solely on the basis of the respondent's commission of a sex offense," and § 10.05(g)'s requirement of a psychiatric examiner's report. That argument stands on its own and this memorandum does not disturb it. What § 10.06(e) adds is the other half of the comparison: Article 10 does not just *require* the state to build its dangerousness case on clinical evidence — it *equips the respondent*, funded if indigent, to generate his own clinical evidence to contest it. The comparison to SORA becomes sharper and more concrete: in the one place New York has legislated how to prove *and how to contest* current sexual dangerousness by clear and convincing evidence, the respondent gets an examiner. In SORA, he gets nothing — not even the *chance* to ask a court for one under the statute itself (see Issue 1). This is a genuinely stronger formulation than "Article 10 requires clinical proof"; it is "Article 10 requires clinical proof and arms the respondent to test it, and SORA does neither."

The same honest limitation the Departure Memo already states about the Article 10 analogy generally applies here with equal force and should not be dropped: this remains a persuasive analogy between two different statutory schemes with different stakes (indefinite civil confinement versus a registration/notification classification), not a case holding SORA must import Article 10's evidentiary machinery. The Board and the People may fairly respond that the Legislature knew how to write an Article 10-style expert-access provision into SORA and chose not to. That response does not disappear because this memorandum sharpens the comparison; it should be met the same way Point III.C already meets it — head-on, not avoided.

### Issue 3: County Law § 722-c reaches SORA proceedings; a direct *Mathews* claim remains untested

**Rule — the § 722-c text itself.** County Law § 722-c authorizes a court, on an ex parte finding that "investigative, expert or other services are necessary" and that "the defendant or other person described in section two hundred forty-nine or section two hundred sixty-two of the family court act, article six-c of the correction law or section four hundred seven of the surrogate's court procedure act, is financially unable to obtain them," to "authorize counsel ... to obtain the services on behalf of the defendant or such other person." "Article six-c of the correction law" is SORA. This is not judicial extension or analogy; the statute's *own text* names SORA respondents as eligible applicants, on the same footing as Family Court Act respondents and Surrogate's Court Procedure Act respondents.

**Validation status of the statutory text.** I obtained this "article six-c of the correction law" language independently from three sources: a New York State Defenders Association practice guide (November 2016, by named staff attorneys, with a full citation apparatus — not an anonymous or AI-fingerprint document, but still a secondary source, treated accordingly below), newyork.public.law's republication of the statute, and a WebSearch synthesis drawing on nysenate.gov's own codification. All three produced matching "article six-C of the correction law" language. I am confident this is the accurate current statutory text, though again I did not personally read raw unprocessed HTML character-by-character. Note that the current per-provider compensation cap appears to be $3,000 (per the newyork.public.law fetch), an increase from the $1,000 cap described in the 2016 NYSDA guide — consistent with a legislative amendment since 2016 rather than an inconsistency in the sources; the current cap figure should be independently confirmed before being cited in a filing, since I could not pin down exactly when it changed.

**The one case found — flagged prominently as NOT independently verified.** The NYSDA guide cites, in a table of caselaw organized by expert type, under the heading "SORA": "*People v Linton*, 94 AD3d 962 (2nd Dept 2012) [The defendant in SORA proceeding may be entitled to the appointment of expert upon finding that services are necessary; necessity not established in instant case]." This is exactly the case this memorandum needed to find, and if accurately described it is directly on point: a published Second Department decision confirming that § 722-c expert funding is available in a SORA proceeding, denying it there only for a case-specific failure to establish necessity — meaning the door is open on a properly supported application. **I was unable to independently locate or read this opinion.** I attempted nycourts.gov's Reporter (blocked by a Cloudflare bot-challenge), law.justia.com's case database (same), courtlistener.com (403 error), Leagle (no matching result found), and Google Scholar (redirected to an authentication wall I could not pass). This citation should therefore be treated as **reconstructed from a secondary source only** — real, professionally produced, and citing itself to a specific reporter volume and page, which gives it more credibility than an unsourced blog claim, but still unconfirmed against the actual opinion text. Before this case is cited in any filing, the Writer or attorney must independently pull it on Westlaw/Lexis, confirm the quoted parenthetical accurately reflects the holding, and Shepardize/KeyCite it. Do not quote language from it as though it were confirmed; at most, cite the existence of the case and its general proposition pending independent confirmation, or omit it until confirmed.

**The direct *Mathews v. Eldridge* due-process route.** Independent of § 722-c, New York courts have squarely held SORA proceedings subject to procedural due process analysis generally. *People v. David W.*, 95 N.Y.2d 130, 138 (2000), holds that "[t]he bedrock of due process is notice and opportunity to be heard," expressly citing *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319, 333 (1976), and holding that a defendant on probation when SORA took effect was entitled to notice and an opportunity to be heard before his risk-level determination was made. I located this holding through a search-engine synthesis citing the case's exact language and pincite, and was not able to independently pull and read the full *David W.* opinion myself in this environment; treat the quoted language as (b) reconstructed from a secondary source pending independent confirmation, though the case's core holding (SORA is subject to *Mathews* balancing) is well-established enough in the secondary literature that I have reasonable confidence in it. What I did *not* find is any New York case applying *Mathews* balancing specifically to a claimed right to expert assistance in a SORA proceeding. That silence cuts both ways, exactly as Point III.A of the Departure Memo already argues about the override-conjunctive-statute question: no case has squarely decided it, which means it has never been tested, not that it has been rejected. A freestanding *Mathews* argument — independent of § 722-c — is available to be made for the first time here, following the same "first-to-raise-it" candor the Departure Memo already uses for the Boreali argument.

### Issue 4: *Ake v. Oklahoma* — the actual holding, and how far it honestly reaches

**The actual holding, with pincites.** I obtained the text of *Ake* directly from Cornell's Legal Information Institute (a verbatim reproduction of the U.S. Reports), and separately read extensive verbatim block quotations of *Ake* embedded in *Harvey v. Commonwealth* (see below), which I read in full. The core holding: "When a defendant has made a preliminary showing that his sanity at the time of the offense is likely to be a significant factor at trial, the Constitution requires that a State provide access to a psychiatrist's assistance on this issue if the defendant cannot otherwise afford one." *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 74 (1985). More precisely, at 83: "[W]hen a defendant demonstrates to the trial judge that his sanity at the time of the offense is to be a significant factor at trial, the State must, at a minimum, assure the defendant access to a competent psychiatrist who will conduct an appropriate examination and assist in evaluation, preparation, and presentation of the defense. This is not to say, of course, that the indigent defendant has a constitutional right to choose a psychiatrist of his personal liking or to receive funds to hire his own." A separate holding addresses capital sentencing specifically: where the State presents psychiatric evidence of a capital defendant's future dangerousness, "due process requires access to a psychiatric examination on relevant issues, to the testimony of the psychiatrist, and to assistance in preparation at the sentencing phase." *Id.* at 83–84.

**Doctrinal context, stated candidly.** *Ake* is a criminal-trial and capital-sentencing case. It is decided under a presumption of innocence, in a proceeding where "constitutional protections reach their apex" (the Virginia Supreme Court's own characterization of *Ake*, discussed below), and its "future dangerousness" holding is specifically tied to capital sentencing, not to any general civil or administrative dangerousness finding. Extending it to SORA — a civil registration and community-notification proceeding that follows a conviction already obtained, with no threat of confinement — is a genuinely long analogical reach, further than either the Boreali or Article 10 analogies already in Point III.C, and it should be presented to the Court with exactly that candor.

**The closest analog found: *Harvey v. Commonwealth*, 297 Va. ___, 829 S.E.2d 534 (Va. 2019).** I obtained and read this opinion in full (not a summary — the complete 26-page slip opinion, majority and dissent, via vacourts.gov). It is the single most useful piece of authority this research turned up on the *Ake*-extension question, and it cuts both ways in a way that should be presented honestly.

*What it holds.* Two indigent men, both previously adjudicated sexually violent predators ("SVPs") under Virginia's civil-commitment statute, had their conditional release revoked after violating its terms; each sought a court-appointed psychological expert to contest revocation, invoking *Ake*. Applying the *Mathews v. Eldridge* three-factor test (citing *Ake* itself, 470 U.S. at 77–80, as an example of a court "applying the *Mathews* three-factor framework"), a majority of the Virginia Supreme Court held the Due Process Clause did *not* require appointment of an expert for that specific hearing. The court's reasoning: the liberty interest at that particular hearing was "diminished" compared to a capital case (a revocation to a term reviewable every six months, not a fixed sentence or death); the State's interest in expedited handling was substantial; and the risk of error was mitigated because the evaluating expert was appointed by the treatment agency rather than the prosecuting authority, the burden of proof rested on the Commonwealth, and the respondent retained a battery of other procedural protections including counsel, cross-examination, and prompt subsequent review.

*What makes it more useful to Respondent than it first appears.* The *Harvey* majority did not hold that indigent SVPs never get an expert. To the contrary, the opinion notes that Virginia's own SVP statute, Code § 37.2-907(A), "entitles an indigent SVP to a court-appointed expert" at the *initial* commitment proceeding — the one that actually adjudicates whether the person is an SVP in the first place. *Harvey*, slip op. at 25 (Mims, J., dissenting, describing the majority's own framework). What the majority declined to extend *Ake* to was a narrower, later, expedited, non-adversarial *revocation* hearing that follows the initial adjudication — not the classification hearing itself. Bernecky's September 11 hearing is a classification hearing, not a revocation hearing; it is the *Harvey* analog to the proceeding Virginia's own statute already funds an expert for, not the one the Virginia court declined to extend funding to. Presented honestly, *Harvey* is weaker support for the proposition "no state extends *Ake*-type reasoning to sex-offender proceedings" than a bare citation might suggest — the closest state to actually confront the question funds the expert at the classification stage and only balked at a later, narrower, follow-on hearing.

*The dissent.* Three of the seven justices (Mims, J., joined by Goodwyn and Powell, JJ.) would have extended *Ake* to the revocation hearing too, reasoning that "the appointment of a mental health expert is essentially an extension of the right to counsel," *Harvey*, slip op. at 22 (quoting *Ake*, 470 U.S. at 83), and that denying an indigent SVP an expert produces exactly the "unconstitutionally disparate treatment that disadvantaged the indigent criminal defendant solely because of his or her relative poverty" that *Ake* itself was written to prevent. *Id.* at 23–24 (citing *Ake*, 470 U.S. at 81–82 & n.8). This is not controlling authority — it is a losing dissent in a sister state's highest court, on a different statutory scheme, addressing a different (narrower) kind of hearing than Bernecky's. But it demonstrates that *Ake*'s extension into civil sex-offender proceedings is a live, seriously contested question among sitting appellate judges, not a settled non-starter — three votes out of seven is not a fringe position.

**No case extending *Ake* to a registration-only (non-commitment) proceeding.** I found no case — in New York, Virginia, or any other jurisdiction searched — extending *Ake* or similar due-process reasoning specifically to a sex-offender *registration/classification* proceeding of the kind SORA conducts, as opposed to a civil-commitment proceeding like Virginia's or New York's own Article 10. That is a real gap and should be stated as such, not glossed over. A search for whether Massachusetts's Sex Offender Registry Board process — a genuine, non-commitment registration classification scheme structurally closer to SORA than any civil-commitment statute — provides indigent respondents with a funded expert did not yield a confirmable answer; the relevant source (publiccounsel.net, the Massachusetts public defender's SORB overview) could not be retrieved in this environment (403 error), and this should be treated as an open research question rather than a confirmed negative or positive.

**How to use this in the brief, stated plainly.** *Ake* should be offered exactly the way the Departure Memo already offers Boreali and Article 10: as a persuasive analogy that this Court is not bound to follow, presented with its real limits stated up front rather than discovered by the Board on reply. The honest version of the argument is not "*Ake* requires a psychiatric expert in SORA proceedings." It is closer to: "New York and its sister states have built funded expert-access rights into every proceeding this Court's own case law treats as analogous to SORA's dangerousness inquiry — Article 10 by statute, SVP civil commitment in the one other state to confront the question directly — and the doctrinal root of all of them, *Ake v. Oklahoma*, rests on the same premise Point III.C already argues from Article 10: that an indigent respondent cannot be asked to contest a clinical dangerousness finding with no clinical tools of his own. SORA's total silence on the question — not just its lower evidentiary bar, but its complete absence of *any* mechanism for the respondent to generate contrary evidence — is what makes this case, and this respondent, a genuinely untested one."

### Issue 5: Level 3's confirmed added consequences

I obtained the following directly from Correction Law §§ 168-f, 168-h, 168-l(6), and 168-o via the New York Senate's codification, through the same fetch-and-extract method described in Issue 1, and treat the confidence level accordingly (high, given the internal consistency of the results and their consistency with the existing Departure Memo's quotation of § 168-l(6), but not a substitute for a McKinney's cite-check).

**Verification frequency.** Correction Law § 168-f distinguishes in-person appearance frequency by level: a Level 3 offender "shall personally appear at the law enforcement agency having jurisdiction within twenty days of the first anniversary [of his initial registration] ... and every year thereafter," while a Level 1 or Level 2 offender need personally appear only "within twenty days of the third anniversary ... and every three years thereafter." Separately, § 168-h requires that "a sex offender designated a level three risk ... shall personally verify his or her address every ninety calendar days with the local law enforcement agency" — a quarterly, in-person address-verification obligation that has no Level 2 equivalent at all.

**Duration and relief from registration.** Both Level 2 and Level 3 registration nominally run "annually for life" under § 168-h. But § 168-o(1) permits "[a]ny sex offender who is classified as a level two risk, and who has not been designated a sexual predator, or a sexually violent offender, or a predicate sex offender," to petition for full relief from all further registration and verification duties after being "registered for a minimum period of thirty years." **Level 3 offenders have no comparable provision anywhere in Article 6-C** — § 168-o's other subdivisions allow any offender, at any level, to petition to *modify the level of notification* (i.e., seek a downward reclassification), but there is no mechanism by which a Level 3 offender can ever be relieved of the registration and verification duty itself, as a Level 2 offender who is not otherwise disqualified eventually can. This is a genuine, structural, and — as far as I could determine — undisputed asymmetry built into the statute, and it supports the argument that the Legislature itself treats Level 3 as categorically different from Level 2, not merely one increment more severe on a continuum. **As flagged in the Issues section above, this specific point does not change Bernecky's own personal exposure, because his independent status as a "Predicate Sex Offender" already excludes him from § 168-o(1) relief regardless of risk level** — this should be used, if at all, as evidence of the statute's general design and the stakes the Legislature itself attached to the distinction, not as a claim that Bernecky specifically loses a 30-year exit ramp he would otherwise have had.

**Subdirectory and notification scope.** Correction Law § 168-l(6)(b) and (c), and § 168-q, both direct that Level 2 and Level 3 information — name, aliases, exact address, and photograph — is provided to the public subdirectory and to law enforcement's compiled listing of "vulnerable organizational entities" (schools, day care centers, nursing homes, places of worship, and similar). The disclosure regime for the two levels is substantially similar in structure and audience. The one confirmed textual difference is that Level 3's disclosure additionally includes "address of the offender's place of employment," Correction Law § 168-l(6)(c) — information not listed among what may be disclosed for Level 2 offenders under § 168-l(6)(b). This is real but narrower than a lay assumption of "Level 3 means everyone finds out, Level 2 means almost no one does" — the assignment's premise should be recalibrated accordingly: the more significant, concrete differences confirmed by the statutory text are the verification-frequency and no-exit-ramp distinctions above, not a dramatically broader public-disclosure regime at Level 3 as such.

**SORA does not itself impose residency or employment-proximity restrictions.** I did not find, and did not expect to find, any provision of Article 6-C itself restricting where a registrant may live or work based on risk level; New York's residency-restriction landscape (where it exists) arises from local law and from parole/probation conditions, not from SORA's own text. The assignment's framing — "in many localities effective residency/proximity restrictions" — should be understood as a reference to that separate, non-SORA legal landscape, which this memorandum did not independently research and which should not be conflated with SORA's own statutory consequences in the brief without separate confirmation.

## 5. CONCLUSION

On the central question, the answer is unambiguous: **no provision of SORA gives Bernecky, or any respondent, a right to a forensic evaluation to contest the "threat to public safety" finding — funded or otherwise — and no New York court has held there is a constitutional right to one either.** That silence is real, and Article 10's contrasting, funded, respondent-side examination right (MHL § 10.06(e)) sharpens the existing Point III.C analogy meaningfully: it is no longer just "Article 10 requires clinical proof," but "Article 10 requires clinical proof and arms the respondent to test it, and SORA does neither."

The *Ake* analogy is real but should be presented as the most distant of the three analogies now available to Point III.C (Boreali, Article 10, and *Ake*) — a criminal/capital doctrine, extended nowhere to a registration-only proceeding, and declined by the one state supreme court to confront it squarely in an adjacent civil-commitment context, albeit by a bare majority over a substantial dissent, and in a way that (on close reading) may cut in Respondent's favor given what Virginia's own statute does at the *initial* commitment stage.

The most concretely useful, non-analogical finding is County Law § 722-c's direct statutory reach into SORA proceedings. This is not merely rhetorical ammunition for a brief; it is a potential affirmative motion Respondent's assigned counsel can actually file in this case, separate from and in addition to the departure argument, to try to obtain the very evaluation SORA itself will not provide.

On Level 3's specific added consequences, the confirmed, citable differences are narrower but more concrete than a generic "Level 3 is much worse" framing: quarterly in-person address verification with no Level 2 equivalent, and — for offenders not independently disqualified as predicate/predator/violent offenders — the complete absence of any eventual exit from registration that Level 2 offenders can eventually obtain. Both should be used candidly, including the honest caveat that the second does not personally benefit Bernecky given his predicate-offender status.
