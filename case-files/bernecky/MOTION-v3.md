# DEFENDANT'S MOTION TO MODIFY CONDITIONS OF SUPERVISED RELEASE
## PURSUANT TO 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2), FED. R. CRIM. P. 32.1(c), & U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4

Defendant Jeffrey Bernecky, proceeding *pro se*, respectfully moves this Court, pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2) and Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32.1(c), to modify the conditions of supervised release imposed by the Judgment entered June 12, 2018 (Dkt. 28). In support of this motion, Mr. Bernecky states as follows.


## PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

The conditions that govern a defendant's reintegration are almost always fixed years before that reintegration begins, at a sentencing hearing where the room's attention is on one question only — how much time the defendant will spend in prison — and rarely on how a computer-monitoring clause will read once that defendant is applying for jobs, or what a treatment provider will demand of him once a program ordered in the abstract acquires an actual administrator with an actual set of rules. The years that separate sentencing from the start of supervision produce exactly the gap this motion addresses: conditions whose text was never tested against a real circumstance, now being tested against several of them at once.

Mr. Bernecky's case shows what that gap looks like in practice, and it produced real consequences before his supervision had even started. He was sentenced in 2018. His ten-year term of supervision commenced on or about July 30, 2026. In the interval, an employer that had offered him permanent, full-time work read Special Condition (a) on its own and grew concerned that a monitoring provision written for Mr. Bernecky might extend to the company's own systems — a possibility no one at sentencing was ever asked to consider. Probation gave two different informal answers to that concern, neither in writing and neither consistent with the other, and the employer told Mr. Bernecky not to report to work until the question was resolved. He went roughly two weeks without pay, at the exact moment he was completing his transition out of Bureau of Prisons custody and had just taken on a one-year apartment lease. In the course of that same job search, Mr. Bernecky was separately told that Special Condition (c) — a condition aimed at deliberate contact with children — would bar him from working anywhere that merely hires or serves anyone under eighteen, a reading that would eliminate an enormous share of entry-level employment. And during a pre-supervision meeting, his probation officer handed him a risk-notification condition the Second Circuit had already held invalid and asked him to sign an acknowledgment of it — the same language this District's own standing order had directed be removed from every judgment in the district, more than seven years earlier.

None of this is a single bad actor or a single unlucky break. It is the same structural problem recurring across every condition addressed below: text drafted broadly enough to cover the worst case, at a moment when nobody needed to test its limits, is now being read as broadly as its words can be stretched by whoever happens to be applying it — an employer's HR department, a treatment provider, a probation officer. Nowhere in this pattern does the 2018 Judgment need to have been wrong for any of it to be true. The problem is what happens after a Judgment goes silent on a question nobody thought to ask, and someone else has to decide what that silence means.

Congress created this authority in Section 3583(e)(2) precisely so that a defendant does not have to wait and find out the hard way, and Rule 32.1's own drafters explained exactly why: "the probationer should be able to obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it." Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's note to subdivision (b) (1979). Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to use that authority now, at the moment the Sentencing Commission's own November 2025 amendment identifies as the right one: the start of supervision. *See* U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a) (2025). This is not a challenge to the 2018 sentence and does not ask the Court to reopen anything the sentencing judge actually decided. It asks for eight sets of corrections, taken up condition by condition in the order the Judgment lists them, so that Mr. Bernecky serves the next ten years knowing what each condition actually requires — rather than discovering it, one violation report at a time, from whoever happens to be enforcing it against him.


## JURISDICTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court may "modify, reduce, or enlarge the conditions of supervised release, at any time prior to the expiration or termination of the term of supervised release, pursuant to the provisions of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure relating to the modification of probation." 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2). Rule 32.1(c) codifies that authority for exactly this circumstance: its own drafters explained that "in cases of neglect, overwork, or simply unreasonableness on the part of the probation officer, the probationer should have recourse to the sentencing court when a condition needs clarification or modification." Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's note to subdivision (b) (1979). That recourse is what Mr. Bernecky invokes here.

The authority is general and forward-looking. *See United States v. Lussier*, 104 F.3d 32, 36 (2d Cir. 1997) (the Advisory Committee's Notes explain that "the sentencing court must be able to respond to changes in the [defendant's] circumstances as well as new ideas and methods of rehabilitation"). Much of this motion rests on that kind of change: since 2018, Mr. Bernecky's family circumstances, the technology these conditions were written to address, and the governing law itself have all moved, and the Parts below identify the specific change where one supports the relief requested.

Two features of this authority make now the right time rather than merely a permissible one. First, Congress drew a deliberate line between the two forms of relief § 3583(e) provides: early termination under § 3583(e)(1) is available only "after the expiration of one year of supervised release," while modification under § 3583(e)(2) — the provision invoked here — carries no waiting period at all and is available "at any time." The Sentencing Commission's November 2025 amendment tracks that same distinction, cross-citing each subsection for the timing rule it states, and affirmatively invites courts to reassess existing conditions "in consultation with the probation officer after the defendant's release from imprisonment." U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a)-(b) (2025). Second, the Second Circuit has already directed a district court to use this precise mechanism at this precise moment. In *United States v. Balon*, the court confronted computer-monitoring conditions whose real-world operation could not be predicted years in advance — "it is currently impossible to predict the state of computer technology at the commencement of Balon's supervised release period" — and remanded with instructions to reconsider those conditions under § 3583(e)(2) near the time supervision actually began. 384 F.3d 38, 46-47 (2d Cir. 2004). Mr. Bernecky is at that point now, and the questions below are no longer speculative: several have already been answered, informally and inconsistently, by people other than this Court.

This Court is also the right body to hear these questions. Where Probation has read a condition to reach further than the sentencing court's own text authorizes, it is the sentencing court that supplies the remedy. *See United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748, 767-68 (2d Cir. 2023) (citing *United States v. Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d 147, 153 (2d Cir. 2020), for the rule that where Probation "does overstep [its] authority," the sentencing court may provide relief).

## ARGUMENT

### I. Special Condition (a) — The Computer and Internet Monitoring Program

Special Condition (a) does three things: it makes Mr. Bernecky's use of any "internet capable device" contingent on participating in CIMP; it authorizes Probation to install monitoring software on any device he "own[s] or operate[s]"; and it authorizes unannounced examination of any computer equipment he "own[s] or use[s]," subject to an instruction that monitoring "be designed to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information." Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. This Part identifies four problems with that text and with how Probation has implemented it. Part I.A addresses whether the condition reaches Mr. Bernecky's employer — a question a real employer has already had to guess the answer to. Part I.B addresses what CIMP monitoring actually consists of, since the condition itself never says. Part I.C addresses two words in the condition's device-scope language, "operated" and "used," that have not aged well since 2018. Part I.D addresses the CIMP Participant Agreement Probation requires Mr. Bernecky to follow, attached as Exhibit A, and five of its provisions that exceed what Special Condition (a) itself authorizes.

**A. No court has ever made the finding the law requires before any condition may reach Mr. Bernecky's employment, and Probation cannot supply that finding by administering the condition as though it had been made.**

Special Condition (a) does not reach an employer's own computer systems, because the only body with the power to extend it there — this Court — never undertook the analysis that extension requires, and Probation may not fill that silence on its own. Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to say so in writing, before the question costs him another job.

The conduct underlying this conviction occurred entirely on devices Mr. Bernecky acquired in his own name; no employer's equipment or account was ever involved. Certification of Jeffrey Bernecky ¶ *. While housed at the Rochester Residential Reentry Center, he obtained temporary placement with a contact-lens manufacturer. *Id.* ¶ *. After roughly ninety to one hundred twenty days — the point at which the company's own practice allows a temporary worker to be considered for direct hire — the manufacturer invited him to apply for permanent, full-time employment. *Id.* ¶ *. He disclosed his conviction and his conditions of supervision in full, including the Judgment itself once a general description did not resolve the company's questions. *Id.* ¶ *. The company's human resources office then asked whether Special Condition (a) would require it to open its own computer systems to federal monitoring simply because Mr. Bernecky was on its payroll. *Id.* ¶ *.

Special Condition (a)'s text does not answer that question. Neither its monitoring authorization nor its examination authorization mentions an employer, an occupation, or a place of business anywhere in its text. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. So Mr. Bernecky asked his probation officer directly whether the company's own account restrictions might substitute for the monitoring the condition otherwise contemplates. Certification ¶ *. The officer's first answer was that whatever the company put in place would need to satisfy an unspecified federal standard. *Id.* ¶ *. When the company, still waiting, told Mr. Bernecky not to report to work until the question was resolved, he left his probation officer a voicemail and forwarded the company's own email describing its concern. *Id.* ¶ *. After roughly a week, the officer told him that an account without internet access, paired with the company's own monitoring capability, would satisfy her, and that she would convey that understanding to the company; she has since contacted the company's human resources department directly. *Id.* ¶ *. Mr. Bernecky does not fault his probation officer for any of this. She was answering a question this Judgment gives her no tool to answer, prompted by an employer that, reasonably enough, wanted assurance before opening its own systems to anyone. But an informal, unwritten, *ad hoc* understanding — reached after two weeks without pay — is presently the only thing standing between Mr. Bernecky and his next job, and the one after that, for as long as this condition goes uncorrected.

The Second Circuit has drawn a clear line about who may impose a restriction of that magnitude. A district court "may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *United States v. Matta*, 777 F.3d 116, 122 (2d Cir. 2015), quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 762-63. Where Probation's own program terms reach past what the sentencing court's condition actually says, those terms "exceed[] the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court, and therefore may not be enforced as written." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68. A restriction that "would pose a significant burden on [a supervisee's] liberty . . . would need to be imposed by the court and justified by particularized on-the-record findings," and cannot rest on "any special condition granting Probation discretion to decide" the question for itself. *Id.* *United States v. Browder* states the same rule in blunter terms: a Probation-imposed term that "would contradict, rather than execute" the sentence actually pronounced is one "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose." 866 F.3d 504, 514 (2d Cir. 2017). And the Second Circuit has applied that same allocation of authority to the specific subject of what an employer may be told: the sentencing court "must determine" whether an employer is to be notified and "may not simply leave" that judgment to a probation officer's "unfettered discretion." *United States v. Peterson*, 248 F.3d 79, 85-86 (2d Cir. 2001) (per curiam).

For a condition that restricts a defendant's employment specifically, the Guidelines add a second, independent prerequisite. A court may impose a condition "prohibiting the defendant from engaging in a specified occupation, business, or profession, or limiting the terms on which the defendant may do so," only if it finds both "a reasonably direct relationship . . . between the defendant's occupation, business, or profession and the conduct relevant to the offense of conviction," and that the restriction "is reasonably necessary to protect the public because there is reason to believe that, absent such restriction, the defendant will continue to engage in unlawful conduct similar to that for which the defendant was convicted." U.S.S.G. § 5F1.5(a).

Nothing in the record before this Court supplies either finding, or any part of the analysis leading to one. Not the Judgment. Not any Statement of Reasons. Not any filing by either party. Mr. Bernecky states, rather than leaves unsaid, that he has not been able to obtain the June 2018 sentencing transcript, which he cannot presently afford; he will supplement the record if it is later produced. But the Judgment is a fully itemized document that sets out each restriction imposed on him, one by one, and no occupational restriction appears among them. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. Nor was there any occasion for one. Mr. Bernecky's offense had no occupational element at all: the images were on devices in his own home, and no employer, workplace, or work account appears anywhere in the conduct of conviction. Certification ¶ *. Section 5F1.5(a)'s findings are a precondition to imposing an occupational restriction, and a court that was never asked to make them, on facts that would not have supported them, did not impose one by implication. The inference is not that the condition's reach into employment is unclear. It is that no such reach was ever created.

That leaves the question of who has been deciding the matter in the meantime, and the answer is the delegation problem in its most concrete form. The scope of Special Condition (a) as applied to Mr. Bernecky's job is currently set by a probation officer's unwritten judgment about what an employer's systems must be able to do — a standard she described to Mr. Bernecky as "federal," but which appears in no order of this Court, no provision of the Judgment, and no document either Mr. Bernecky or his employer has ever been shown. Certification ¶ *. That is precisely the arrangement *Matta* forbids and *Kunz* declined to enforce: a defendant's liberty — here, his ability to hold a job at all, at the same time Standard Condition 7 requires him to work at least thirty hours a week — made contingent on an officer's discretion the sentencing court never conferred.

Candor requires addressing the decision the government will cite first. In *United States v. Brown*, the Second Circuit held that a computer-monitoring condition reaching devices used "in the course of employment" was not an occupational restriction under § 5F1.5(a), because the condition did not "target 'a specified occupation, business, or profession'" — and it held so even assuming the condition functionally required the defendant to notify future employers of his conviction. 173 F.4th 45, 50-51 (2d Cir. 2026). Mr. Bernecky does not suggest that holding is weakened by the plain-error posture in which the appeal arrived. The panel wrote in unconditional terms, resolved the definitional question itself, and expressly declined to follow a contrary summary order. *Id.* at 51 (declining to follow *United States v. Gorychka*, No. 23-6457, 2024 WL 3964287 (2d Cir. Aug. 28, 2024)). It is a holding, and it binds.

It also answers a question Mr. Bernecky does not ask. Brown asked a court to *reclassify* a condition — to hold that an existing, employment-facing monitoring clause was, in substance, an occupational restriction and therefore invalid for want of § 5F1.5 findings. Mr. Bernecky asks for the opposite. He does not seek to have Special Condition (a) reclassified, narrowed as excessive, or invalidated in any respect. He asks that it be read as what it says, and he invokes § 5F1.5 not as a ground for striking a condition but as the reason no employment-facing condition was ever created in the first place. The textual difference between the two conditions is the whole difference between the two cases: Brown's condition expressly reached devices used "in the course of employment" and carried its own employer-consent valve — monitoring "as permitted by the employer." *Id.* at 50. Special Condition (a) contains neither. *Brown* decided what to make of a condition that speaks to employment. It did not decide that a condition silent on employment reaches it anyway.

The Second Circuit's employer-notification line points the same way, and Mr. Bernecky presents it as an alternative ground rather than the lead one, because *Brown* has unsettled part of it. That line has treated even a bare requirement that a defendant tell an employer about his conviction as an occupational restriction demanding the § 5F1.5 showing. *See United States v. Jenkins*, 854 F.3d 181, 194-95 (2d Cir. 2017); *United States v. Love*, No. 24-1706-cr, 2025 WL 1077176 (2d Cir. Apr. 8, 2025); *United States v. Ruff*, No. 18-3247-cr (2d Cir. Nov. 14, 2019) (vacating outright). To whatever extent that line survives *Brown*'s narrowing of *Gorychka*, it favors Mr. Bernecky *a fortiori*: Special Condition (a) contains no notification requirement at all, Mr. Bernecky volunteered his disclosure without being asked, and the condition has nonetheless produced something considerably beyond notification — a live dispute with a real employer about that employer's own systems.

The Tenth Circuit has confronted materially the same confusion about materially the same kind of condition, and its answer is instructive precisely because it did not go the way the government there wanted. In *United States v. Mike*, the government argued that computer-monitoring conditions "cannot be read fairly" to reach an employer's computers. The panel disagreed, holding that a probation officer "could reasonably believe that the computer monitoring conditions apply not only to computers that Mike owns, but also to those owned by others, including Mike's employer," and remanded for the district court to clarify the conditions' scope — directing that if the court did intend that reach, "it must make the necessary findings." 632 F.3d 686, 694-95 (10th Cir. 2011). On a separate condition requiring the defendant to notify potential employers of his convictions, the same panel vacated outright, agreeing that such notification "constitutes an occupational restriction and the court did not make the findings required by law." *Id.* at 699-700. *Mike* therefore confirms two things at once, and Mr. Bernecky accepts both: an open-ended monitoring condition can be read broadly by the officer administering it, and if a court wants that breadth, the court must say so and make findings. That is the relief requested here — a clarification from this Court, rather than a guess from anyone else.

One further consideration favors resolving this now rather than later. Mr. Bernecky's injury is his own and needs no borrowing: two weeks of lost wages, a job held open on nothing more durable than an officer's verbal assurance, and a repeat of the same problem waiting at every subsequent employer. But the burden also falls on a party with no way to be heard. An employer with no connection to this criminal case is left to guess whether hiring Mr. Bernecky opens its own computer systems to federal monitoring, and it will never realistically appear here to protect its own interest in staying out of someone else's supervision. Courts have long accounted for exactly that kind of practical barrier. *See Powers v. Ohio*, 499 U.S. 400, 410-15 (1991); *Singleton v. Wulff*, 428 U.S. 106, 117 (1976) (permitting physicians to assert patients' rights where a "genuine obstacle" separated the right-holder from the courthouse); *cf. Kowalski v. Tesmer*, 543 U.S. 125, 130-31 (2004) (no standing absent a comparable obstacle).

**Requested relief as to Part I.A:** an order amending Special Condition (a) to add clarifying language confirming that, consistent with the absence of any U.S.S.G. § 5F1.5 finding in this record, the condition does not reach Mr. Bernecky's employment and imposes no obligation on any employer — for example, "with the exception of systems necessary for employment."

**B. Special Condition (a) never says what monitoring actually consists of, and the most invasive available method cannot honor the privilege limit the condition itself imposes.**

Special Condition (a) promises that any monitoring "shall be designed to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information or any private material that is not illegal or reasonably likely to lead to illegal material." Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. A monitoring method that captures every character Mr. Bernecky types has nothing left to avoid. Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to specify a method that can actually keep the condition's own promise.

Mr. Bernecky served an earlier term of supervision under a materially similar computer-monitoring condition, and Probation's administration of that condition resulted in continuous keystroke logging — capture of everything he typed on every covered device. Certification of Jeffrey Bernecky ¶ *. Special Condition (a) itself never specifies how CIMP monitoring is carried out; left unspecified, the gap is filled by whatever Probation's standard practice happens to be, and nothing distinguishes Mr. Bernecky's current supervision from that history absent a different order from this Court. *Id.* ¶ *.

The choice among monitoring methods is not a detail of administration. It is a choice between points on a spectrum the Second Circuit has already mapped, and the two ends are not close. Comparing methods in *United States v. Lifshitz*, the court observed that "[c]onstant inspection of the documents that Lifshitz creates on his computer might be more like searching his diary or inspecting his closets than it is like the highly targeted diagnosis accomplished by drug testing," whereas "software that alerted a probation officer only when Lifshitz was engaging in impermissible communications over e-mail or the Internet would bear much greater resemblance to screening a probationer's urine for particular drugs — as opposed to investigating a sample to ascertain all medical conditions from which the individual suffered." 369 F.3d 173, 192 (2d Cir. 2004). The same opinion identified, as a narrower alternative on remand, "monitoring software that searches for particular suspect words and phrases rather than recording all varieties of computer-related activity." *Id.* at 193 n.11. Content-blind capture of everything typed is the far end of that spectrum, and § 3583(d)(2) permits only the deprivation of liberty that is "reasonably necessary."

Privilege is the sharpest reason the choice matters. A method that records every keystroke cannot distinguish an ordinary message from the kinds of communication the law has long protected as confidential: a conversation with an attorney, "the oldest of the privileges for confidential communications known to the common law," *Upjohn Co. v. United States*, 449 U.S. 383, 389 (1981); a session with a psychotherapist, protected under Federal Rule of Evidence 501 because candid treatment depends on confidentiality, *Jaffee v. Redmond*, 518 U.S. 1, 10-15 (1996); a confession made to clergy in a spiritual capacity, *In re Grand Jury Investigation*, 918 F.2d 374, 384 (3d Cir. 1990); and a private communication between spouses, *Trammel v. United States*, 445 U.S. 40, 51 (1980); *Blau v. United States*, 340 U.S. 332 (1951). Each is captured along with everything else, before anyone has any opportunity to sort privileged material out. That matters here because the Second Circuit has already held this Judgment's privilege language to be a real, enforceable constraint on Probation and not mere hortatory text: the clause "constrains the supervisors in their surveillance . . . by requiring them not to abuse their power and access." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 766-67. Mr. Bernecky's argument is not that the Constitution compels a privilege carve-out — no decision this motion has located so holds, and he does not claim otherwise. It is narrower and entirely internal to his own Judgment: a method that captures everything cannot be a method "designed to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information," because the reading has already happened by the time the design would operate.

Formal privilege is only part of what indiscriminate capture reaches. A diary, a draft of a memoir, research into a stock purchase, or an unsent complaint about a probation officer are not privileged in the technical sense, and none of them is the target of any supervisory interest CIMP exists to serve. The Supreme Court has described what is at stake at the scale a modern device makes possible: a phone or computer today holds "the sum of an individual's private life," a volume of personal material that, before such devices existed, "most people cannot lug around" at all. *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373, 393-97 (2014). And *Kunz* has recognized the same point in this exact context — internet-connected devices are now of "nearly essential" importance to modern life, so a monitoring choice made once at the outset governs a very large share of everything a supervisee does for the next decade. 68 F.4th at 773 (quoting *United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d 88, 96 (2d Cir. 2019)).

Mr. Bernecky's own offense does not call for monitoring at that scale. He was convicted of possessing images. His conduct involved no chat, no solicitation, no grooming, and no text-based communication of any kind. Certification ¶ *. For that same category of offense, the Second Circuit has already identified a narrower tool as adequate: "a more focused restriction, limited to pornography sites and images, can be enforced by unannounced inspections of [a probationer's] premises and examination of material stored on his hard drive or removable disks." *United States v. Sofsky*, 287 F.3d 122, 127 (2d Cir. 2002), quoted in *Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d at 189. *Sofsky* addressed a total access ban rather than a monitoring regime, and Mr. Bernecky presents the point as what it is — the Second Circuit's own view of what a proportionate tool looks like for an image-based offense, applied here by analogy rather than by direct holding.

None of this means Mr. Bernecky should go unmonitored. This is his second child-pornography conviction, as the Statement of Facts above states plainly, and he agrees that his computer use warrants real supervision. The relief he seeks is supervision pointed at the actual risk. This District's CIMP vendor, IPPC Technologies, already markets a periodic, point-in-time review tool called Express Scan, which it describes as scanning a Windows or Mac computer for system information, installed programs, multimedia, recycle-bin contents, keyword hits, and browser history and searches, and which it positions expressly as a triage tool rather than continuous surveillance. Mr. Bernecky offers to submit to it on any computer he owns, configured to flag image content and search terms relevant to child pornography. Express Scan is a computer product and would not cover his smart phone; for that device, and for any device it cannot reach, he asks only that the method chosen be one that does not capture the content of everything he writes — domain-level review, file-type filtering, image-hash scanning, or the keyword-triggered approach *Lifshitz* itself identified.

**Requested relief as to Part I.B:** an order specifying that Special Condition (a)'s monitoring method shall be IPPC's Express Scan, or an equivalent periodic, non-continuous tool, configured to flag image content and search terms relevant to child pornography, in place of continuous keystroke capture; and, as to any device such a tool cannot cover, any method that does not indiscriminately capture the content of Mr. Bernecky's writing — including domain review, file-type filtering, image-hash scanning, or keyword-triggered flagging.

**C. "Operated" and "used" no longer supply a workable boundary on whose devices Special Condition (a) reaches, and should give way to the ownership standard the condition already uses elsewhere.**

Special Condition (a) ties its two most intrusive authorizations to verbs other than ownership: Probation may "surveil all activity on computer(s) or connected device(s) owned *or operated* by the defendant," and may conduct unannounced examinations of "any computer equipment owned *or used* by the defendant." Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B (emphasis added). In 2018, "operated" and "used" may have read as harmless synonyms for "owned." Ordinary life has since moved from a world in which a person sat down at a computer he owned and connected it to the internet, to one in which most of what a person interacts with over the course of an ordinary day is itself a networked computer. Federal law's own definition shows how far the shift reaches: a "computer" is "an electronic, magnetic, optical, electrochemical, or other high speed data processing device performing logical, arithmetic, or storage functions." 18 U.S.C. § 1030(e)(1). Untethered from ownership, "operated" and "used" sweep in a public library terminal, a store's self-checkout kiosk, a bank's ATM, and a car's onboard navigation system — none of which the condition's drafters could plausibly have meant to place under CIMP surveillance, and the last of which is, in ordinary English, the paradigmatic thing a person "operates" at all.

*Kunz* confirms that this Circuit reads CIMP boilerplate sensibly rather than literally when a literal reading would sweep too far. Faced with an encryption clause that, read literally, would have required a supervisee to seek permission before visiting any website beginning with "https," the panel said it "would be absurd to interpret Probation as purporting to require" that, and construed the clause narrowly instead of striking it. 68 F.4th at 764-65. But the facts there were milder than these in one decisive respect: no one in *Kunz* had actually acted on the expansive reading, so the court needed only say that nobody would. Here, someone did. An employer was worried enough about what "operated" and "used" might require of its own systems to hold Mr. Bernecky's job for two weeks, Certification ¶ *, and the question was resolved not by any settled construction of the text but by an improvised, unwritten understanding, *id.* An interpretive canon that depends on everyone recognizing the same obvious meaning cannot resolve a dispute that has already proven the meaning is not obvious.

The government will point out that this Circuit has turned aside a challenge built on the reach of a monitoring condition to ordinary networked appliances. It has. *See United States v. Leone*, No. 19-1670, 2020 WL 2610536 (2d Cir. May 20, 2020) (summary order) (rejecting a vagueness challenge premised on "[m]icrowaves and modern refrigerators," citing *United States v. Balon*, 384 F.3d 38, 43 (2d Cir. 2004)). Mr. Bernecky is not making that challenge. He does not ask the Court to hold Special Condition (a) unconstitutionally vague, and no part of the relief he seeks depends on that label. He asks this Court to exercise its own modification authority under § 3583(e)(2), at the moment *Balon* itself identified as the right one. And *Balon* — the case *Leone* relies on — is the decision that remanded a computer-monitoring condition precisely so it could be reconsidered under § 3583(e)(2) "near the time of" the defendant's release, because the state of the technology could not be predicted at sentencing. 384 F.3d at 46-47. That authority does not stand in the way of this request. It points toward it.

The boundary that still does useful work is not connectivity but capability. A modern smart television illustrates the difference: it connects to the internet to deliver services such as Netflix, often with no web browser at all, and no one contends that a device built only to stream commercial video presents the risk CIMP exists to manage. Other courts have looked for a functional line of the same kind. *See United States v. Ullman*, No. 14-1414, 2015 WL 3559221 (10th Cir. June 8, 2015) (treating "devices without Internet access, such as gaming systems," as outside a comparable condition's reach). That particular line no longer does much work, since gaming systems, appliances, and cars are now routinely connected; the line that does is the capacity to access, search for, or display child pornography.

Rather than ask the Court to police that boundary case by case, Mr. Bernecky proposes striking "or operated" and "or used," leaving "owned by the defendant" as the operative standard — the word the condition already uses without difficulty. In exchange, he proposes a targeted provision addressing the gap an ownership-only rule would otherwise leave. A prohibition keyed to capability alone would still reach a library terminal, a self-checkout kiosk, or the PACER terminal in this courthouse, each technically capable of displaying an illegal image if someone misused it. The line that separates a real circumvention risk from ordinary public infrastructure is personal, private possession, not momentary public use: "With the exception of employer devices necessary for work performance, the defendant shall not personally and privately possess any smart cell phone, laptop, desktop computer, tablet, or other device capable of connecting to the internet and displaying images of child pornography." That reaches what "operated" and "used" were likely meant to reach — a borrowed device standing in for one Mr. Bernecky does not own — without reaching a library computer, an ATM, or a courthouse terminal.

**Requested relief as to Part I.C:** an order striking "or operated" and "or used" from Special Condition (a)'s device-scope clauses, leaving "owned by the defendant" as the operative standard, together with the provision quoted immediately above.

**D. The CIMP Participant Agreement governs Mr. Bernecky's day-to-day computer use, and five of its provisions exceed what Special Condition (a) authorizes.**

At the start of his supervision, Mr. Bernecky was presented with this District's Computer Monitoring Program Participant Agreement, attached as Exhibit A. He is complying with it in full. He nonetheless objects to several of its terms as applied to him, and a few issues arising from their application require the Court's attention.

Mr. Bernecky owns two devices that qualify as "internet capable" under Exhibit A's own terms: a Roku television and an Android smart cell phone. Ex. A, Item 2; Certification of Jeffrey Bernecky ¶ *. He purchased the television before his supervision began and reported it to Probation at the outset, having understood browser-less smart televisions to fall outside CIMP's device-authorization requirement. Certification ¶ *. He told his probation officer that the Roku does not support general web browsing; Probation imposed a parental lock on it regardless, preventing him from installing programs or applications without separate clearance. *Id.* ¶ *. That lock does not reach the one path by which outside content actually arrives on the television: the Roku's casting function lets another device on the same network send its own content to it whether or not the Roku itself is locked. *Id.* ¶ *. As for the phone, Mr. Bernecky uses an Android smart cell phone with service through US Mobile. *Id.* ¶ *. Consistent with Exhibit A's instructions, he notified his probation officer of the phone and contacted the CIMP vendor, IPPC Technologies, to arrange installation of the required monitoring software. *Id.* ¶ *. That phone — his own personal device, on his own carrier plan — now displays on its lock screen the message "This device belongs to your organization." *Id.* ¶ *. That is not custom text a vendor wrote to assert a property claim. It is text the Android operating system displays automatically whenever an application has been granted Device Owner status, the highest level of control Android permits any program to have over a device, and the level Google's own documentation reserves for equipment a company owns and issues to its employees. Google, *Full Device Management*, Android Enterprise Help, https://support.google.com/work/android/answer/9562029.

What actually governs Mr. Bernecky's daily computer and internet use, then, is not Special Condition (a)'s text standing alone. It is Exhibit A — a twenty-one-item form used across this District's CIMP caseload, the same form the Second Circuit construed on appeal from this District in *Kunz*. This Part is necessarily an as-applied challenge: Exhibit A did not exist and was never placed before Judge Larimer at the June 12, 2018 sentencing. Judgment (Dkt. 28). It is a document Probation drafted and required Mr. Bernecky to sign at intake eight years later, and no part of it appears anywhere in the record the sentencing court reviewed.

*Kunz* supplies the framework. Where a CIMP-form provision is ambiguous, or would demand something absurd read with total literalism, the court construes it narrowly rather than striking it. 68 F.4th at 762-66. But where a provision cannot be reconciled with the special condition's own text, it "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court," and Probation "may not enforce" it. *Id.* at 767-68. *Browder* states the same rule: a provision that "would contradict, rather than execute" the sentence pronounced is one "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose." 866 F.3d at 514. Five provisions call for attention under that framework.

*Item 21 — seizure authority.* Item 21 requires Mr. Bernecky to "permit the seizure and removal of hardware and data storage media for further analysis by law enforcement or the probation officer based upon reasonable suspicion that a violation of a condition of supervision or unlawful conduct by any person has or is about to occur." Ex. A, Item 21. Special Condition (a) authorizes something narrower in three respects. As to *who* may act, it authorizes only "the U.S. Probation Office" to install monitoring applications and examine devices; Special Condition (f), the general search condition, names no actor but reaches only Mr. Bernecky's own property. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. Item 21 adds "law enforcement," empowered to seize hardware independent of Probation. Congress made a broader model available: 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(23) authorizes search of a defendant's "computer, other electronic communication or data storage devices or media" "by any law enforcement or probation officer." The sentencing court did not select it. As to *whose conduct* triggers seizure, Special Condition (a) ties its examination trigger to "impermissible/suspicious activity" on equipment "owned or used by the defendant." Item 21 substitutes "unlawful conduct by any person," so that a roommate's or visitor's suspected and unrelated crime becomes grounds to seize Mr. Bernecky's hardware. Every model Congress or the Commission has made available ties the trigger to the defendant. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(23) ("the person"); U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(7) ("unlawful conduct by the defendant"), quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 758-59. As to *timing*, Special Condition (a) speaks of activity "occurring," and Special Condition (f) requires "reasonable suspicion," which presupposes something has already left a trace. Item 21 adds seizure where conduct "is about to occur," converting an after-the-fact tool into a predictive one.

*Item 3 — device authorization.* Item 2 requires Mr. Bernecky to notify Probation in advance of any device he owns or can access. Ex. A, Item 2. Special Condition (a) requires exactly that, and Mr. Bernecky has no quarrel with it: notice gives Probation complete visibility without gating what he may own. Item 3 does something different. It forbids him to "use, own, or access any device which allows internet access other than what I have been authorized to use by the Probation Office," listing "internet appliances, mobile telephones, tablets, electronic games, gaming consoles, blu ray players, and SMART TV's." Ex. A, Item 3. It supplies no standard for what earns authorization and no limit on what Probation may withhold it for, and it sorts devices by product label rather than by anything they actually do. A tablet ships with a general-purpose browser; a Blu-ray player reads an optical disc; and a Roku, by the manufacturer's own account, "provide[s] access to stream video and music from the internet" but "does not provide the ability to browse the internet on your television." Roku, *Does My Roku Streaming Device Have an Internet Web Browser?*, https://support.roku.com/article/can-i-browse-the-internet.

*Kunz* has already struck down functionally the same defect in this identical program. It confronted a CIMP clause under which Probation could have limited the defendant to a single internet-capable device, and held that the clause "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court" because the sentencing court's own text said nothing supporting a Probation-controlled device restriction. 68 F.4th at 767-68. Such a restriction, the panel explained, "would pose a significant burden on [a supervisee's] liberty, and therefore would need to be imposed by the court and justified by particularized on-the-record findings," and would require "an express, case-specific rationale." *Id.* Item 3 is the same vice in a broader form: rather than capping Mr. Bernecky at one device, it lets Probation decide, device by device and by no stated standard, whether he may have any device at all. No case-specific rationale for that appears anywhere in this record.

Two facts show what the undisclosed discretion looks like in practice. Special Condition (a) authorizes technology that can "surveil . . . activity" — technology that watches and reports. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. Device Owner status, now running on Mr. Bernecky's phone, does more: it can remotely erase the device, block its own removal, and dictate what other software may be installed at all. Surveillance is observation; Device Owner status is control. And the Roku shows the same discretion from the other direction — a device locked down for a risk it does not present, by a lock that does not even reach the casting function through which outside content actually arrives.

Mr. Bernecky does not ask the Court to leave Probation without a tool to identify devices that present a genuine monitoring risk. He asks that pre-authorization attach only to a device that has (i) general-purpose web-browsing capability, or (ii) an application-installation mechanism through which an ordinary user could add web-browsing, anonymization, or peer-to-peer file-sharing capability, and that (iii) is compatible with the CIMP monitoring software Probation actually uses. Whatever device-management privilege the vendor uses to enroll an authorized device should likewise be no broader than surveilling that device's activity requires — not the organization-ownership tier Android reserves for hardware a company owns.

In exchange, Mr. Bernecky offers Probation something the Judgment does not currently give it. Special Condition (a) conditions physical examination on a trigger — "[a]s triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity" — so Probation's authority to inspect depends on suspicion having already arisen. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. Mr. Bernecky consents to physical inspection of any of his personal devices, on request, at any time, without any requirement of prior suspicion. That consent necessarily extends only to devices within his own authority to consent to, and not to a device owned or jointly controlled by a third party who has not consented. *See United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164, 171 (1974). The parsimony clause calls for that exchange rather than a categorical rule: § 3583(d)(2) permits "no greater deprivation of liberty than is reasonably necessary," and *Kunz* has already recognized how much of ordinary life now depends on devices of this kind. 68 F.4th at 773.

*Items 9 and 17 — encryption.* Item 9 provides that Mr. Bernecky "will not use any encrypted email accounts"; Item 17 that he "shall not use any type of encryption unless pre-approved by my probation officer." Ex. A, Items 9, 17. This is not new territory. *Kunz* construed its own CIMP paragraph 18 — word for word the same text as Item 17 — and, faced with the argument that a literal reading would require pre-approval before visiting any "https" website, agreed a literal reading would be absurd but did not strike the clause. It construed the restriction narrowly and affirmed it as construed: "so long as no encryption is initiated by or at the behest of [the supervisee], and so long as any encryption initiated by external forces does not meaningfully impair Probation's ability to monitor [his] behavior . . . we construe the encryption restrictions to allow the reasonable use of services and resources that happen to employ encryption in some way." 68 F.4th at 764. The panel's textual hook was the personal verb — what the supervisee himself will or will not actively "use" — as against a process "entirely outside of his control." *Id.* at 762-64. Item 9 uses that identical verb, and the same construction should govern it: Mr. Bernecky may not affirmatively choose an end-to-end-encrypted service to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, but he may hold an ordinary Gmail or Outlook account that the provider itself encrypts in transit and at rest, invisibly to him, by default. Candor requires noting that *Kunz*'s narrowing discussion addressed paragraphs 7, 16, and 18, not its paragraph 10 counterpart to Item 9; the extension is by direct analogy, not an on-point holding, and Mr. Bernecky presents it as exactly that. Item 9 also has a gap Item 17 does not: Item 17 contains its own escape valve, "unless pre-approved by my probation officer," and Item 9 has none, leaving no route to approval even for a legitimate use — an employer's IT department requiring signed correspondence, or an encrypted portal used by counsel or by this Court.

*Item 16 — security software.* Item 16 forbids Mr. Bernecky to "purchase, download, possess and/or install any anti-virus, anti-spyware, firewall, or internet security application without advanced authorization." Ex. A, Item 16. *Kunz*'s narrowing discussion does not reach the corresponding clause, so Item 16 stands on its own. Read literally, it forbids something Mr. Bernecky cannot avoid: every modern operating system ships with antivirus and firewall software already installed and active, and a user does not "purchase, download, possess and/or install" what the manufacturer installed before the device reached him. Taken at its word, Item 16 would require him to seek advance authorization merely to leave his own operating system's factory defaults running, or else disable them — leaving the very device Probation monitors more vulnerable to compromise. Nothing in Special Condition (a) mentions security software at all. Meanwhile, another arm of the same federal government instructs every American to do what Item 16 gates: the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency advises that "[a] reputable antivirus software application is an important protective measure against known malicious threats" and "recommends that all computers and mobile devices on your home network run antivirus software," alongside its instruction to "[i]nstall a network firewall." CISA, *Home Network Security*, https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/home-network-security. Probation's legitimate interest is in knowing what security software runs on a monitored device. Notice satisfies that interest; prior authorization is not required to serve it.

*Item 5 — private browsing.* Item 5 provides: "While using my monitored device, I shall not use any form of 'in-private' browsing at any time." Ex. A, Item 5. Special Condition (a) authorizes Probation "to install any application as necessary to surveil all activity on computer(s) or connected device(s)" — so the monitoring Probation deploys is premised on capturing activity regardless of browser mode. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. If that premise holds, Item 5 adds nothing and is surplusage. If it does not, that is a fact about the adequacy of the method Probation chose, addressed in Part I.B, not a basis for a separate categorical ban the Judgment never mentions. Either way, Item 5 does independent work the Judgment never authorized. *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 514. A narrower clarification is also warranted. Mr. Bernecky uses DuckDuckGo for ordinary browsing. Certification ¶ *. DuckDuckGo describes its protections as blocking third-party trackers — its stated purpose is to "stop most 3rd-party trackers from loading in the first place" — not as hiding anything from software running locally on the device, and its tracker-blocking is always on rather than a mode a user switches into. DuckDuckGo, *Web Tracking Protections*, https://duckduckgo.com/duckduckgo-help-pages/privacy/web-tracking-protections. Google's own documentation makes the contrast concrete: even a genuine Incognito session does not defeat monitoring by whoever controls the device or network, since "organizations that manage your network, like your school, employer, or internet service provider, may be able to observe your activity in Incognito." Google, *Browse in Incognito Mode*, Chrome Help, https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/95464.

**Requested relief as to Part I.D:** an order (1) striking from Item 21 the words "by law enforcement," "or unlawful conduct by any person," and "or is about to occur"; (2) replacing Item 3's device-authorization gate with one requiring Probation's advance authorization only for a device that has general-purpose web-browsing capability, or an application-installation mechanism through which an ordinary user could add web-browsing, anonymization, or peer-to-peer file-sharing capability, and that is compatible with the CIMP monitoring software Probation uses — coupled with Mr. Bernecky's consent to physical inspection, without prior suspicion, of any device within his own authority to consent to, and a limit confining any device-management privilege used to enroll an authorized device to no more than surveilling that device's activity requires; (3) construing Items 9 and 17 to reach only encryption Mr. Bernecky himself affirmatively initiates to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, and adding to Item 9 the pre-approval mechanism Item 17 already contains; (4) narrowing Item 16 to require notice to Probation, not prior authorization, before installing or running any antivirus, anti-spyware, firewall, or other internet security software, whether factory-installed or added; and (5) confirming that Item 5 reaches only browser-native private or incognito session modes, not ordinary use of a privacy-respecting search engine that does not defeat device-level monitoring.


### II. Special Conditions (b) and (h) — Treatment Provider Rules

Special Condition (b) requires Mr. Bernecky to "follow the rules and regulations" of a sex-offense-specific treatment program; Special Condition (h) places him in a mental health treatment program supervised the same way. The problem is not that a provider's rule might exceed what this Court ordered — *Browder* already establishes that it cannot. The problem is what stands between Mr. Bernecky and a violation report the moment he says so.

*Browder* shows exactly what that costs. Browder's treatment provider required him to sign an agreement barring contact with *any* minor, including his own children, even though his sentence specifically excluded his own children from that restriction. He objected in writing and did not sign. His probation officer sought a violation anyway — without first confirming she had read the written objection — and Browder was detained and found by the district court to have violated his conditions. 866 F.3d at 507-08, 513-14. It took a full appeal to undo that. The Second Circuit held that the provider's rule "would contradict, rather than execute," Browder's actual sentence, and that "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose it," *id.* at 514, and confirmed what should have been true from the start: Browder "was therefore well within his rights to object to that term of the agreement," *id.* at 513. The court was direct about what had gone wrong: "the key question is not whether permission, if sought, would be granted; it is whether Browder can be required, by the Probation Office or its designee, to ask in the first place." *Id.* at 514.

Rule 32.1's drafters anticipated this and answered it the same way Mr. Bernecky now asks this Court to. A probationer "should be able to obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it," precisely because "in cases of neglect, overwork, or simply unreasonableness on the part of the probation officer, the probationer should have recourse to the sentencing court when a condition needs clarification or modification." Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's note to subdivision (b) (1979). Browder is what happens when that recourse exists in theory but not in the procedure actually available: he did precisely what the Advisory Committee said a probationer should be able to do, and it still took an appeal, rather than a request to his own sentencing court, to establish that he had been right all along.

This Part asks for a narrow procedure, not supervision of Probation's general practice. Mr. Bernecky requests the right to object in writing to his probation officer to any rule imposed by a provider under Special Condition (b) or (h), or any application of such a rule, that he believes conditions his liberty beyond what this Court's Judgment authorizes, and — if the officer overrules that objection — the right to seek this Court's clarification before any violation report is filed on that basis. This does not ask the Court to compel a provider to keep treating a defendant who will not accept its rules; a provider remains free to terminate. It asks only that a timely written objection made under this procedure keep that termination from being treated, by itself, as a violation of supervision, with the Probation Office making other arrangements for treatment instead. The choice to terminate would be the provider's own, made in response to a rule this Court never authorized, and it cannot be converted into Mr. Bernecky's failure to comply with anything this Court actually ordered. Nothing in *Browder*'s reasoning turns on which kind of provider is involved, and Special Condition (h) uses the same structure as Special Condition (b) — the officer "supervise[s] the details," and Mr. Bernecky "is not to leave such treatment until completion or as ordered by the Court" — so the same procedure should govern both.

**Requested relief as to Part II:** an order providing that, where Mr. Bernecky believes a rule imposed by a provider under Special Condition (b) or (h), or a provider's application of such a rule, conditions his liberty beyond what this Court's Judgment authorizes, he may object in writing to his probation officer and may seek this Court's clarification if the objection is overruled; that no violation report may be filed, and no violation found, based solely on a provider's termination of Mr. Bernecky following a timely written objection made under this procedure; and that in that circumstance the Probation Office must make other arrangements for his treatment rather than treat the resulting absence of treatment as a violation.


### III. Special Condition (c) — Contact with Minors and Loitering

Special Condition (c) bars "deliberate contact with any child under 18 years of age, excluding his biological or adopted children, unless approved by the probation officer or by the Court." Mr. Bernecky has no biological or adopted children, so the only category of contact the condition permits without advance approval has never existed for him and never will. Every contact with a child — including with his own nieces, nephews, or any other minor relative — requires case-by-case advance permission, for the next ten years, before it happens. Three problems follow.

**A. Requiring advance approval before any contact with a minor family member will functionally exclude Mr. Bernecky from ordinary family life.**

Mr. Bernecky's sister had children while he was incarcerated. Certification ¶ *. Under this condition, seeing them — at his mother's house, at a holiday, at any gathering where they might be present — requires approval in advance, every time. And where the visit is unannounced, his sister arriving at their mother's house with her children while he happens to be there, there is no opportunity to seek approval at all; the condition supplies no mechanism for that scenario except placing him in technical violation the moment he does not immediately leave. No allegation has ever surfaced that Mr. Bernecky is a danger to a minor family member.

The Second Circuit has recognized how untenable that structure is. Confronting a nearly identical restriction, it observed that the condition "would apply with full force to all routine family interaction — for example, Thanksgiving dinners or seders or christenings." *United States v. Jenkins*, 854 F.3d 181, 194 (2d Cir. 2017). Nor does an approval mechanism cure the problem merely by existing. The Tenth Circuit rejected that argument when the government made it against a materially identical restriction: "While these provisions may allow Defendant to have some contact with his son, there is still an impermissible infringement of Defendant's ability to freely associate with his family." *United States v. Lonjose*, 663 F.3d 1292, 1303 (10th Cir. 2011). *Lonjose* arose under the same statute invoked here, and the court remanded for the condition to be narrowed so that it "reasonably relates to Defendant's offense." *Id.*

The interest at stake is constitutionally protected and is not confined to a defendant's own children. *See Moore v. City of East Cleveland*, 431 U.S. 494, 499, 503 (1977) (plurality opinion) (striking an occupancy ordinance that assumed, as this condition does, that a person's family is limited to his own children). Mr. Bernecky's parents and his sister have their own interest in their relationships with their grandchildren and children — "perhaps the oldest of the fundamental liberty interests recognized by this Court," *Troxel v. Granville*, 530 U.S. 57, 65 (2000) (plurality opinion) — and a condition that makes it impossible for him to be present at an ordinary family gathering without advance clearance burdens their interest as surely as his own. The closest case makes the point about grandchildren specifically: in *United States v. Weiskopf*, arising from this same District, the Second Circuit remanded a condition barring deliberate contact with "any child under 18 years of age, excluding his biological or adopted children, unless approved by the probation officer or by the Court" — materially the same language — as applied to the defendant's grandchildren, because no individualized finding had been made as to them and the defendant had not been alleged to pose a danger to them. 841 F. App'x 304 (2d Cir. 2021) (summary order). That is Mr. Bernecky's situation, one generation removed.

The governing standard confirms the requirement. Where a condition implicates a fundamental liberty interest, the sentencing court must "carefully examine" it and find both that it involves no greater deprivation of liberty than reasonably necessary and that it is narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest. *United States v. Myers*, 426 F.3d 117, 124-26 (2d Cir. 2005). A district court's failure to explain why a no-contact-with-minors condition is necessary is error "unless obvious from the record." *United States v. Bleau*, 930 F.3d 35, 43 (2d Cir. 2019). No such assessment addressing family contact has ever been made in this case, at sentencing or since.

**Requested relief as to Part III.A:** an order adding "or minor family members" to Special Condition (c)'s existing carve-out, so that contact with Mr. Bernecky's minor family members does not require case-by-case advance approval.

**B. The condition should make clear that it does not reach incidental contact in ordinary public life.**

The problem is not that "deliberate contact" is genuinely ambiguous to an appellate court. It likely is not: on the second appeal in *Bleau*, the Second Circuit held that a condition barring "direct contact" with minors without pre-approval was "not unduly restrictive" in part because the defendant "is permitted unapproved incidental contact with minors during daily activities in public places," distinguishing *Jenkins*, where no such carve-out existed. *United States v. Bleau*, No. 19-3931-cr (2d Cir. Feb. 4, 2021) (summary order). Other circuits read comparable language the same way. *See United States v. DaSilva*, 844 F.3d 8, 14 (1st Cir. 2016) ("associational restrictions are usually read to exclude incidental encounters").

But it is not the Second Circuit that will read this condition for the next ten years — it is Probation, and Mr. Bernecky already has direct evidence of how these conditions get read once they apply to him. As Part I describes, Probation gave two different, shifting answers about how far Special Condition (a) reaches an employer's systems, neither written down and both broader than the text requires. And as Part III.C describes, Mr. Bernecky was separately told that this very condition would bar him from working anywhere that hires or serves anyone under eighteen. The word itself supplies no protection: "deliberate" means "characterized by awareness of the consequences," a sense that requires no bad purpose at all. *Deliberate*, Merriam-Webster.com Dictionary, https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/deliberate. Taken at that meaning, asking a teenage cashier to ring up a sandwich, telling a teenage bagger to put the bread on top, or calling out to a child about to step into traffic are each "deliberate" contact — and each is exactly what no one could believe this condition was written to prohibit.

**Requested relief as to Part III.B:** an order clarifying that "deliberate contact" does not include incidental, unplanned encounters with minors in the ordinary course of public life.

**C. The condition imposes no restriction on Mr. Bernecky's choice of occupation, and should say so.**

For the reasons set out in Part I.A, no condition in this Judgment can be read to restrict Mr. Bernecky's employment: the record contains no U.S.S.G. § 5F1.5 finding, and no such restriction was ever imposed. But because Mr. Bernecky was told, in the course of the same job search described in Part I, that this condition would preclude him from working anywhere that hires or serves anyone under eighteen, Certification ¶ *, he asks that the point be settled now rather than after it costs him another job. He has never been alleged to have used employment as a means of access to a minor, or as a catalyst for any offense; his conduct occurred entirely on devices in his own home.

There is also a forward-looking problem that has nothing to do with anything Mr. Bernecky would do. At any moment, his employer could lawfully hire a sixteen- or seventeen-year-old for any position — federal and New York law both permit it, outside a defined list of hazardous occupations. 29 C.F.R. pt. 570, subpt. E; N.Y. Lab. Law § 132. Under the reading Mr. Bernecky was given, that single lawful hiring decision — made by someone else, with no notice to him and no way for him to prevent it — would place him in jeopardy of violating his supervision through no act of his own, at the very moment Standard Condition 7 requires him to keep working.

**Requested relief as to Part III.C:** a clarification that Special Condition (c) does not apply to Mr. Bernecky's employment in any respect.


### IV. Special Conditions (d) and (i) — Financial Monitoring and Disclosure

Special Condition (d) requires Mr. Bernecky to give Probation access to "any requested personal and/or business financial information" in order to monitor his "compliance with not buying or subscribing to online services that provide child pornography." Special Condition (i) requires the same open-ended access, tied instead to "the collection of any unpaid fine or restitution." Each gives Probation more than its own stated purpose can account for.

No one has ever alleged that Mr. Bernecky purchased child pornography; his conviction is for possession, and nothing in the record suggests his access involved payment of any kind. Even taking the condition's stated purpose at face value, the records capable of showing such a payment are a specific and identifiable set — credit and debit card statements, PayPal or comparable electronic transaction records, and cryptocurrency wallet or exchange records — not a brokerage statement, a retirement account, or any other record incapable of showing a payment to a child-pornography vendor. Nor does detecting such a payment require anything beyond the vendor's name; the stated purpose is served in full once Mr. Bernecky discloses to whom a payment was made, without the amount, the account number, or any other detail.

*United States v. Jenkins* does not map onto this problem exactly, but its logic governs. There the Second Circuit struck a condition forbidding a child-pornography defendant from incurring any credit card charge or opening any line of credit without prior approval, holding that "[n]othing in the record suggests these restrictions on Jenkins's use of credit cards are 'reasonably necessary' . . . to protect the public or to deter [him] from continuing to engage in the conduct for which he was convicted — possession of child pornography." 854 F.3d at 194. The court did not mince words: "Why Jenkins should be prohibited from buying a drink on an airplane or taking an Uber ride or making a purchase on Amazon unless the transaction is pre-approved by a probation officer cannot be divined from the record we are reviewing." *Id.* Special Condition (d) requires disclosure rather than permission, and Mr. Bernecky does not treat the two as equivalent. But *Jenkins*'s point survives that distinction: a condition responding to a possession offense with no financial element cannot reach further than that offense gives Probation reason to look.[^1]

Special Condition (i) presents a different problem. It ties Probation's access to Mr. Bernecky's finances, and its control over his assets, to "the collection of any unpaid fine or restitution." Mr. Bernecky believes the $500 restitution has been paid in full. If the record confirms that, the condition's entire stated purpose has been fulfilled — which is itself the change in circumstances § 3583(e)(2) exists to address. The guideline Special Condition (i) implements says the same in its own terms: a financial-disclosure condition of this kind is authorized "if the court imposes an order of restitution, forfeiture, or notice to victims, or orders the defendant to pay a fine," U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(3) (2025), and the companion debt-obligation condition applies only where the defendant is not "in compliance with the payment schedule," *id.* § 5D1.3(d)(2). Full payment removes the guideline's own trigger for either. The Commission's most recent amendments confirm the point: courts should "conduct an individualized assessment to determine what, if any" discretionary conditions remain warranted, *id.* § 5D1.3(b)(1), because conditions imposed "at original sentencing, often years before the defendant begins supervision," can become conditions that are "no longer appropriate after the defendant's release," *id.* § 5D1.4(a).

**Requested relief as to Part IV:** an order (1) clarifying that Special Condition (d) requires disclosure only of the vendor's name for transactions made by credit card, debit card, PayPal or comparable electronic payment service, or cryptocurrency, and does not extend to the amount of any transaction, any account number, or any other financial record — including brokerage, investment, or retirement account records — incapable of showing a payment to a child-pornography vendor; and (2) vacating Special Condition (i) upon confirmation that the $500 restitution obligation has been paid in full.


### V. Special Condition (g) — Polygraph and Computerized Voice Stress Analyzer Testing

Special Condition (g) provides:

> The defendant shall submit to polygraph, computerized voice stress analyzer or other such testing, not to exceed twice in a calendar year, and an additional two re-tests per year, as needed. That testing may include examinations using a polygraph, computerized voice stress analyzer, or other similar device to obtain information necessary for supervision, case monitoring, and treatment. The defendant shall answer the questions posed during the examination, subject to the defendant's right to challenge in a court of law the use of such statements as violations of the defendant's Fifth Amendment rights. In this regard, the defendant shall be deemed not to have waived the defendant's Fifth Amendment rights by making any such statements. The results of any polygraph pre-tests and polygraph examinations may be disclosed to the US Probation office and the Court, but shall not be further disclosed without a court order. The defendant is required to contribute to the cost of services rendered.

Mr. Bernecky does not object to submitting to polygraph or CVSA testing. He does not ask the Court to strike Special Condition (g), to relieve him of testing, or to disturb the twice-a-year schedule and its permitted re-tests. He objects to being commanded to *answer*. The Fifth Amendment protects the right not to answer an incriminating question at the moment it is asked. A promise that some court, later, may decide whether the answer should have been compelled is not that right — by then the answer has been given, and what it revealed cannot be recalled.

The Court should strike three sentences: "The defendant shall answer the questions posed during the examination, subject to the defendant's right to challenge in a court of law the use of such statements as violations of the defendant's Fifth Amendment rights"; "In this regard, the defendant shall be deemed not to have waived the defendant's Fifth Amendment rights by making any such statements"; and "The results of any polygraph pre-tests and polygraph examinations may be disclosed to the US Probation office and the Court, but shall not be further disclosed without a court order." The third is surplusage once the first two are gone: it exists only to govern what becomes of statements Mr. Bernecky was compelled to give, and once he is no longer compelled to give any, there is nothing left for a court-order-gated disclosure rule to protect. Everything else remains exactly as written, including the condition's own first sentence commanding him to *submit to* testing. What remains asks nothing of Mr. Bernecky that the condition's opening sentence does not already ask, and it needs no savings clause to protect a right a bare submission requirement never put at risk.

The governing framework is *Minnesota v. Murphy*, which holds that unconstitutional compulsion exists where the government, "either expressly or by implication, asserts that invocation of the privilege would lead to revocation" or another substantial penalty. 465 U.S. 420, 435 (1984). Murphy lost on that standard, and the two reasons he lost are the two reasons Mr. Bernecky's condition is different. Murphy's own probation condition said nothing at all about his freedom to decline to answer — it required truthfulness if he spoke, not an answer to every question that was put to him. And nothing in Murphy's case, express or implied, threatened revocation for invoking the privilege. Special Condition (g) is the mirror image on both points. It says something about Mr. Bernecky's freedom to decline: it commands that he "shall answer." And it is a condition of supervised release, so a failure to comply with it is, by definition, a violation. The threatened penalty is not a separate statement an officer would have to make in the room; it is built into the condition's own grammar.

That distinction matters because of what invoking the privilege has always required. For decades the Supreme Court has instructed that a witness invokes by doing exactly one thing: declining to answer the question asked, with nothing more required at that moment to protect himself, the validity of the claim being for a court to assess afterward if it is challenged. *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951). Special Condition (g) commands the opposite of that instruction. It tells Mr. Bernecky that the one act the Constitution has always said suffices to protect him — simply declining to answer — is itself a violation of his supervision. This is *Murphy*'s "classic penalty situation" itself, reached by no analogy at all: the penalty is implied by the condition's own command, rather than announced by an officer in the room.

The Tenth Circuit is the only court of appeals to have addressed the question squarely in this setting, and it held that "a threat to revoke one's probation for properly invoking his Fifth Amendment privilege is the type of compulsion the state may not constitutionally impose." *United States v. Von Behren*, 822 F.3d 1139, 1150 (10th Cir. 2016). *Von Behren* is out-of-circuit and persuasive only, and it identified for itself the circumstances in which its result would come out differently: where the question is relevant only to supervised-release status rather than to criminal exposure, where the answer carries no risk of incrimination, or where immunity has been given. *Id.* at 1149 n.9. The government will say Special Condition (g) supplies the third of those, through its savings clause. It does not. Immunity is a promise that the answer and its fruits cannot be used against the speaker — the Fifth Amendment's own coextensive substitute for the privilege. What Special Condition (g) supplies is a preserved right to argue, at some later time and in some later proceeding, that a particular use was improper. Those are different things, and the difference is the entire question presented here. A defendant who is given immunity has lost nothing by answering. A defendant who is given a right to litigate has lost the disclosure itself and retains only an argument about what may be done with it.

The Second Circuit has not decided this. Its one polygraph decision, *United States v. Johnson*, upheld language close enough to Special Condition (g)'s that the resemblance is unlikely to be coincidence — an answer requirement paired with a preserved right to challenge later use. 446 F.3d 272 (2d Cir. 2006). Mr. Bernecky states plainly that *Johnson* is adverse-leaning authority on a condition much like his own. But the theory *Johnson* presented was narrower than the one raised here: Johnson argued that, absent formal use and derivative-use immunity, he faced an unconstitutional choice between confessing to past conduct and standing in violation for remaining silent — a *Kastigar*-style immunity claim built on his own facts. The panel was never asked, and never answered, whether commanding an answer at all, backed only by a later right to litigate the answer's use, is itself the compulsion the Fifth Amendment forbids. That question remains open in this Circuit.

The government's likely answer — that any prosecution flowing from a polygraph answer is speculative — misconceives the rule rather than applies it. Once a person has a valid basis to invoke the privilege, the government may not compel the answer at all; it does not get to compel the answer now on the promise that a court will sort out later whether it should have. The threshold that triggers the rule is deliberately low: a statement is incriminating if it supplies merely "a lead or a link in the chain of evidence that could lead to prosecution." *Von Behren*, 822 F.3d at 1144 (quoting *Maness v. Meyers*, 419 U.S. 449, 461 (1975)); *accord United States v. McDonald*, No. 1:11-CR-01075-MV-1, 2026 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 681, at *\_\_\_ (D.N.M. Jan. 6, 2026) (applying the identical "lead or link" standard from *United States v. Richards*, 958 F.3d 961, 967 (10th Cir. 2020)). Nor must an investigation already exist: "[t]hat is not to say that an active investigation is necessary to make an answer incriminating. Answers that could provide a 'lead' or a 'link' in potential future criminal investigations are also incriminating." *McDonald*, 2026 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 681, at *\_\_\_. Special Condition (g) gets the sequence backward. The constitutional question — whether the answer may be compelled at all — has to be asked before the answer is given. The harm the privilege exists to prevent occurs the moment the answer is spoken; a later ruling that the statement is inadmissible cannot make an investigator un-know what the answer told him, or stop him from following where it leads. The savings clause is also, by its own terms, indefinite about where that later litigation would happen: it preserves a right to challenge use "in a court of law," without saying which one. "Answer first, litigate later" is not a diminished version of the Fifth Amendment's protection. It is the absence of that protection, wearing a savings clause.

The two most recent decisions on this question confirm the diagnosis, and candor requires disclosing that each rejected the facial challenge before it. In *Richards*, the Tenth Circuit upheld a condition requiring the defendant "to submit to periodic polygraph testing," because nothing in the record showed the government had threatened, expressly or by implication, to seek revocation for a valid invocation. 958 F.3d at 967. Applying constitutional avoidance, the court read the condition to leave the defendant "free to legitimately exercise his Fifth Amendment right without facing the risk that a valid assertion of his privilege . . . will result in revocation." *Id.* at 968. Judge Vazquez of the District of New Mexico reached the same result on the same reasoning, denying a facial challenge to a condition requiring the defendant to "*submit to* clinical polygraph examinations," because "neither the Government nor any other entity ha[d] threatened, explicitly or implicitly, to revoke Mr. McDonald's supervised release if he refuses to answer a question during a polygraph examination on valid Fifth Amendment grounds." *McDonald*, 2026 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 681, at *\_\_\_.

Neither decision helps the government here, because neither condition was written the way this one is. Both courts could reach their result only because the operative verb in each condition was *submit to* — language about attendance and participation, not a command to answer. *McDonald* said so expressly: a defendant "may be required to '*submit to* clinical polygraph examinations,' but that does not necessarily translate to 'truthfully *answer* even incriminating questions.'" *Id.* at *\_\_\_. Special Condition (g) closes that gap by its own terms. It does not tell Mr. Bernecky to *submit to* an examination; it tells him to *answer* the questions posed during one, and there is no reading of "shall answer" that leaves the room to decline that "submit to" leaves open. Constitutional avoidance lets a court choose among readings the text can bear; it cannot manufacture a reading the text forecloses. Judge Vazquez recognized as much, noting that a defendant remains free to move "to modify [his] conditions to include language that better strikes a balance" between his constitutional rights and Probation's supervisory interest — precisely the motion Mr. Bernecky now brings. *Id.* at *\_\_\_.

The relief requested is also what the Judiciary's own policy already tells the officer administering this condition to do. The Administrative Office directs that "[i]f the person on supervision refuses to answer a specific question on the grounds that it is self-incriminating, the officer should not compel (e.g., through threat of revocation) the person to answer the question," and that where "there is uncertainty about whether the invocation of the privilege against self-incrimination is valid . . . the probation officer should refer the matter to the court to make this determination." Guide to Judiciary Policy, Vol. 8E, § 250(b). That is exactly the structure Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to make explicit: invocation question by question, with any dispute about a specific invocation referred to this Court, rather than resolved unilaterally in the examination room or deferred to a later proceeding reviewing an answer already given. Special Condition (g)'s "shall answer" does not merely omit that safeguard. It instructs the officer to do what the policy tells him not to.

Two closing observations, offered as reasons the requested modification costs the government nothing rather than as independent grounds for relief. The record contains no instance of dishonesty by Mr. Bernecky — he has never been found, or alleged, to have lied to a probation officer, a treatment provider, or this Court. Certification ¶ *. Whatever verification interest polygraph testing serves, it is not answering a demonstrated problem in this case, which is a reason to prefer the narrower version of the condition where the two versions are otherwise equally available. And Mr. Bernecky's record raises the stakes of the request rather than lowering them. This is his second child-pornography conviction. A compelled answer from a defendant in that position carries materially greater consequences than the same answer from a first-time offender, which is precisely why the protection should operate at the moment of the question rather than after it.

**Requested relief as to Part V:** an order striking, in full and without replacement, Special Condition (g)'s compelled-answer sentence, its accompanying non-waiver sentence, and the now-surplus disclosure sentence, leaving Mr. Bernecky's obligation to *submit to* polygraph and CVSA testing otherwise intact.


### VI. Special Condition (h) — Alcohol Abstinence During Treatment

Special Condition (h) requires participation in mental health treatment and, "[w]hile in treatment or taking psychotropic medication," complete abstinence from alcohol. The provider-rule problem common to any court-ordered treatment program is addressed in Part II and applies here as well. This Part addresses the abstinence requirement itself.

Mr. Bernecky does not object to treatment. He objects to a blanket abstinence rule that applies whether or not any physician has ever said a medication he takes interacts with alcohol. The Second Circuit has vacated a comparable blanket restriction for lacking the individualized nexus § 3583(d) demands. *United States v. Betts*, 886 F.3d 198, 202-03 (2d Cir. 2018) (striking a total alcohol ban as not reasonably related to the § 5D1.3(b) factors where nothing in the record tied the offense, any violation, or any history to alcohol). It has since applied the same rule to a nearly identical "abstain from alcohol during treatment" clause. *See United States v. Rodriguez*, No. 22-1820-cr (2d Cir. Dec. 7, 2023) (summary order). The same gap exists here: nothing in this record ties alcohol to Mr. Bernecky's offense or his history at all. His offense involved images on a device.

The clause's own wording compounds the problem. Its trigger is disjunctive — "[w]hile in treatment *or* taking psychotropic medication" (emphasis added) — so talk therapy alone, with no medication in the picture, is enough to require abstinence, even though no interaction risk can exist without a medication. And treating "psychotropic medication" as a single uniform category ignores that alcohol-interaction risk varies sharply across drug classes. This precise clause is this District's standard template for mental-health-treatment conditions, not a finding made about Mr. Bernecky.

**Requested relief as to Part VI:** an order amending Special Condition (h) so that the alcohol-abstinence requirement applies only upon, and only for as long as, a treating physician determines that alcohol is contraindicated for a specific medication or treatment Mr. Bernecky is actually receiving.


### VII. Standard Conditions 5 and 8 — No Defined Standard for "Approved" or "Permission"

Standard Condition 5 requires Mr. Bernecky to live only at a place "approved by the probation officer." Standard Condition 8 forbids him to communicate with anyone he knows has a felony conviction without the probation officer's advance permission. He knows from each condition's text that permission is required. Neither says what earns it — what an officer is supposed to look for, what Mr. Bernecky would need to show, or what separates a place or a person that gets approved from one that does not.

Because nothing defines what satisfies either standard, an officer is free to condition approval on anything at all. Written this way, these conditions turn Mr. Bernecky's home, his associations, and his speech into something to be granted or withheld at will rather than something governed by a standard he could point to and say he had met. The vagueness principle speaks directly to this: "[a] condition is too vague if it requires a reasonable person to speculate as to its meaning." *United States v. Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77, 80-81 (2d Cir. 2010). Knowing permission is required does Mr. Bernecky no good if nothing tells him what earns it. It is also a delegation problem: a district court "may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122, quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 762-63.

Nor is a standard hiding elsewhere. The only articulated basis Probation has offered for a residence decision is that "the probation officer assesses whether the change is appropriate to meet the defendant's risk and needs and whether it poses any risk to the defendant or to others" — broad enough to justify any outcome and narrow enough to guide none. U.S. Courts, *Overview of Probation and Supervised Release Conditions*, ch. 2, § V (July 2024). Probation knows how to write a real standard when it wants one: its own search-and-seizure guidelines require a written plan, supervisor approval, and a defined reasonable-suspicion showing before a search of Mr. Bernecky's home. Guide to Judiciary Policy, Vol. 8E, Appx. 3A. No comparable structure governs a decision that can put him out of that home entirely.

Mr. Bernecky does not ask the Court to take Probation out of the business of approving a residence or permitting contact. Those are legitimate supervisory tools. He asks only that the tools be governed by a standard he can meet and point to.

**Requested relief as to Part VII:** an order directing that Mr. Bernecky be provided a defined standard or checklist governing what will satisfy "approved" under Standard Condition 5 and "permission" under Standard Condition 8. Mr. Bernecky reserves the right to challenge before this Court the specifics or substance of any standard Probation proposes in response.


### VIII. Standard Condition 12 — Risk Notification

Standard Condition 12, as it appears in Mr. Bernecky's Judgment, provides: "If the probation officer determines that you pose a risk to another person (including an organization), the probation officer may require you to notify the person about the risk and you must comply with that instruction. The probation officer may contact the person and confirm that you have notified the person about the risk." That is the exact condition the Second Circuit held invalid, agreeing that it "is vague and affords too much discretion to the probation officer," and vacating it as indistinguishable from an employer-notification condition already struck down for leaving the obligation to "the probation officer's unfettered discretion." *United States v. Boles*, 914 F.3d 95, 111-12 (2d Cir. 2019) (quoting *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 86).

Within two months of *Boles*, this District entered a standing order directing that the very language Mr. Bernecky's Judgment still contains be removed from every judgment imposing supervision, and replaced with a version requiring the court itself — not the probation officer alone — to determine, based on "criminal record, personal history and characteristics, and the nature and circumstances of [the] offense," that a defendant poses a risk before any notification requirement attaches. *Amended Standing Order, In re United States v. Boles* (W.D.N.Y. Mar. 22, 2019) (Geraci, C.J.). That order directs amendment of "the Judgment and Commitment order in all criminal cases in which a term of probation or supervised release is imposed." *Id.* Mr. Bernecky's Judgment was entered on June 12, 2018 — nine months before the standing order issued — and was never conformed to it. During a pre-supervision meeting, his probation officer presented him with this exact, since-invalidated language and required him to sign a document acknowledging his understanding of it. Certification ¶ *.

Mr. Bernecky asks only that the Court apply to him the correction this District has already ordered for everyone else.[^2]

**Requested relief as to Part VIII:** an order amending Standard Condition 12 to match the language adopted by this District's March 22, 2019 standing order following *Boles*: requiring the Court, in consultation with the probation officer and based on Mr. Bernecky's criminal record, personal history and characteristics, and the nature and circumstances of his offense, to determine that he poses a risk before any notification requirement attaches.
