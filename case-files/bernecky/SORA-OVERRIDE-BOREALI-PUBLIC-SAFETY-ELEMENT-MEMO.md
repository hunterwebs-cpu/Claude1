## 1. INTRODUCTION

This memo picks up where `SORA-DIGITAL-OFFENSE-NO-PHYSICAL-RISK-DEPARTURE-MEMO.md` and `SORA-OVERRIDE-1-ELEMENTS-VERIFICATION-MEMO.md` leave off. Those memos cover the conventional path to a lower classification — arguing for a *discretionary downward departure* from the Override 1 presumptive Level 3. This memo covers a different, more fundamental theory: that Override 1, standing alone, cannot lawfully produce a Level 3 classification at all, because Correction Law § 168-l(6) requires **two** conjunctive findings — "the risk of repeat offense is high" **and** "there exists a threat to the public safety" — and Override 1 (a creature of the Board's own Guidelines, not the statute) collapses both into a single categorical fact (a prior felony sex conviction) with no case-specific finding on the public-safety prong at all.

I address four questions, in the order assigned: (1) whether this specific two-clause argument has ever been presented to and decided by a New York court, as opposed to merely assumed away; (2) whether *Boreali v. Axelrod* administrative-overreach doctrine has ever been applied to SORA's override system, and whether an as-applied challenge is viable here; (3) what NY law requires to prove current dangerousness by clear and convincing evidence in the closest civil analogue (Mental Hygiene Law Article 10), and what that shows about the Case Summary's own showing; and (4) whether raising this theory triggers CPLR 1012(b) Attorney General notice.

**Scope limitations.** I did not re-research or re-verify Override 1's basic elements (covered in the Override Memo) or the digital-offense/no-physical-risk departure theory (covered in the Digital-Offense Memo). ## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1:** Has any New York court actually presented and decided a challenge to whether Override 1, standing alone, can satisfy or substitute for the "threat to public safety" clause of § 168-l(6), as distinct from the "risk of repeat offense" clause?

*Summary conclusion:* No. Every override case I could locate and read — *Moss*, *Gunter*, and the override discussions cited within them — treats the override as producing the Level 3 classification "automatically" once the People prove the qualifying prior conviction by clear and convincing evidence, with no separate finding, argument, or discussion of the public-safety clause as distinct from the repeat-offense clause. This appears to be a genuinely unlitigated, first-impression question in New York.

**Issue 2:** Is a *Boreali*-style administrative-overreach challenge to Override 1 viable as applied to a purely digital-offense case with no contact-offense conduct in either conviction?

*Summary conclusion:* It is a colorable, non-frivolous argument that has never been tried against SORA's override system specifically or, so far as I could find, against any comparable categorical-override/irrebuttable-presumption scheme built by guidance rather than statute. But it faces real headwinds: the Guidelines are expressly statutorily authorized (§ 168-l(5)), the Board's "subject matter expertise" is entitled to "careful consideration" (per *Moss*, quoting *Cook*), and — most practically — the override mechanism has been used, discussed, and implicitly relied on by the Court of Appeals itself in *Gillotti* and *Moss* without the Court ever being asked to test its validity against this theory. A first-instance trial court is unlikely to be the venue that invalidates a mechanism the Court of Appeals has repeatedly applied without comment. I rate this a real but likely losing argument at the SORA-hearing level, worth raising to preserve for appeal, not worth leading with.

**Issue 3:** Does NY law in analogous civil-dangerousness proceedings require expert clinical/actuarial evidence to prove current dangerousness by clear and convincing evidence, or does a bare qualifying prior conviction suffice on its own — and does that show the Case Summary here cannot meet § 168-n's burden on the public-safety element?

*Summary conclusion:* In the closest analogue — Mental Hygiene Law Article 10 civil management — the statute itself is built around a diagnosed clinical condition ("mental abnormality") and expressly forbids a factfinder from concluding dangerousness "solely on the basis of the respondent's commission of a sex offense" (MHL § 10.07(d)). A prior conviction, standing alone, is explicitly insufficient there. That is a real and useful analogy, but it is an analogy, not controlling SORA authority — no SORA case yet imports Article 10's evidentiary structure into the override context, and Override 1's own text asks only whether the offender "has a prior felony conviction for a sex crime," full stop. The argument is available and, read together with *Gillotti*'s own acknowledgment that the CP-to-contact-offense link is scientifically "uncertain," is a serious argument — but it is a novel argument built by analogy, not an existing holding.

**Issue 4:** Does raising this as a challenge to the Guidelines' validity/application (rather than a facial constitutional challenge to the SORA statute) trigger CPLR 1012(b) notice to the Attorney General?

*Summary conclusion:* Unclear, and I could not find a case resolving it either way for a *Boreali*-type argument specifically. CPLR 1012(b)'s trigger is the "constitutionality" of a statute or "a rule and regulation adopted pursuant thereto" — a pure statutory-interpretation/ultra-vires argument that the Guidelines exceed the authority § 168-l(5) delegated is arguably not a "constitutionality" challenge on its face. But *Boreali* doctrine is itself rooted in the separation-of-powers clause of the New York Constitution, and a *Boreali*-style argument is frequently pled in those terms — which would place it squarely inside CPLR 1012(b)'s trigger. Given the ambiguity and the low cost of compliance, my recommendation is to serve notice out of caution if this argument is ever put in writing (a brief, a post-hearing submission, or an appeal), rather than to litigate the question of whether notice was required.

## 5. DISCUSSION/ANALYSIS

### Issue 1: Has the two-clause distinction ever been briefed and decided?

**Rule.** Section 168-l(6) requires *both* that "the risk of repeat offense is high" *and* that "there exists a threat to the public safety" before a Level 3 designation is authorized. Section 168-l(5) instructs the Board to develop guidelines to assess *both* of those things. On its face, nothing in the statute says a single categorical fact can substitute for a finding on the second clause.

**Application — what I actually found on direct read of the override cases.**

I read *Moss*, 43 N.Y.3d 584 (2025); *Gunter*, 217 A.D.3d 788 (2d Dep't 2023); *Stagles*, 222 A.D.3d 1341 (4th Dep't 2023); *Adams*, 216 A.D.3d 1376 (3d Dep't 2023); and *Gillotti*, 23 N.Y.3d 841 (2014) directly and in full for this assignment, specifically looking for any discussion, briefing, or argument distinguishing the "risk of repeat offense" clause from the "threat to public safety" clause in the override context. I found none. Specifically:

- ***Moss*** is the most sustained Court of Appeals discussion of Override 1 available. Its entire analysis is about whether an *undisturbed but collaterally-doubted* prior conviction can trigger the override — i.e., whether the *predicate fact* (a valid prior conviction) exists — not about whether, once that fact exists, it also establishes the *public-safety threat*. The Court's own framing of the statute — quoting § 168-l(5)'s "risk of a repeat offense... and the threat posed to the public safety" language verbatim, 43 N.Y.3d at 588 — shows the Court is aware the statute has two clauses. But defendant Moss never argued that even a valid, undisturbed 2006 conviction failed to independently establish "a threat to the public safety" as opposed to "risk of repeat offense." His argument was entirely about the conviction's validity. The Court affirms the override "properly implemented" (43 N.Y.3d at 590) without any discussion of the two clauses as separate elements requiring separate proof.
- ***Gunter*** states, as adopted holding (not dicta): "The People bear the burden of proving the applicability of a particular override by clear and convincing evidence" and, once that burden is met, "a SORA court is not possessed of any discretion in determining whether to apply [an] override; the application of the override is automatic" (217 A.D.3d at 789, quoting *Johnson*, 135 A.D.3d at 720-721, quoting *Gordon*, 133 A.D.3d at 836). "The applicability of a particular override" is treated as a single, unitary fact question (does the prior conviction exist and qualify) — not two separate questions (does it exist, and does it separately establish a public-safety threat). Gunter's own departure argument (family support, treatment response, age/health) never raised the two-clause theory either.
- ***Stagles*** and ***Adams*** are not override cases at all — both are points-based Factor 3/7 departure cases. They are useful for the departure theory (already covered in the Digital-Offense Memo) but say nothing about the override's relationship to the two statutory clauses.
- ***Gillotti*** is the Court of Appeals' most careful treatment of the departure standard generally, and it is the one place I found the Court explicitly acknowledging the *scientific uncertainty* of the CP-to-contact link (23 N.Y.3d at 859) — but *Gillotti* is a Factor 3/7 departure case, not an override case, and never discusses Override 1 or the two-clause structure.

- ***People v. Bernecky*, 161 A.D.3d 1540 (4th Dep't 2018)** — this client's own prior SORA appeal, read directly for this memo — likewise never raises the two-clause theory, because the 2018 proceeding reached Level Two through ordinary point-scoring, with no override in play at all. But the 2018 opinion is independently valuable evidence for Issue 1 in a different way: it shows that the very same 2012 conduct now being used to trigger Override 1's automatic "high risk and threat to public safety" conclusion was, after full adversarial litigation in 2018, found to support only a *moderate*-risk classification — the clearest available illustration in this client's own record of exactly the gap Issue 1 identifies between what the override presumes and what an actual individualized adjudication on the same facts previously found. See Section 3 above for the important countervailing point (the intervening reoffense) that a court would likely raise in response.

**Conclusion on Issue 1.** Every override case I could locate and read treats "the applicability of the override" as a single, automatic, unitary determination once the predicate conviction fact is proven by clear and convincing evidence — full stop. No case I found presents, briefs, or decides the specific argument that the override satisfies only the "risk of repeat offense" clause and not the independent "threat to public safety" clause. I looked specifically for this in the cases most likely to raise it (the override cases themselves, and this client's own prior SORA litigation) and did not find it even mentioned as a rejected argument — it appears never to have been argued at all, not merely to have lost. **This looks like a genuine first-impression question in New York.** That cuts both ways tactically: no adverse precedent forecloses it, but no favorable precedent supports it either, and a court asked to invalidate an "automatic" mechanism it has applied without hesitation in case after case will likely be skeptical of an argument raised for the first time by a single unrepresented-in-precedent litigant at the trial level.

I did not find, and did not have time within this assignment to exhaustively search, every reported override case in New York — there are almost certainly override cases beyond the five I read closely. The conclusion above is based on the cases identified as most relevant across three research passes in this matter (this one and the two prior SORA memos), not an exhaustive Shepard's-style survey of every Override 1 case in New York. I flag this explicitly in Section 8.

### Issue 2: *Boreali v. Axelrod* and administrative-overreach doctrine applied to Override 1

**Rule — read directly from the New York Court of Appeals opinion, 71 N.Y.2d 1 (1987).**

Having now read Judge Titone's majority opinion directly and in full, the governing framework is stated at 71 N.Y.2d at 11-12: "A number of coalescing circumstances that are present in this case persuade us that the difficult-to-define line between administrative rule-making and legislative policy-making has been transgressed. While none of these circumstances, standing alone, is sufficient to warrant the conclusion that the [agency] has usurped the Legislature's prerogative, all of these circumstances, when viewed in combination, paint a portrait of an agency that has improperly assumed for itself '[t]he open-ended discretion to choose ends.'" The Court then works through four factors, each of which I quote here exactly as it appears in the opinion, with pincites:

1. **Unguided balancing of competing concerns.** The agency "constructed a regulatory scheme laden with exceptions based solely upon economic and social concerns," and had "not been given any legislative guidelines at all for determining how the competing concerns of public health and economic cost are to be weighed" (71 N.Y.2d at 12-13).
2. **Writing on a clean slate.** "[T]he PHC did not merely fill in the details of broad legislation describing the over-all policies to be implemented. Instead, the PHC wrote on a clean slate, creating its own comprehensive set of rules without benefit of legislative guidance," in contrast to "the 'interstitial' rule making that typifies administrative regulatory activity" (71 N.Y.2d at 13).
3. **Legislative stalemate.** "[T]he agency acted in an area in which the Legislature had repeatedly tried — and failed — to reach agreement in the face of substantial public debate and vigorous lobbying by a variety of interested factions" (71 N.Y.2d at 13-14).
4. **No special expertise actually exercised.** "[N]o special expertise or technical competence in the field of health was involved in the development of the antismoking regulations challenged here" (71 N.Y.2d at 14).

Applying these, the Court held the Public Health Council's comprehensive smoking code "ultra vires" — an exercise of "inherently legislative" policy-choice that the broad enabling statute (Public Health Law § 225(5)(a)) could not, consistent with the separation-of-powers clause of the New York Constitution (art. III, § 1), be construed to authorize (71 N.Y.2d at 6, 9, 16). Judge Bellacosa's dissent — which I also read in full — is worth noting for what it reveals about the argument's vulnerability: he would have upheld the regulation precisely because the PHC's authority had been "exercised regularly with this court's express approbation" for 75 years without ever being narrowed (71 N.Y.2d at 17), and he warned that the majority's approach would "be welcomed by opponents of all kinds of existing laws from now on" (71 N.Y.2d at 19) — i.e., that *Boreali* invites exactly the kind of long-settled-practice objection the Board would raise against Bernecky's argument here (see below).

**Has *Boreali* ever been applied to SORA's Guidelines or override system?** I searched specifically for this and found nothing. No case, law review article, or bar association report I could locate discusses a *Boreali*-style challenge to the SORA Guidelines generally or to any of the four overrides specifically. This is, so far as this research could determine, an unexplored theory in the SORA context.

**Application — running Override 1 through the four factors precisely, on Bernecky's specific facts.**

*Factor 2 (clean slate vs. filling in details) — the strongest factor for the challenge.* Section 168-l(6) requires the SORA court to find, as a predicate to Level 3, both that "the risk of repeat offense is high" *and* that "there exists a threat to the public safety." Override 1 does not fill in interstitial detail on how to make that second finding — it substitutes a single categorical fact for it entirely, with (per *Gunter*, 217 A.D.3d at 789) *zero* discretion left to the SORA court once the predicate conviction is proven. That is closer to "writing on a clean slate" than to "interstitial" rule-making: nothing in § 168-l(5) or its enumerated factors instructs the Board that a prior conviction should be treated as conclusively, irrebuttably establishing a public-safety threat regardless of how remote in time, how it compares to the instant offense, or what intervening evidence might show. Bernecky's own case makes the point concretely: the 2012 conviction was already found, on the merits, in 2018, to support only a *moderate*-risk classification (see Section 3) — the override does not build on that prior individualized finding, it displaces it with a new categorical one.

*Factor 4 (no special expertise actually exercised) — also a real toehold.* Override 1 applies no clinical or actuarial judgment whatsoever; it is a bare records check for a qualifying prior conviction. That stands in real contrast to the RAI's individually scored factors (offense conduct, treatment response, supervision), which at least purport to embody the Board's forensic-risk-assessment expertise. An override that asks only "does a qualifying conviction exist" is not an exercise of the Board's "subject matter expertise" in the sense *Moss* invokes that phrase (43 N.Y.3d at 588) — it is closer to the PHC's "simple code" in *Boreali* itself, which the Court found reflected no exercise of the agency's technical competence at all (71 N.Y.2d at 14).

*Factor 1 (unguided balancing of competing concerns) — does not clearly apply, and this is a real weakness.* Unlike the PHC in *Boreali*, which openly balanced public-health goals against "economic dislocations" in its own stated findings (71 N.Y.2d at 12), nothing in the Guidelines' text or in the Board's Position Statement (which I read directly in the prior verification memo) shows the Board weighing competing *economic or social* concerns in adopting Override 1. Override 1 reads as a straightforward risk-weighting judgment, not a cost-benefit trade-off between incommensurable interests. This factor, on the current record, does not support the challenge.

*Factor 3 (legislative stalemate) — no evidence found, and this is a real weakness.* *Boreali* required evidence that "the Legislature had repeatedly tried — and failed — to reach agreement" on the specific policy the agency then adopted on its own (71 N.Y.2d at 13-14) — in that case, 40 failed bills over more than a decade, discussed at length in the opinion (71 N.Y.2d at 7). I found no comparable legislative history showing the New York Legislature considered and rejected codifying an automatic override, or specifically rejected defining "threat to public safety" more precisely than § 168-l(5) does. Without that showing, this factor is simply unavailable on the current record — not because it cuts against the challenge, but because there is nothing to put on the scale for it either way absent further legislative-history research (see Recommendations).

**Honest tally.** Of *Boreali*'s four factors, this record plausibly supports two (factors 2 and 4), does not support one (factor 1), and is simply unresearched on one (factor 3). *Boreali* itself stressed that none of its four factors is independently sufficient — it is the combination that mattered in that case, where all four were present on a well-developed record. Having only two (or at most three, pending legislative-history research) of four factors, several of them weaker analogues than the smoking-code facts that produced them, means this is a materially thinner *Boreali* case than *Boreali* itself. That does not make the argument frivolous — factor 2 in particular is a genuinely strong match — but it does mean the Writer should not overstate how squarely this fact pattern tracks the precedent.

Beyond the four-factor analysis itself, two further considerations matter:

- § 168-l(5) is an express statutory delegation authorizing the Board to "develop guidelines and procedures to assess the risk of a repeat offense... and the threat posed to the public safety," "based upon" enumerated factors that include "the number, date and nature of [the offender's] prior offenses" (§ 168-l(5)(b)(iii), as quoted in *Moss*, 43 N.Y.3d at 588). A court could characterize Override 1 as simply *weighting* one enumerated statutory factor so heavily that it becomes dispositive — a policy choice about how much a prior conviction should count, not an act of "writing on a clean slate" the way the PHC's comprehensive smoking code did, which had no comparable statutory anchor at all (Public Health Law § 225(5)(a) said nothing about smoking specifically). This is a real distinction working against the challenge, and it is the natural rejoinder to the factor-2 argument above.
- *Moss* itself affords "careful consideration to the Board's interpretations of th[e] Guidelines, recognizing the Board's legislative mandate to promulgate them and the Board's subject matter expertise" (43 N.Y.3d at 588, quoting *Cook*, 29 N.Y.3d at 129). That is Court of Appeals deference language working directly against a challenge to the Guidelines' validity.
- **The single strongest practical headwind, exactly as the assignment anticipated, and exactly what Judge Bellacosa's dissent in *Boreali* itself warns about:** the Court of Appeals has used, discussed, and applied the override system in both *Gillotti* (which describes and relies on the Guidelines' overall structure without ever suggesting any part of it is ultra vires) and *Moss* (which affirms an override application on a different theory entirely, treating "the Guidelines' text" as self-evidently controlling, 43 N.Y.3d at 590) without ever being asked to test, and therefore without ever validating, whether the override mechanism itself is a lawful exercise of the delegated guideline-making authority. That silence is not precedent either way — it is simply the absence of the question ever being raised — but as a matter of institutional reality, a trial-level SORA court is unlikely to be the first tribunal willing to hold that a mechanism the state's highest court has repeatedly and unremarkably applied for nearly two decades is actually ultra vires. This argument's best audience is an appellate court, not the September 11 hearing itself.

**Conclusion on Issue 2.** This is a real, non-frivolous, apparently novel argument, with one factor (factor 2, the "clean slate" factor) that maps onto Override 1's structure unusually well — arguably better than it maps onto ordinary agency rule-filling generally, because the override does not merely interpret an ambiguous statutory term, it dispenses with an entire statutory element's individualized-finding requirement. But it is a materially weaker *Boreali* case than *Boreali* itself on the other three factors, and it faces the same institutional headwind *Boreali*'s own dissent flagged: long, unquestioned administrative and judicial reliance is a real (if not doctrinally dispositive) reason courts hesitate to be the first to say no. I recommend treating it as a preservation-and-appeal argument, not a hearing-winning argument, and pairing it with (not substituting it for) the departure theory already developed in the other two memos.

### Issue 3: The Article 10 evidentiary analogy

**Rule — read directly from the statute.** Mental Hygiene Law § 10.03(i) defines "mental abnormality" as "a congenital or acquired condition, disease or disorder that affects the emotional, cognitive, or volitional capacity of a person in a manner that predisposes him or her to the commission of conduct constituting a sex offense and that results in that person having serious difficulty in controlling such conduct" (quoted as I read it directly from the statute via nysenate.gov). Section 10.05(g) requires a "written report from a psychiatric examiner that includes a finding as to whether the respondent has a mental abnormality" as a prerequisite before the Attorney General may even file a civil-management petition. Most directly on point: **§ 10.07(d)** — which I confirmed verbatim, directly from the statute — instructs that "the jury may not find solely on the basis of the respondent's commission of a sex offense that the respondent is a detained sex offender who suffers from a mental abnormality." Section 10.07 further requires the ultimate finding of mental abnormality (and, at disposition, of "dangerous sex offender requiring confinement" status) to be proven by clear and convincing evidence.

In other words: in the New York proceeding structurally closest to what a "threat to public safety" finding is actually supposed to represent — an individualized judicial determination that a specific person currently presents a heightened danger of sexual reoffense — the Legislature has written into the statute itself an explicit prohibition on inferring that danger from the bare fact of a qualifying conviction alone. A diagnosed clinical condition, established through a psychiatric examiner's report and (ordinarily) expert testimony, is a structural precondition to the proceeding even existing.

**A useful contrast — Extreme Risk Protection Orders.** I also checked CPLR Article 63-A (ERPOs), the other clear-and-convincing-evidence civil-dangerousness proceeding available in New York, to see whether *every* C&C dangerousness finding in New York requires clinical evidence, or whether Article 10's structure is a deliberate legislative choice specific to sex-offense civil commitment. CPLR § 6343 requires the petitioner to prove "by clear and convincing evidence" that the respondent "is likely to engage in conduct that would result in serious harm to himself, herself or others" — but ERPO's dangerousness finding is not gated behind a diagnosed clinical condition; it is a behavioral, fact-specific finding a lay factfinder can make directly from evidence of the respondent's own conduct and statements, without any statutory requirement of expert psychiatric testimony. This confirms that Article 10's insistence on a diagnosed clinical predicate is not simply "how New York proves dangerousness" generally — it is a deliberate structural choice New York makes specifically where the underlying judgment is inherently clinical (a "condition, disease or disorder" affecting volitional capacity), as opposed to a straightforwardly factual judgment about a person's recent conduct and statements (ERPO).

**Application to Bernecky.** SORA's "threat to public safety" finding sits doctrinally much closer to Article 10's clinical judgment than to ERPO's behavioral one — it is asking, in substance, whether this particular person's psychology and history make him currently likely to pose a danger of sexual reoffense, which is exactly the kind of individualized clinical-risk judgment Article 10 requires expert evidence to support and exactly the kind of judgment *Gillotti* itself flags as scientifically contested for CP offenders specifically ("the relationship between an offender's child pornography offenses and the likelihood he or she will commit hands-on offenses is uncertain due to the continuing development of scientific research," 23 N.Y.3d at 859). Read together, Article 10's structural insistence on a diagnosed clinical predicate and *Gillotti*'s acknowledgment of scientific uncertainty in exactly this offense category support the argument that a Case Summary built entirely from narrative conviction facts — with no psychiatric examiner's report, no actuarial instrument, no expert opinion of any kind — cannot, without more, meet § 168-n(3)'s clear-and-convincing burden on the public-safety element specifically, as opposed to the bare-facts-suffice repeat-offense element.

**The honest limitation on this argument.** This is an analogy, not a citation to controlling SORA authority. No SORA case imports Article 10's evidentiary structure into the override or departure context, and existing override case law (*Gunter*, *Lobello*) treats the DA's clear-and-convincing burden as satisfied by proof of the qualifying conviction itself — nothing more. A court could reasonably reject the analogy on the ground that SORA classification (a registration/notification consequence) and Article 10 civil management (involuntary confinement) are different statutory schemes with different stakes, and that the Legislature knew how to write an Article-10-style clinical-evidence requirement into SORA if it wanted one, and did not. That is a fair distinction, and the Writer should anticipate it. But it does not defeat the argument that, at minimum, the Board's own reasoning here — "provides compelling evidence that he poses a serious risk to public safety" from the bare fact of the prior conviction, per the Case Summary — is doing exactly the inferential work Article 10's Legislature considered and expressly rejected as insufficient in the closest analogous proceeding, which is a legitimate point to press even if it does not compel a particular outcome.

**Validation note on *Matter of State v. Donald DD*, 24 N.Y.3d 174 (2014).** I found this case through secondary-source search (Court of Appeals holding that an antisocial personality disorder diagnosis, standing alone alongside the underlying sex-crime facts, is legally insufficient to support a mental-abnormality finding without an independent diagnosis) — it reinforces the same point (bare facts plus a non-specific label are not enough; a diagnostically-grounded, individualized showing is required). I was **not** able to independently read this opinion (Justia and CourtListener both 403'd on this attempt). Treat this citation as category (b) — a lead worth pulling and confirming before it is used in any filing, not a confirmed citation.

### Issue 4: CPLR 1012(b) Attorney General notice

**Rule — read directly from the statute.** CPLR 1012(b) requires notice to the Attorney General "[w]hen the constitutionality of a statute of the state, or a rule and regulation adopted pursuant thereto is involved" in an action to which the state is not already a party, and bars a court from considering "any challenge to the constitutionality" of a state statute, local law, or regulation unless proof of service of that notice is filed.

**Application.** The operative trigger is "constitutionality," not any challenge to a rule or regulation's lawfulness. A pure *Boreali*-style argument that Override 1 exceeds the guideline-making authority § 168-l(5) actually delegated is, on its strictest reading, a statutory-interpretation/ultra-vires argument, not framed as a violation of any specific constitutional provision — and on that framing, it arguably falls outside CPLR 1012(b)'s literal trigger. But this framing is fragile: *Boreali* doctrine itself is expressly grounded in the separation-of-powers principle drawn from N.Y. Constitution art. III, § 1 (confirmed directly in the Third Department *Boreali* opinion I read: "By virtue of this constitutional mandate, the Legislature may not delegate its lawmaking function to an administrative agency"). A *Boreali* argument pled or argued in separation-of-powers terms — which is how *Boreali* claims are conventionally framed — would likely be treated by a court as implicating "constitutionality" for CPLR 1012(b) purposes, even though the underlying doctrine is sometimes also described in purely administrative-law terms.

I could not find any case squarely deciding whether a *Boreali*-type challenge to an agency guideline triggers CPLR 1012(b) notice, in either direction. This is a genuinely open procedural question on the current record, not a resolved one.

**Practical recommendation, independent of the doctrinal answer.** A SORA classification hearing is not a plenary CPLR action, and the Attorney General does not ordinarily appear at one — the DA represents "the state" at SORA hearings under § 168-n(3). If this theory is raised only orally at the September 11 hearing, in the context of contesting the override's application to this specific offender, the CPLR 1012(b) question may never practically arise. But if the argument is ever reduced to a written motion, post-hearing brief, or appellate brief that argues the Guidelines/override provision is invalid as exceeding statutory authority — as opposed to simply arguing the override should not be *applied* to these facts — the safer course is to serve CPLR 1012(b) notice on the Attorney General regardless of how the argument is characterized, given the uncertainty above and the low cost of compliance relative to the risk that a favorable ruling could later be vacated or bypassed for lack of notice.

## 6. CONCLUSION

On Issue 1, I am confident: this is a first-impression argument in New York. I found no case that presents or decides the two-clause distinction, in the override context or otherwise, across six override-adjacent cases read directly and in full (including this client's own 2018 SORA appeal, which never had occasion to raise it).

On Issue 2, my honest read, now grounded in a direct reading of the actual Court of Appeals opinion rather than a secondary reconstruction, is that the *Boreali* theory is intellectually serious but tactically weak at the trial level and stronger as an appellate preservation point. Two of the four *Boreali* factors (the "clean slate" factor and the "no special expertise exercised" factor) map onto Override 1 unusually well; the other two (unguided economic/social balancing, and legislative stalemate) are unsupported on the current record. The better side of the argument, on the merits, is probably Bernecky's — Override 1's total absence of any individualized public-safety finding is hard to reconcile with the statute's plain conjunctive text, and the "clean slate" factor is a genuinely strong match — but the better side of the *litigation prediction*, given institutional deference, the Court of Appeals' repeated unquestioning use of the override system, and the missing third and fourth factors, is the Board's. I would put this at meaningfully less than even money to succeed before the SORA court itself, materially better on a fully preserved appeal.

On Issue 3, the Article 10 analogy is a genuinely useful rhetorical and structural argument — it gives concrete statutory content to what "public safety" ought to require that the SORA Guidelines never spell out — but it is a persuasive analogy, not a controlling citation, and should be presented to the court as such.

On Issue 4, I recommend erring toward serving CPLR 1012(b) notice if this argument is ever committed to a written filing that argues the Guidelines exceed statutory authority, rather than resolving the open question by omission.

## 7. RECOMMENDATIONS

1. **Do not lead the September 11 hearing with the Issue 1/2 theory.** It is a first-impression, uphill argument at a trial-level SORA hearing that has no authority to declare a Guidelines provision ultra vires in the first place (that relief realistically requires an Article 78/declaratory-judgment vehicle or an appeal, not a SORA classification hearing). Raise it to preserve the issue, in the alternative to and after the conventional departure argument.
2. **Prepare an honest answer to the "second offense" counterargument before raising the 2018-case comparison (Section 3).** The 2018 Level Two classification on the same 2012 conduct is a genuinely powerful illustration of Issue 1/Issue 2's theoretical point, but the instant offense is a second, more recent CP conviction — the People's obvious response (recency and repetition of qualifying conduct as independent evidence of increased dangerousness) needs to be anticipated and addressed head-on, not left for opposing counsel to raise first.
3. **Pull and read *Matter of State v. Donald DD / Kenneth T.*, 24 N.Y.3d 174 (2014), and NYC C.L.A.S.H., Inc. v. New York State Office of Parks, Recreation & Historic Preservation, 27 N.Y.3d 174 (2016)** (the modern Court of Appeals case most often cited for restating and applying the *Boreali* factors in a non-*Boreali* fact pattern) before either is cited — both remain secondary-source leads only.
4. **Consider whether the vehicle matters more than the argument.** If the team is serious about the *Boreali* theory, it may be worth discussing with the client and lead counsel whether an Article 78/declaratory-judgment challenge to Override 1's validity (a proceeding to which CPLR 1012(b) unambiguously applies, and where the Attorney General's participation would actually be useful to have on the record either way) is a better vehicle than folding it into the SORA hearing itself.
5. **Investigate whether the People or the Board intend to offer any expert or actuarial evidence at the September 11 hearing.** If the hearing proceeds on the Case Summary alone, the Article 10 evidentiary-gap argument (Issue 3) is at its strongest and should be pressed directly against the specific sentence in the Case Summary quoted in Section 3 above.
6. **Research the legislative history behind § 168-l(5)/(6) and Override 1's 2006 promulgation specifically for *Boreali* factor 3 (legislative stalemate).** This memo found no evidence either way on whether the Legislature ever considered and failed to codify an automatic override or a more precise "threat to public safety" standard; that factor is currently unresearched, not merely unsupported, and filling it in could materially strengthen (or further weaken) the Issue 2 analysis.
7. **This memo's Issue 1 conclusion rests on six cases, not an exhaustive survey.** Before representing to any court that this is unquestionably a first-impression question, a broader citator-style search across all reported Override 1 cases (not just the ones already in this matter's file) should be run.
