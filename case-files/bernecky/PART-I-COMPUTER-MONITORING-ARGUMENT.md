### I. Special Condition (a) — The Computer and Internet Monitoring Program

Special Condition (a) does three things: it makes Mr. Bernecky's use of any "internet capable device" contingent on participating in CIMP; it authorizes Probation to install monitoring software on any device he "owns or operates"; and it authorizes unannounced examination of any computer equipment he "owns or uses," subject to an instruction that monitoring "avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information." Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. This Part identifies four separate problems with that text and with how Probation has actually implemented it. Part I.A addresses whether the condition reaches Mr. Bernecky's employer, a question a real employer has already had to guess the answer to. Part I.B addresses what CIMP monitoring actually consists of, since the condition itself never says. Part I.C addresses two words in the condition's device-scope language — "operated" and "used" — that have not aged well since 2018. Part I.D addresses the CIMP Participant Agreement that Probation actually requires Mr. Bernecky to sign, attached as Exhibit A, and five of its provisions that exceed what Special Condition (a) itself authorizes.

**A. The sentencing court made no occupational-restriction finding, and a real employer has already had to guess what Special Condition (a) means for its own systems.**

Mr. Bernecky's conduct underlying this conviction occurred entirely on devices he acquired in his own name; no employer's equipment or account was ever involved. Certification of Jeffrey Bernecky ¶ *. While housed at the Rochester Residential Reentry Center, he obtained temporary placement with a contact-lens manufacturer. *Id.* ¶ *. After roughly ninety to one hundred twenty days — the point at which the company's own practice allows a temporary worker to be considered for direct hire — the manufacturer invited him to apply for permanent, full-time employment. *Id.* ¶ *. He disclosed his conviction and his coming conditions of supervision in full, including the Judgment itself once a general description did not resolve the company's questions. *Id.* ¶ *. The company's human resources office then asked whether Special Condition (a) would require it to open its own computer systems to federal monitoring simply because Mr. Bernecky was on its payroll. *Id.* ¶ *. Special Condition (a)'s text does not answer that question. Neither its monitoring authorization nor its examination authorization mentions an employer, an occupation, or a place of business anywhere in its text, Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B, and no one ever asked the sentencing court to address the subject.

Mr. Bernecky asked his probation officer directly whether the company's own account restrictions might substitute for the monitoring the condition otherwise contemplates. Certification ¶ *. The officer's first answer was that whatever the company put in place would need to satisfy an unspecified federal standard — an honest answer, but one drawn from no finding this Court has ever made, because none exists. *Id.* ¶ *. When the company, still waiting on an answer, told Mr. Bernecky not to report to work until the question was resolved, he left his probation officer a voicemail and forwarded the company's own email describing its concern. *Id.* ¶ *. After roughly a week, the officer told him that an account without internet access, paired with the company's own monitoring capability, would satisfy her, and that she intended to convey that same understanding directly to the company. *Id.* ¶ *. Mr. Bernecky does not fault his probation officer for any of this; she was answering a question this Judgment gives her no tool to answer, prompted by an employer that, reasonably enough, wanted assurance before opening its own systems to anyone. What the record shows is that an informal, unwritten, ad hoc understanding — reached after two weeks without pay — is presently the only thing standing between Mr. Bernecky and his next job, and the one after that, for as long as this condition goes uncorrected.

An occupational restriction requires the sentencing court to find "a reasonably direct relationship . . . between the defendant's occupation, business, or profession and the conduct relevant to the offense of conviction," together with a finding that nothing less restrictive would work. U.S.S.G. § 5F1.5(a); *see United States v. Peterson*, 248 F.3d 79, 85-86 (2d Cir. 2001) (per curiam). Nothing in this record supplies that relationship. Every other restriction on Mr. Bernecky's liberty appears as a written condition on Sheet 3B of the Judgment; an occupational restriction, had the sentencing court meant to impose one, would appear the same way. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. None does. Special Condition (a) restricts what Mr. Bernecky may do with his own devices. It says nothing about his occupation, his employer, or his choice of work, because on the face of the document the sentencing court actually signed, it was never written to, and nothing in this record supplies the "reasonably direct relationship" § 5F1.5 demands between his occupation and his offense.

The Second Circuit has already required this same showing for restrictions considerably milder than what Special Condition (a) has already produced. Even a bare employer-*notification* clause has been treated as an occupational restriction demanding the § 5F1.5 showing. *See United States v. Love*, No. 24-1706-cr, 2025 WL 1077176 (2d Cir. Apr. 8, 2025); *United States v. Jenkins*, 854 F.3d 181 (2d Cir. 2017); *United States v. Ruff*, No. 18-3247-cr (2d Cir. Nov. 14, 2019) (vacating outright); *United States v. Lombardi*, No. 17-2352-cr (2d Cir. Mar. 22, 2018) (remanding); *United States v. Piper*, No. 19-1058-cr (2d Cir. Mar. 30, 2020) (remanding). Special Condition (a) contains no notification requirement at all — Mr. Bernecky volunteered his own disclosure — and it has already produced something well beyond notification: a live capability dispute with a real employer, months before supervision even began.

*United States v. Brown*, 173 F.4th 45 (2d Cir. 2026), does not control a different result. Brown argued that his condition operated as a compelled notification requirement; Mr. Bernecky makes no comparable claim, since his disclosure was voluntary and the employer already accepted it. Brown's condition also contained its own "as permitted by the employer" consent mechanism, which Special Condition (a) lacks entirely, and the Brown panel resolved the occupational-restriction question on plain-error review, not de novo.

The Tenth Circuit confronted the identical confusion over materially the same condition language in *United States v. Mike*, and did not leave the ambiguity standing. 632 F.3d 686, 696-97 (10th Cir. 2011). There, even the government conceded the condition "cannot be read fairly" to reach an employer's computers, and the court remanded with instructions to clarify the condition, directing that if the district court actually meant to reach an employer's devices, it would first need to make the § 5F1.5 findings. *Id.*; *see also United States v. Matteson*, 327 F. App'x 791 (10th Cir. 2009). The government may respond that Special Condition (a)'s own "internet capable device" language reaches an employer's computers regardless of what any individual employee account can do. That reading proves too much: on that logic, every employer of every person on CIMP supervision has its entire computer network conscripted into a federal monitoring program the moment it hires that person — a result no § 5F1.5 finding in this record authorizes and no employer would knowingly accept. The relief *Mike* itself ordered is the relief requested here: a clarification that, absent any § 5F1.5 finding in this record, Special Condition (a) will not be read to reach an employer's own systems.

A further, independent reason favors resolving this now. An employer with no connection to this criminal case, and no opportunity to be heard in it, is left to guess whether hiring Mr. Bernecky opens its own computer systems to federal surveillance, and that employer will never realistically appear in this case to protect its own interest in staying out of someone else's supervision. Courts have long accounted for exactly this kind of interest even when the party before the court is not the one who holds it, because the actual holder faces a practical barrier to litigating the violation itself. *See Powers v. Ohio*, 499 U.S. 400, 410-15 (1991) (permitting a defendant to raise excluded jurors' own rights because the jurors faced a practical barrier to litigating the violation themselves); *Singleton v. Wulff*, 428 U.S. 106, 117 (1976) (permitting physicians to assert patients' rights where a "genuine obstacle" separated the right-holder from the courthouse); *Warth v. Seldin*, 422 U.S. 490, 499-500 (1975) (describing the same practical-obstacle exception); *cf. Kowalski v. Tesmer*, 543 U.S. 125, 130-31 (2004) (denying standing where no comparable obstacle existed). The employer here faces exactly the obstacle *Powers* and *Singleton* describe: it has no vehicle to appear in someone else's criminal case and protect its own interest before the harm — losing an employee it wanted to hire, or losing the use of its own systems — has already occurred.

**Requested relief as to Part I.A:** Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to amend Special Condition (a) to add clarifying language confirming that, consistent with the absence of any § 5F1.5 finding in this record, the condition does not reach his employment and imposes no burden on any employer — for example, language along the lines of "with the exception of systems necessary for employment."

**B. Special Condition (a) never specifies what monitoring actually consists of, and keystroke logging is the wrong default.**

Mr. Bernecky served an earlier term of supervision under a materially similar computer-monitoring condition, and Probation's administration of that condition resulted in continuous keystroke logging — capturing everything he typed on every covered device. Certification of Jeffrey Bernecky ¶ *. Special Condition (a) itself never specifies how CIMP monitoring is actually carried out; left blank, that gap gets filled by whatever Probation's standard practice happens to be, and nothing distinguishes Mr. Bernecky's current supervision from that history absent a different order from this Court. *Id.* ¶ *. Keystroke logging is not one option on a menu of monitoring methods. It is the single most extreme point on that spectrum — a wholesale, content-blind capture of every keystroke rather than any kind of targeted review. *See United States v. Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d 173, 192-93 & n.11 (2d Cir. 2004) (comparing unfiltered monitoring to "searching a defendant's diary," and identifying keyword- or phrase-triggered review as a narrower alternative). The choice among monitoring methods belongs to the Court in the first instance, not to Probation's administrative default.

Privilege is the sharpest reason the choice matters. Keystroke logging cannot distinguish an ordinary message from the kinds of communication the law has long protected as confidential: a conversation with an attorney, "the oldest of the privileges for confidential communications known to the common law," *Upjohn Co. v. United States*, 449 U.S. 383, 389 (1981); a confession to clergy, made in a spiritual capacity with a reasonable expectation of confidence, *In re Grand Jury Investigation*, 918 F.2d 374, 384 (3d Cir. 1990); a session with a psychotherapist, protected under Federal Rule of Evidence 501 because candid treatment depends on confidentiality, *Jaffee v. Redmond*, 518 U.S. 1, 10-15 (1996); and a private communication between spouses, *Blau v. United States*, 340 U.S. 332 (1951); *Trammel v. United States*, 445 U.S. 40, 51 (1980). Keystroke logging captures every one of these along with everything else, before anyone has any chance to sort privileged material out. Special Condition (a) already promises that monitoring will "avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information or any private material that is not illegal," Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B, and *Kunz* has held that promise to be a real, enforceable limit on Probation, "constrain[ing] the supervisors in their surveillance . . . by requiring them not to abuse their power and access." 68 F.4th at 766-67. There is nothing left for a method that records everything to avoid; everything has already been captured before the promise can operate.

Formal privilege is only part of what keystroke logging would reach. A diary, a private draft of a memoir, research into a stock purchase, or a running, unsent complaint about a probation officer are not privileged in the technical sense, but none is the target of any supervisory interest CIMP is meant to serve, and a computer being where a person keeps his private thoughts should not cost him the ability to keep them private. The Supreme Court has already described what that loss looks like at the scale a modern device makes possible: a phone or computer today holds "the sum of an individual's private life," a volume of personal material that, before devices like these existed, "most people cannot lug around" at all. *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373, 393-97 (2014).

Mr. Bernecky's own offense does not call for monitoring at that scale. He was convicted of possessing images, not of anything involving written communication, and the Second Circuit has already approved considerably narrower tools for this same category of offense: a restriction "limited to pornography sites and images" in *United States v. Sofsky*, 287 F.3d 122, 126-27 (2d Cir. 2002), and the keyword-triggered alternative *Lifshitz* itself identified, rather than "recording all varieties of computer-related activity." 369 F.3d at 193 n.11.

None of this means Mr. Bernecky should go unmonitored. This is his second sex-offense-related conviction, as set out in the Statement of Facts above, and he agrees that his computer use warrants real supervision. The relief he seeks is supervision pointed at the actual risk: WDNY's own CIMP vendor, IPPC Technologies, already sells a periodic, non-continuous review tool called "Express Scan." Mr. Bernecky offers to submit to it, configured specifically to flag image content, search terms, and other material relevant to child pornography, in place of a method that logs everything he types regardless of subject.

**Requested relief as to Part I.B:** an order specifying that Special Condition (a)'s monitoring method will be IPPC's Express Scan, or an equivalent periodic, non-continuous tool, configured to flag image content, search terms, and other material relevant to child pornography, in place of continuous keystroke capture; or, if that configuration is not administratively available, any other method that does not sweep in privileged or non-image material — domain review, file-type filtering, image-hash scanning, or the keyword-triggered approach *Lifshitz* already identifies.

**C. The words "operated" and "used" no longer supply a workable boundary on whose devices Special Condition (a) reaches, and should be struck in favor of the ownership standard the condition already uses elsewhere.**

Special Condition (a) ties its two most intrusive authorizations to a verb other than ownership: Probation may "surveil all activity on computer(s) or connected device(s) owned *or operated* by the defendant," and may conduct unannounced examinations of "any computer equipment owned *or used* by the defendant." Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. In 2018, "operated" and "used" may have read as harmless synonyms for "owned." Ordinary life has since moved from an internet-access world — a defendant sits down at a computer he owns and connects it to the internet — to an internet-of-things world, in which most of what a person interacts with over the course of an ordinary day is itself a networked computer. Federal law's own definition of "computer" shows how far that shift has gone: "an electronic, magnetic, optical, electrochemical, or other high speed data processing device performing logical, arithmetic, or storage functions." 18 U.S.C. § 1030(e)(1) (Computer Fraud and Abuse Act). Read against that definition, and untethered from ownership, "operated" and "used" sweep in a public library's computer terminal, a store's self-checkout kiosk, a bank's ATM, and a car's onboard entertainment and navigation system — devices Mr. Bernecky "uses" in the ordinary sense of the word, none of which the condition's drafters could plausibly have meant to place under CIMP surveillance.

*Kunz* shows this Court is willing to read CIMP boilerplate sensibly rather than literally when a literal reading would sweep too far, but the facts there were considerably milder than these. The question in *Kunz* was whether an encryption-related clause required pre-approval every time a supervisee visited an ordinary "https" site or sent an encrypted message; no one had actually treated that reading as live, and the court needed only say so. 68 F.4th at 764-65. Here, the broader reading is not hypothetical. An employer already worried enough about what "operated" and "used" might require of its own systems to withhold Mr. Bernecky's job for two weeks, Certification of Jeffrey Bernecky ¶ *, and Probation resolved the question only through an improvised, unwritten understanding, not through any settled interpretation of the condition's text. *Id.* An interpretive canon built on everyone recognizing the same "obvious" meaning does not solve a problem that has already produced a real dispute over what that meaning is. The fix Mr. Bernecky requests is accordingly to strike the words, not to ask the Court to bless a narrower reading and hope the next employer reads it the same way.

Other circuits have recognized that some device-scope boundary is necessary. *United States v. Ullman* drew that boundary at internet access itself, treating "devices without Internet access, such as gaming systems" as outside a comparable condition's reach. No. 14-1414, 2015 WL 3559221 (10th Cir. June 8, 2015). That particular boundary no longer does useful work — gaming systems, medical devices, home appliances, and cars are now routinely internet-connected, so a line drawn at internet access alone would exempt almost nothing. The boundary that still does useful work is the one *Kunz* itself points to: capability to access, search for, or display child pornography specifically, not connectivity in the abstract. A modern smart television illustrates the difference. It connects to the internet to deliver services like Netflix and Apple TV, often without any web browser at all, and no one could seriously argue that Netflix is airing illegal child pornography, or that a device built only to stream it presents the risk CIMP exists to manage. "Operate" plainly reaches any system Mr. Bernecky might interact with in public — a self-checkout kiosk, a car equipped with OnStar or FordPass Connect, and countless other everyday devices — not just the private setting in which child pornography is actually accessed, in the typical case and in his own.

Rather than ask the Court to police that boundary case by case, Mr. Bernecky proposes striking "or operated" and "or used" from the condition's device-scope clauses, leaving "owned by the defendant" as the operative standard — the word the condition already uses elsewhere without difficulty. In exchange, he proposes a targeted provision addressing the risk an ownership-only standard might otherwise leave open. A prohibition keyed to "capable of" accessing child pornography alone would still sweep in a library terminal, a self-checkout kiosk, or even the PACER terminal at the courthouse — all technically capable of displaying an illegal image if someone misused them that way. The line that actually separates a real circumvention risk from ordinary public infrastructure is personal, private possession, not momentary public use: "With the exception of employer devices necessary for work performance, the defendant shall not personally and privately possess any smart cell phone, laptop, desktop computer, tablet, or other device capable of connecting to the internet and displaying images of child pornography." That formulation reaches what "operated" and "used" were likely meant to reach in the first place — a borrowed device standing in for one Mr. Bernecky does not himself own — without also reaching a library computer, a self-checkout kiosk, an ATM, or a courthouse terminal. Employer systems, addressed in Part I.A above, remain unaffected.

**Requested relief as to Part I.C:** an order striking "or operated" and "or used" from Special Condition (a)'s device-scope clauses, leaving "owned by the defendant" as the operative standard, together with the following provision: "With the exception of employer devices necessary for work performance, the defendant shall not personally and privately possess any smart cell phone, laptop, desktop computer, tablet, or other device capable of connecting to the internet and displaying images of child pornography."

**D. The CIMP Participant Agreement governs Mr. Bernecky's day-to-day computer and internet use, and five of its provisions exceed what Special Condition (a) authorizes.**

At the start of his supervision, Mr. Bernecky was presented with the District's Computer Monitoring Program Participant Agreement, attached as Exhibit A. He is complying with it in full. He nonetheless objects to several of its terms as applied to him, and a small number of additional issues have arisen in the course of applying them that require the Court's attention.

Mr. Bernecky owns two devices that qualify as "internet capable" under Exhibit A's own terms: a Roku television and an Android smart cell phone. Ex. A, Item 2; Certification of Jeffrey Bernecky ¶ *. He purchased the television before his supervision began and reported it to Probation at the outset. Certification ¶ *. He purchased that particular television, in relevant part, because he understood browser-less smart televisions to fall outside CIMP's device-authorization requirement. [FACT PENDING — client email sent: the specific basis for that understanding]. According to Mr. Bernecky, he told his probation officer that the Roku does not support general web browsing, and Probation imposed a parental lock on it regardless, preventing him from installing programs or applications on the device without Probation's separate clearance. Certification ¶ *. That lock does not even reach the one path by which outside content actually gets onto the television: the Roku's casting function lets another device on the same network send its own content to it regardless of whether the Roku itself is locked. Certification ¶ *. As for the phone, Mr. Bernecky uses a [FACT PENDING — client email sent: make and model] smart cell phone, with service through US Mobile. Certification ¶ *. Consistent with Exhibit A's own instructions, he notified his probation officer of the phone and separately contacted the CIMP vendor, ICCP, to arrange installation of the required monitoring software. [FACT PENDING — client email sent: the details of the ICCP installation, including any PIN-code or enrollment steps]. That phone — his own personal device, on his own carrier plan — now displays, on its lock screen, the message "This device belongs to your organization." Certification ¶ *. That is not custom text a vendor wrote to assert a property claim; it is text the Android operating system displays automatically whenever an app has been given Device Owner status, the highest level of control Android allows any program to have over a device, and the level Google's own documentation reserves for equipment a company owns and issues to its employees. Google, *Full Device Management*, Android Enterprise Help, https://support.google.com/work/android/answer/9562029 (accessed Aug. 11, 2026).

Since supervision began, what actually governs Mr. Bernecky's day-to-day computer and internet use is not Special Condition (a)'s text standing alone. It is Exhibit A itself — a twenty-one-item form used across this District's CIMP caseload, the same form the Second Circuit construed on appeal from this same district in *United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748 (2d Cir. 2023). This Part is necessarily an as-applied challenge, not a facial one: Exhibit A did not exist, and was never placed before, Judge Larimer at Mr. Bernecky's June 12, 2018 sentencing. Judgment (Dkt. 28). It is a document Probation itself drafted and required Mr. Bernecky to sign at intake, eight years later, and no part of it appears anywhere in the record the sentencing court actually reviewed. Parts I.A through I.C above challenge Special Condition (a)'s own text; this Part challenges how Probation has implemented that text through a form of its own making, one the sentencing court had no occasion to review.

*Kunz* supplies the framework for evaluating it. Where a CIMP-form provision is ambiguous, or would demand something absurd read with total literalism, the court construes it narrowly rather than striking it. *See id.* at 762-66 (encryption and alteration-notice clauses). But where a CIMP-form provision cannot be reconciled with the special condition's own text at all, the court holds that Probation "exceed[ed] the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court," and that Probation "may not enforce" the offending provision. *Id.* at 767-68 (single-device restriction). *United States v. Browder* states the same rule in blunter terms: a Probation-drafted provision that "would contradict, rather than execute" the sentence actually pronounced is one "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose." 866 F.3d 504, 514 (2d Cir. 2017). Five provisions of Exhibit A call for the Court's attention under that framework, addressed below in the order that reflects how far each strays from what Special Condition (a) and Special Condition (f) actually authorize: Item 21's seizure authority, which fails the framework outright; Item 3's device-authorization gate, which fails it the same way *Kunz* has already held a CIMP clause unenforceable once in this identical program; Items 9 and 17's encryption bans, which *Kunz* has already construed word for word, and which this motion asks the Court simply to apply; Item 16's blanket security-software ban, an overbreadth problem *Kunz*'s construction never reached; and Item 5's categorical ban on private browsing, an independent *Browder* problem *Kunz* never addressed.

Item 21 of Exhibit A provides:

> "I shall permit the seizure and removal of hardware and data storage media for further analysis by law enforcement or the probation officer based upon reasonable suspicion that a violation of a condition of supervision or unlawful conduct by any person has or is about to occur."

Ex. A, Item 21. Special Condition (a) authorizes something narrower in three respects, and Item 21 exceeds each of them.

The actor is the first respect. Special Condition (a) authorizes only "the U.S. Probation Office" to install monitoring applications and conduct examinations of Mr. Bernecky's devices. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. Special Condition (f), the Judgment's general search condition, names no actor at all — it authorizes "a search of [Mr. Bernecky's] person, property, vehicle, place of residence or any other property under his control" without saying who may conduct it — but it, too, reaches only Mr. Bernecky's own property, never a third party's. *Id.* Item 21 adds an actor neither condition contains: "law enforcement," empowered to seize hardware on its own authority, independent of Probation. Congress made a broader model of exactly that authority available in 2018. 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(23) is a ready-made discretionary condition authorizing search of a defendant's "computer, other electronic communication or data storage devices or media" "by any law enforcement or probation officer with reasonable suspicion concerning a violation of a condition of probation or unlawful conduct by the person." The sentencing court did not select that language; it wrote Special Condition (a) and Special Condition (f) instead, limited to the U.S. Probation Office and to Mr. Bernecky's own property. A form Probation drafted for its own administrative use cannot import, on its own initiative, statutory language Congress made available and the sentencing court did not choose.

Whose conduct triggers the seizure is the second respect. Special Condition (a) ties its examination trigger to "impermissible/suspicious activity" on "computer equipment owned or used by the defendant" — Mr. Bernecky's own activity, on his own equipment. Item 21 substitutes "unlawful conduct by any person," so that a roommate's, a visitor's, or a family member's suspected and wholly unrelated crime becomes Probation's basis for seizing Mr. Bernecky's own hardware. Even the broadest model available to the sentencing court ties its trigger to "the person" — the defendant, not any person who happens to be present. 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(23). The Sentencing Guidelines' own recommended search condition confirms the same limit, authorizing a search "upon reasonable suspicion concerning a violation of a condition of supervised release or unlawful conduct by the defendant." U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(7), quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 758-59. Every version of this authority Congress or the Sentencing Commission has made available ties the trigger to the defendant's own conduct; Item 21's "any person" trigger appears in no statute, no guideline, and no provision of this Judgment.

Timing is the third respect. Special Condition (a) speaks of impermissible or suspicious activity "occurring" — something happening, or already having happened — and Special Condition (f) requires "reasonable suspicion," a standard that itself presupposes some conduct has already left a trace to be suspicious of. Item 21 adds a trigger neither condition contains: seizure where a violation or unlawful conduct "is about to occur," converting a monitoring and after-the-fact search tool into a predictive one. Nothing in Special Condition (a) or Special Condition (f) mentions anticipated conduct. (*Kunz*'s panel never reached whether the Fourth Amendment tolerates an anticipatory, any-person trigger like Item 21's, since counsel there disavowed that challenge at oral argument under *United States v. Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d 173 (2d Cir. 2004); 68 F.4th at 754 n.1. Mr. Bernecky relies on *Kunz* here only for what it actually held — that a CIMP-form provision exceeding the sentencing court's own special-condition text will not be enforced.)

Items 2 and 3 of Exhibit A are best read together, since it is the contrast between them that exposes the actual problem. Item 2 provides:

> "I shall notify the U.S. Probation Office, in advance, of any computer(s), connected device(s), internet capable devices and/or gaming devices I own or have access to. I shall notify the probation officer, in advance, of any additional computer/connected device(s) I acquire or have access to during the course of my supervision."

Ex. A, Item 2. Special Condition (a) itself requires exactly this — advance notification "of any computer(s), automated service(s), or connected device(s) that will be used during the term of supervision" — and Item 2 executes that language faithfully. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. Mr. Bernecky has no quarrel with it: a notice requirement gives Probation complete visibility into every device he owns or can access, without gating what he may own or use in the first place. Item 3 does something different:

> "I shall not use, own, or access any device which allows internet access other than what I have been authorized to use by the Probation Office. This includes, but is not limited to, internet appliances, mobile telephones, tablets, electronic games, gaming consoles, blu ray players, and SMART TV's."

Ex. A, Item 3. It forbids Mr. Bernecky from using, owning, or even accessing any internet-capable device unless Probation has separately authorized that specific device, with no textual standard for what earns authorization and no limit on what Probation may withhold it for. Special Condition (a) authorizes Probation to install monitoring software on, and examine, devices Mr. Bernecky already owns or uses; it says nothing about Probation deciding, in the first instance and by no stated standard, what he may acquire at all.

The list Item 3 uses to describe what needs authorizing shares the same defect. "Internet appliances, mobile telephones, tablets, electronic games, gaming consoles, blu ray players, and SMART TV's" are named together as a single category, sorted by product label rather than by anything the devices actually do. A modern tablet or mobile telephone typically ships with a full, general-purpose web browser; a blu ray player's function is reading an optical disc; and, as Part I.C above explains, a modern smart television routinely connects to the internet to deliver services like Netflix and Apple TV, often without any web browser at all. Roku confirms this is not an assumption for at least one major platform: its own support documentation states that its devices "provide access to stream video and music from the internet" but that the platform "does not provide the ability to browse the internet on your television." Roku, *Does My Roku Streaming Device Have an Internet Web Browser?*, Roku Support, https://support.roku.com/article/can-i-browse-the-internet (accessed Aug. 11, 2026). Item 3's list gates all of these devices alike, by category, without distinguishing which of these labels actually describes a browsing risk.

*Kunz* has already struck down functionally the same defect in this identical program. It confronted a CIMP clause under which Probation could have limited the defendant there to a single internet-capable device, and held that the clause "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court" because the sentencing court's own special-condition text said nothing supporting a Probation-controlled device restriction. 68 F.4th at 767-68. A restriction of that kind, the panel explained, "would pose a significant burden on [a supervisee's] liberty, and therefore would need to be imposed by the court and justified by particularized on-the-record findings," not left to "any special condition granting Probation discretion to decide" the question itself. *Id.* Item 3 is that same vice in a more sweeping form: instead of capping Mr. Bernecky at one device, it lets Probation decide, device by device and by no stated standard, whether he may have any device at all. If a single-device cap requires "an express, case-specific rationale" from the sentencing court, *id.*, a blanket authorization requirement covering every device Mr. Bernecky might ever want to use requires no less — and no such rationale appears anywhere in this record.

Two facts already established above show what that undisclosed discretion looks like in practice: the Device Owner status now running on Mr. Bernecky's phone, and the parental lock Probation placed on his Roku. Special Condition (a) authorizes technology that can "surveil . . . activity" already occurring on a device — technology that watches and reports. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. What is running on Mr. Bernecky's phone does more than that: Device Owner status, as already described, can remotely erase the entire device, block its own removal, and dictate what other software may be installed on it at all, reaching the device's own contents rather than merely observing them. Surveillance is passive observation; Device Owner status is active control, including the power to alter what is on the device. Mr. Bernecky raises this fact for a narrow, well-supported point: even where Probation does grant "authorization" under Item 3, the method chosen to implement that authorization has already gone further than surveillance requires, and the phone's own operating system is the evidence.

The Roku shows the same undisclosed discretion from the opposite direction — not access expanded beyond what surveillance requires, as with the phone, but a device locked down for a risk it does not present. Under Item 3's own list, a Roku television falls within "SMART TV's" and is gated the same way as a laptop or a smartphone, even though it lacks the one capability — reaching the open internet through a general browser — that would connect it to the risk CIMP exists to manage. And the parental lock already described above does not even accomplish its evident purpose on this device: because the casting function lets another device on the same network send its own content to the television regardless of whether the Roku itself is locked, whatever monitoring interest that cast content calls for is already addressed at the level of the device generating it, not by a lock on a television that was never capable of generating that content in the first place.

Mr. Bernecky is not asking the Court to leave Probation without any tool to police which devices present a genuine monitoring risk. What Part I.C above proposes for a related question — whose devices Special Condition (a)'s monitoring and examination authority reaches at all, once "operated" and "used" are struck — this section proposes for a narrower one: which of Mr. Bernecky's own devices need Probation's pre-clearance before he may use them. Pre-authorization should attach only to a device that has (i) general-purpose web-browsing capability, or (ii) an application-installation mechanism through which an ordinary user could add general web-browsing, anonymization, or peer-to-peer file-sharing capability, and that (iii) is compatible with whatever CIMP monitoring software Probation actually uses. The Roku is the case this standard is built to resolve: a device that connects to the internet but offers no browsing or file-transfer path of its own is not, on this test, a device Item 3 could reasonably gate. Whatever device-management privilege Probation's vendor uses to enroll an authorized device should likewise be no broader than what surveilling that device's activity actually requires — not the organization-ownership tier Android reserves for hardware a company, not an individual supervisee, owns.

In exchange for that narrower gate, Mr. Bernecky offers Probation suspicion-free physical access, which it does not currently have under the Judgment's own text. Special Condition (a) already conditions physical examination of Mr. Bernecky's devices on a trigger — "as triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity" — so Probation's authority to inspect a device, as the Judgment is actually written, depends on some suspicion already having arisen. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. Mr. Bernecky consents to physical inspection of any of his personal devices, on Probation's request, at any time, without any requirement of prior suspicion — a more searching term than Special Condition (a) itself imposes, offered to make the narrower device-authorization gate an easy exchange rather than a net loss to Probation's actual monitoring interest. That consent is necessarily limited to devices within Mr. Bernecky's own legal authority to consent to; it does not extend to a device separately owned by, or under the sole or joint control of, a third party who has not himself consented. *See United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974) (third-party consent to a search is valid only where the consenting party possesses "common authority over or other sufficient relationship to the premises or effects sought to be inspected").

The parsimony clause calls for this exchange rather than a categorical device rule. 18 U.S.C. § 3583(d)(2) requires that any condition impose "no greater deprivation of liberty than is reasonably necessary," and *Kunz* has already recognized what an internet-capable device means to an ordinary life today: such devices are "nearly essential" to modern life. 68 F.4th at 773 (citing *United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d at 96 (2d Cir. 2019)). A rule that lets Probation withhold authorization from any device, on no stated standard, and enroll whatever device it does authorize under a management tier built for hardware someone else owns, is not the narrowly tailored gate § 3583(d)(2) requires. A rule tied to the function that actually matters, paired with Mr. Bernecky's offer of suspicionless physical access, is.

Items 9 and 17 of Exhibit A present a related but distinct problem:

> Item 9: "I understand that I will not use any encrypted email accounts."
> Item 17: "I shall not use any type of encryption unless pre-approved by my probation officer."

Ex. A, Items 9, 17. This is not new territory. *Kunz* construed its own CIMP paragraph 18 — word for word the same text as Item 17 — and its own paragraph 10 — word for word identical to Item 9. 68 F.4th at 757. Faced with an argument that a literal reading of paragraph 18 would require pre-approval before visiting any ordinary "https" website, the panel agreed a literal reading would be absurd but did not strike the clause. It construed the restriction narrowly and affirmed the condition as construed: "so long as no encryption is initiated by or at the behest of [the supervisee], and so long as any encryption initiated by external forces does not meaningfully impair Probation's ability to monitor [his] behavior . . . we construe the encryption restrictions to allow the reasonable use of services and resources that happen to employ encryption in some way." *Id.* at 764. The panel's textual hook, applied across each clause it construed this way, personalizes the obligation to what the supervisee himself will or will not actively do or "use," rather than to a process "entirely outside of his control and of which he himself has no advance notice." *Id.* at 762-64.

Item 9 uses the identical operative verb: Mr. Bernecky "will not use any encrypted email accounts." A court applying *Kunz*'s own reasoning to that identical construction would very likely read it the same way it read paragraph 18 — barring Mr. Bernecky from himself affirmatively choosing an end-to-end-encrypted service to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, not from holding an ordinary Gmail, Outlook, or comparable account that the provider itself encrypts in transit and at rest, invisibly to him, by default. Mr. Bernecky does not ask this Court to declare Item 9 impossible to comply with and therefore void. That framing would ask the Court to accept the maximalist literal reading *Kunz*'s own panel already rejected for the sibling clause. The relief requested instead is a written order construing Items 9 and 17 the way *Kunz* construed paragraph 18: reaching only encryption Mr. Bernecky himself affirmatively initiates to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, and excluding the ordinary, provider-side transport and at-rest encryption built into mainstream commercial email and web services. Candor requires noting that *Kunz*'s narrowing discussion never mentions paragraph 10 by name, addressing only paragraphs 7, 16, and 18, *id.* at 762-66; the argument that the same construction governs Item 9 is an extension by direct analogy, not an on-point holding, and Mr. Bernecky presents it as exactly that.

Item 9 also has a gap Item 17 does not. Item 17 already contains its own escape valve — "unless pre-approved by my probation officer" — and Item 9 has none. Even under the narrowed reading requested here, Item 9 as written gives Mr. Bernecky no way ever to seek approval for a legitimate use of an encrypted email service — for instance, if a future employer's IT department required PGP-signed correspondence, or if counsel or the Court itself communicated through an encrypted portal. Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to add the same pre-approval mechanism Item 17 already has to Item 9.

Item 16 presents a problem *Kunz* never had occasion to construe:

> "I shall not purchase, download, possess and/or install any anti-virus, anti-spyware, firewall, or internet security application without advanced authorization by the U.S. Probation Office and my probation officer."

Ex. A, Item 16. *Kunz*'s narrowing-construction discussion addresses paragraphs 7, 16, and 18 of its own CIMP form — the notice-before-alteration clause, the "ALL repairs" clause, and the encryption clause. 68 F.4th at 762-66. It does not address paragraph 17, the security-software clause that is Item 16 here, so Item 16 stands on its own.

Read literally, Item 16 forbids something Mr. Bernecky cannot avoid. Every modern operating system ships with its own antivirus and firewall software already installed and active by default — Windows Defender on Windows, a comparable built-in firewall on macOS — and a user does not "purchase, download, possess and/or install" what the manufacturer installed before the device ever reached him. Taken at its word, Item 16 would require Mr. Bernecky to seek Probation's advance authorization merely to leave his own operating system's factory-default protections running, or else disable them to comply — leaving the very device Probation is monitoring more vulnerable to compromise. Nothing in Special Condition (a) mentions security software at all; its closest textual hook is the anti-tampering concern for the monitoring software specifically, a concern that runs in the opposite direction from a rule that discourages ordinary computer hygiene.

The problem extends beyond what already runs by default. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency — an arm of the same federal government responsible for Mr. Bernecky's supervision — advises that "[a] reputable antivirus software application is an important protective measure against known malicious threats," that it "can automatically detect, quarantine, and remove various types of malware, such as viruses, worms, and ransomware," and that "CISA recommends that all computers and mobile devices on your home network run antivirus software," the same guidance that separately tells users to "[i]nstall a network firewall" and "[i]nstall firewalls on network devices." CISA, *Home Network Security*, https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/home-network-security (accessed Aug. 11, 2026). Item 16 requires Mr. Bernecky to obtain Probation's advance authorization before doing what one arm of the federal government instructs every American, unconditionally, to do to protect himself.

The relief requested does not eliminate Probation's legitimate interest in knowing what security software runs on Mr. Bernecky's monitored devices. It narrows Item 16 to what that interest actually requires: notice to Probation, not its prior authorization, before installing or running any antivirus, anti-spyware, firewall, or other internet security software — the same notice-not-permission line requested above for Item 3's device-authorization gate. Whether the software in question came pre-installed or is something Mr. Bernecky adds himself, Probation's interest is satisfied by knowing it is there.

Item 5 presents a final, independent problem: "While using my monitored device, I shall not use any form of 'in-private' browsing at any time." Ex. A, Item 5. This clause does not appear among the provisions *Kunz* addressed; it stands on *Browder* alone. Special Condition (a) authorizes Probation "to install any application as necessary to surveil all activity on computer(s) or connected device(s)" — meaning the monitoring software Probation actually deploys is premised on capturing activity regardless of browser mode. Judgment (Dkt. 28), Sheet 3B. If that premise holds, Item 5 adds nothing the monitoring itself does not already accomplish, and it is surplusage. If it does not hold, that is a fact about the adequacy of the monitoring method Probation has chosen — addressed in Part I.B above — not a basis for a separate, categorical ban on a mode of browsing that Special Condition (a) never mentions. Either way, Item 5 does independent work the Judgment never authorized, *Browder*'s "contradict, rather than execute" problem in its cleanest form. 866 F.3d at 514.

A narrower, related concern warrants clarification alongside the broader request. Mr. Bernecky uses DuckDuckGo for ordinary browsing. Certification ¶ *. DuckDuckGo's own description of its protections is limited to blocking third-party trackers and cookies from following activity across the open web; its stated purpose is to "stop most 3rd-party trackers from loading in the first place." DuckDuckGo, *Web Tracking Protections*, DuckDuckGo Help Pages, https://duckduckgo.com/duckduckgo-help-pages/privacy/web-tracking-protections (accessed Aug. 11, 2026). Nothing in that description claims, or was designed, to hide anything from software running locally on the device itself, and DuckDuckGo's tracker-blocking is always on by default rather than a mode a user switches into or out of the way Item 5's "in-private browsing" describes. Google's own documentation of a genuine private-browsing mode makes the contrast concrete: even an actual Incognito session does not defeat monitoring by whoever controls the device or network it runs on — "organizations that manage your network, like your school, employer, or internet service provider, may be able to observe your activity in Incognito." Google, *Browse in Incognito Mode*, Chrome Help, https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/95464 (accessed Aug. 11, 2026). If a genuine Incognito session cannot defeat monitoring by whoever manages the device, ordinary use of a search engine that only blocks third-party advertising trackers cannot either. Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to confirm that Item 5 reaches only browser-native private or incognito session modes, not ordinary use of a privacy-respecting search engine or browser that hides nothing from the monitoring software already installed on his device.

**Requested relief as to Part I.D:** an order (1) striking "by law enforcement" from Item 21, leaving "the probation officer"; striking "or unlawful conduct by any person," limiting Item 21's trigger to Mr. Bernecky's own conduct; and striking "or is about to occur," limiting Item 21's trigger to a violation or unlawful conduct that has actually occurred; (2) replacing Item 3 with a provision requiring Probation's advance authorization only for a device that has general-purpose web-browsing capability, or an application-installation mechanism through which an ordinary user could add general web-browsing, anonymization, or peer-to-peer file-sharing capability, and that is compatible with the CIMP monitoring software Probation actually uses — coupled with Mr. Bernecky's consent to physical inspection, without prior suspicion, of any device within his own legal authority to consent to, and a limit confining any device-management privilege Probation's vendor uses to enroll an authorized device to no more than what surveilling that device's activity requires; (3) an order construing Items 9 and 17 to reach only encryption Mr. Bernecky himself affirmatively initiates to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, excluding ordinary provider-side transport and at-rest encryption, together with an order adding to Item 9 the same pre-approval mechanism Item 17 already contains; (4) an order narrowing Item 16 to require notice to Probation, not its prior authorization, before installing or running any antivirus, anti-spyware, firewall, or other internet security software, regardless of whether that software came factory-installed on the device or was added by Mr. Bernecky himself; and (5) an order confirming that Item 5 reaches only browser-native private or incognito browsing modes, not ordinary use of a privacy-respecting search engine or browser that does not defeat device-level monitoring.
