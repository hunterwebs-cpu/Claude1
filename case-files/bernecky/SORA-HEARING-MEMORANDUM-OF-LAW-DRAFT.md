## PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

Jeffrey Bernecky has one type of offense in his history: possessing, and in 2012 distributing, child pornography over the internet. No court has ever convicted him of touching a child, and the Board of Examiners of Sex Offenders' own Risk Assessment Instrument confirms it. Of the fifteen factors the RAI scores, twelve are zero — every factor that measures forcible compulsion, physical injury, sexual contact, number of victims, and relationship to a victim. Respondent's raw score is 70. On the RAI's own printed scale, 70 sits inside "Level 1 (low)." The Board's own Case Summary says so in as many words: "Mr. Bernecky is assessed as a Level I (Low) risk."

Level One is not what the Board recommends. A single automatic override — the bare existence of Respondent's 2012 conviction, itself a non-contact, digital offense — converts that Level One assessment into a presumptive Level Three, with no other finding of any kind. Respondent asks this Court for a downward departure to Level Two, the classification his own record actually supports.

Three grounds compel that result. First, controlling New York authority recognizes that mechanically applying a scoring instrument built around contact offenders to a defendant like Respondent risks "an excessive level of registration." People v. Gillotti, 23 N.Y.3d 841, 860 (2014). The Fourth Department — this Department — has granted exactly this kind of departure to a child-pornography-possession defendant since Gillotti was decided. People v. Stagles, 222 A.D.3d 1341 (4th Dep't 2023).

Second, the record the Board built this recommendation from does not hold up. The Case Summary attributes to Respondent an admission he did not make in those words; it recites a file count off by a factor of ten from the government's own contemporaneous filing; and it relies on presentence-report paragraphs that Respondent's own assigned counsel formally objected to in writing — objections this record does not show were ever resolved. A federal presentence report, the Second Circuit explained more than forty years ago, is built from "hearsay statements that are not subject to cross-examination," is exempt entirely from the Federal Rules of Evidence, and can contain real "misinformation" even after a defense challenge to it succeeds. United States v. Charmer Industries, Inc., 711 F.2d 1164, 1171, 1176 (2d Cir. 1983). That is the document the Board asks this Court to treat as clear and convincing proof.

Third, and most fundamentally, Correction Law § 168-l(6) requires two separate findings before a Level Three designation may issue: that "the risk of repeat offense is high," and that "there exists a threat to the public safety." Respondent does not dispute the first. He disputes that the second has ever been shown. Override 1 supplies nothing but the fact of a prior conviction — no individualized finding of present dangerousness at all — and the closest analogous New York civil-dangerousness proceeding, Mental Hygiene Law Article 10, forbids exactly that inference: a factfinder there "may not find solely on the basis of the respondent's commission of a sex offense" that a present mental abnormality exists. MHL § 10.07(d). The Board's Case Summary does precisely what Article 10 forbids, in one sentence, when it calls the prior conviction "compelling evidence that he poses a serious risk to public safety."

For these reasons, developed fully below, Respondent respectfully requests a downward departure to Risk Level Two.

## ARGUMENT

### POINT I

### A NON-CONTACT OFFENDER WHOSE OWN SCORE FALLS WITHIN LEVEL ONE IS ENTITLED TO A DOWNWARD DEPARTURE

#### A. The Governing Standard

A SORA respondent seeking a downward departure — including departure from an automatic override — must show, first, a mitigating factor "of a kind, or to a degree, that is otherwise not adequately taken into account by the Guidelines," and second, the facts supporting that factor by a preponderance of the evidence. Gillotti, 23 N.Y.3d at 861. The Court of Appeals resolved a prior split among the Appellate Division departments to reach that standard, holding "a defendant must prove the existence of the mitigating circumstances upon which he or she relies in advocating for a departure by a mere preponderance of the evidence." Id. at 863-64. Only after both showings are made does a court reach a third, discretionary step: weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances under the totality of the record. Id. at 861. This is Respondent's burden here, and it is a modest one — the ordinary civil standard, not the heightened clear-and-convincing standard the People bear on the classification itself under Correction Law § 168-n(3).

#### B. A Digital-Only Offense Does Not Establish the Physical Danger SORA's Classification Scheme Is Built to Address

The Court of Appeals has recognized, specifically as to child-pornography offenders, that mechanical application of the Guidelines' scoring model risks "an excessive risk calculation," and that "in deciding a child pornography offender's application for a downward departure, a SORA court should, in the exercise of its discretion, give particularly strong consideration to the possibility that adjudicating the offender in accordance with the guidelines point score and without departing downward might lead to an excessive level of registration." Gillotti, 23 N.Y.3d at 860. The Court also recognized real scientific uncertainty on the very premise a Level Three classification assumes: "the relationship between an offender's child pornography offenses and the likelihood he or she will commit hands-on offenses is uncertain due to the continuing development of scientific research." Id. at 859. The record before the Court in Gillotti itself included expert testimony that child-pornography users who have never committed a hands-on offense have "only a 4% rate of committing such hands-on offenses in the future," a rate the expert described as "considerably lower" than the rate for other offender types. Id. at [pincite to be confirmed].

Although United States v. Falso, 544 F.3d 110 (2d Cir. 2008), concerned the validity of a search warrant, the inferential fallacy Justice Sotomayor identified there is applicable across legal issues: "It is an inferential fallacy of ancient standing to conclude that, because members of group A ... are likely to be members of group B ... then group B is entirely, or even largely composed of, members of group A." Falso, 544 F.3d at [pincite to be confirmed]. A scoring instrument that treats every child-pornography offender as though he belongs to the class of contact offenders commits exactly that fallacy, and Gillotti's own "excessive registration" language is the doctrinal answer to it in SORA's own vocabulary.

The Fourth Department has already granted this kind of departure, on this kind of record, since Gillotti was decided. In People v. Stagles, 222 A.D.3d 1341 (4th Dep't 2023), this Court's own Department reduced a child-pornography-possession offender from a presumptive Level Two to Level One, holding that the Factor 3/7 point assessment "overestimated his risk of re-offense and danger to the public," quoting Gillotti, 23 N.Y.3d at 860. Stagles was 19, had no prior record, was never accused of contact conduct, never shared his images, and received a probationary sentence — facts meaningfully more favorable than Respondent's on several dimensions, and Respondent does not pretend otherwise. But the legal principle Stagles applies does not depend on those particular facts: a scoring instrument calibrated to contact-offense risk overstates the risk a non-contact, image-based offender actually presents, and a court in this Department has acted on that principle, not merely discussed it. People v. Adams, 216 A.D.3d 1376 (3d Dep't 2023), reached the identical result on a different record — a departure from a presumptive Level Three to Level Two, crediting the absence of physical contact, the absence of any paid subscription, a comparatively low image volume, and "exceptional" treatment participation. Respondent's own record shares every one of those favorable features except image volume, which the government's own 2012 filing places at 6,200 files — a figure the Case Summary reports as 62,000, a discrepancy discussed above and one this Court should resolve, if at all, in Respondent's favor given the absence of any PSR in this record to adjudicate it.

Respondent's own RAI is Board-created, contemporaneous, documentary evidence of exactly this point: every factor measuring contact-offense conduct — violence, forcible compulsion, physical injury, sexual contact, number of victims, victim relationship — scores zero. That is not argument. It is the Board's own instrument.

#### C. Gunter Does Not Foreclose This Record

The People will likely cite People v. Gunter, 217 A.D.3d 788 (2d Dep't 2023), for the proposition that a below-override point score cannot itself justify departure, because Override 1 "applies irrespective of the points scored on the risk assessment instrument." Id. at 790. That holding is correct, and Respondent does not ask this Court to depart merely because his raw score of 70 sits below even the Level Two threshold of 75. Gunter also rejected treatment-response and family-support showings that were, on that record, unsupported by any documentary evidence. Id. at 789-90. That is not this record. Respondent's treatment engagement is documented directly in the Board's own Case Summary — sustained participation at Endeavor from before the instant offense through phase-two treatment, a 2017 polygraph examination in which "no deception was indicated," and a counselor's own assessment that Respondent "took accountability for his actions and showed insight into his offending behavior." The distinction Gunter draws is between undocumented assertion and Board-documented fact. Respondent's showing is the latter.

#### D. Reoffense During Treatment Is a Relapse, Not an Escalation, and the Board's Case Summary Does Not Grapple With the Difference

The Case Summary states that "no departure is recommended as prior detection, sanction and treatment have not deterred his conduct." That sentence states a true fact plainly: Respondent committed the instant offense while under supervision and in active treatment following his 2012 conviction. Respondent does not minimize that fact. But the inference the Case Summary draws from it — that continued viewing of child pornography during treatment shows treatment has failed to reduce Respondent's dangerousness — does not follow without a premise the Case Summary never states and this record does not support: that a return to compulsive, non-contact viewing behavior is functionally equivalent to an escalation toward hands-on abuse. It is not. A relapse into compulsive consumption of pornography, without more, is a different category of conduct from an offender who moves from viewing images toward seeking physical access to a child — and this record contains no evidence of the latter at any point in Respondent's history. The instant offense, like the 2012 offense before it, is digital, possession-based, and non-contact. Gillotti's own acknowledgment that "the relationship between an offender's child pornography offenses and the likelihood he or she will commit hands-on offenses is uncertain," 23 N.Y.3d at 859, applies with particular force to a relapse that repeats the same non-contact conduct rather than escalating beyond it. The Board's Case Summary treats reoffense itself as dispositive of dangerousness without asking what kind of reoffense occurred. That is precisely the individualized inquiry Gillotti's "particularly strong consideration" language requires a SORA court to undertake before treating a point score — or, as here, an override — as the last word.

### POINT II

### THE BOARD'S CASE SUMMARY RESTS ON A PRESENTENCE REPORT THAT IS UNRELIABLE BY DESIGN, FILTERED THROUGH A SECOND LAYER OF PARAPHRASE THIS COURT HAS NEVER SEEN

#### A. What a Presentence Report Actually Is, in the Second Circuit's Own Words

Everything in the Board's Case Summary describing the instant and prior offenses traces back to one document neither this Court nor Respondent's own counsel has ever seen: the federal presentence report. The Second Circuit has described exactly what that kind of document is, and is not, in terms that control here. A presentence report "may well contain hearsay statements that are not subject to cross-examination ... or information bearing no relationship to the crime for which the defendant is to be sentenced," and the Federal Rules of Evidence do not apply to sentencing proceedings at all. United States v. Charmer Industries, Inc., 711 F.2d 1164, 1171 (2d Cir. 1983) (citing Fed. R. Evid. 1101(d)(3)). "No formal limitations inhibit the collection of information for such reports. The reports frequently contain hearsay and information not relevant to the crime charged." Id. at [pincite to be confirmed].

The unreliability is not merely theoretical, and it does not depend on a defendant's diligence in contesting it. "[T]here is no requirement that the defendant voice his objection to any statement with which he disagrees, and we assume that not all statements with which a defendant might take issue are actually challenged. Indeed, in a significant number of cases, the defendant does not even request disclosure of the presentence report." Id. at [pincite to be confirmed] (citing Fennell & Hall, Due Process at Sentencing: An Empirical and Legal Analysis of the Disclosure of Presentence Reports in Federal Courts, 93 Harv. L. Rev. 1615, 1641 n.141 (1980)). And even where a defendant does object, and the objection succeeds in the narrow sense that the sentencing court declines to rely on the challenged statement, the statement itself does not disappear: "even where the defendant's challenge to a presentence report statement has been 'successful,' in the sense that the district court has determined that it will not rely on the statement in imposing sentence, the statement nevertheless remains in the report ... although the safeguards provided by Rule 32(c) ensure that any material misinformation in the report that is called to the court's attention will not affect the sentencing decision, they do not mean that the presentence report contains no such misinformation." Id. at [pincite to be confirmed]. A court confronted with a challenged statement "may, in its discretion, decline to hold an evidentiary hearing with respect to the challenges," and even where a hearing is held, "the government may be permitted to rest simply on proof that the source of the probation officers' hearsay information is reliable and be excused from producing — or identifying — an informant who provided the disputed information." Id. at [pincite to be confirmed]. That is why a presentence report "may not properly be disclosed without authorization by the court," and why a third party seeking it must show "a compelling need for disclosure ... to meet the ends of justice." Id. at 1175-76.

Every one of these features is present in this record, not merely possible in the abstract. AFPD Hosken's objection to Paragraphs 79 through 81 was not required by anything in federal practice — Charmer confirms as much — and this record does not show it was ever resolved. Paragraph 70's restitution figure was specifically contested and this record does not show what became of that dispute either. The reoffense allegation, the file counts, and the two-incident polygraph account the Case Summary relies on all trace to a document built precisely the way Charmer describes: assembled without formal evidentiary limitation, exempt from the rules of evidence, and capable of containing real misinformation whether or not anyone objected to it.

#### B. A Federal PSR, Filtered Through the Board's Own Paraphrase, Is Not the Document New York's Reliable-Hearsay Rule Was Built For

Correction Law § 168-n(3) permits a SORA court to "consider reliable hearsay evidence submitted by either party." People v. Mingo, 12 N.Y.3d 563 (2009), construes "reliable" to mean that "a reasonable person would deem it trustworthy" given the circumstances of the proof's development. Mingo separately holds that "[n]o foundation is necessary" for case summaries and presentence reports specifically, "because such documents are created under statutory mandates and their origins and function are well-known to SORA courts." That rule was built for the document New York SORA courts see in case after case: an in-state PSR, prepared under CPL 390 by New York probation officers, for New York sentencing judges.

The document before this Court is not that document. It is the Board's own narrative characterization of a federal presentence report — a document prepared under an entirely different rule, Fed. R. Crim. P. 32, by federal probation officers, for a federal sentencing judge, and never itself examined by this Court, by the Board's own staff who cite it, or by Respondent's counsel in this proceeding. What stands between this Court and the underlying events is not one layer of hearsay but at least two: an investigative account, filtered through a federal probation officer's summary, filtered again through the Board's own further paraphrase — a paraphrase this record already shows to be imprecise, in at least the quotation and file-count discrepancies described above. Mingo's foundation-free rule rests on New York SORA courts' routine familiarity with how a New York PSR is actually built. Nothing in this record shows this Court, or any Monroe County SORA court, is equally familiar with how a federal PSR — filtered through this Board's own additional layer of characterization — comes to be.

Mingo itself supplies the mechanism for testing exactly this kind of challenged reliability: once a specific hearsay assertion is challenged, foundation may be established "either through a pre-hearing affirmation, an offer of proof, or by testimony from a witness with knowledge of the internal procedures." Respondent specifically challenges the following assertions in the Case Summary as lacking any such foundation, and respectfully requests that this Court require the People to establish foundation for each, by one of Mingo's three named methods, before giving any of them weight: the statement that Respondent "could not help what he was doing"; the 62,000-file figure; the two-incident, 2005/2007 account sourced to the 2017 polygraph interview; and the conclusory statement that the prior conviction "provides compelling evidence that he poses a serious risk to public safety that is not captured by scoring alone."

#### C. Correction Law § 168-n(3) Requires Independent Proof of Every Fact Not Actually Litigated at the Federal Proceeding — And Most of the Case Summary's Content Was Never Litigated at All

Correction Law § 168-n(3) states that "[f]acts previously proven at trial or elicited at the time of entry of a plea of guilty shall be deemed established by clear and convincing evidence and shall not be relitigated." That sentence marks the outer limit of what the Board may treat as automatically established. Respondent's guilty plea to the 2018 offense established the elements of that offense — possession of child pornography following a prior conviction. It did not "elicit" the file counts from the 2012 case, the precise wording of a 2010 admission, or the granular content of a 2017 polygraph pretest interview. None of that content was proven at any trial; none of it was elicited at any plea colloquy. It exists in this record only as the Board's paraphrase of a probation officer's paraphrase.

Nor does federal sentencing practice supply the missing proof. Disputed facts at federal sentencing are found, at most, by a preponderance of the evidence — not clear and convincing evidence. United States v. Vaughn, 430 F.3d 518 (2d Cir. 2005) (federal sentencing courts "remain statutorily obliged ... to find facts relevant to sentencing by a preponderance of the evidence"); United States v. Garcia, 413 F.3d 201 (2d Cir. 2005) (same). Undisputed facts fare no better; Rule 32(i)(3)(A) permits a sentencing court to accept an undisputed PSR statement "as a finding of fact" on nothing more than the defendant's silence — not because any evidentiary showing, at any standard, was ever made. A fact nobody was required to prove at all is not a fact proven by a preponderance of the evidence, and it is certainly not a fact proven by clear and convincing evidence. Whatever standard actually produced any particular sentence in the Case Summary, it was not New York's clear-and-convincing standard, and § 168-n(3)'s own text does not treat it as though it were. The remedy is not merely verifying that the Case Summary accurately restates the PSR — though Respondent has shown above that in at least two respects it does not. The remedy Correction Law § 168-n(3) itself supplies is that the People must independently prove, by clear and convincing evidence, at this hearing, any fact that was not actually proven at trial or elicited at the plea. Respondent respectfully requests that this Court hold the People to that standard as to every disputed fact identified in this memorandum, and demands, pursuant to § 168-n(3)'s own disclosure-and-subpoena mechanism, production of the underlying PSR pages, any Rule 32(i)(3)(C) determinations, and the June 1, 2012 Position Statement the Case Summary invokes.

#### D. This Is Not a Theoretical Risk. The Case Summary Has Already Been Shown to Be Wrong, Incomplete, or Unverifiable — Three Times, on This Record Alone

The Board's Case Summary is not hypothetically unreliable. It is demonstrably so, on this record, without resort to anything beyond documents already discussed above. It attributes to Respondent an admission of permanent incapacity — "could not help what he was doing" — that does not appear in those words anywhere in the primary record; the verbatim quotation available shows Respondent said "can't help it," a materially different statement the Board's own paraphrase erases. It states a file count of 62,000, a full order of magnitude higher than the 6,200 figure the government's own contemporaneous 2012 filing reports. And it recites, without qualification or attribution to a specific PSR paragraph, a two-incident account that appears to trace to presentence-report paragraphs Respondent's own counsel formally objected to and that this record does not show were ever resolved. It also omits, entirely, a treating clinician's contemporaneous opinion that Respondent's risk for a contact offense was low, and Respondent's own disclosed history of childhood sexual abuse. A document that has already been shown, on the limited record available to Respondent's own counsel, to misstate a quotation, misstate a figure by a factor of ten, rely on unresolved and contested paragraphs, and omit favorable evidence is not a document this Court can treat as clear and convincing proof of anything it asserts without independent verification.

### POINT III

### OVERRIDE 1 SUPPLIES NO PROOF OF THE INDEPENDENT "THREAT TO PUBLIC SAFETY" ELEMENT SECTION 168-l(6) REQUIRES

#### A. The Statute Is Conjunctive, and Respondent Concedes Only Half of It

Correction Law § 168-l(6) authorizes a Level Three designation only "[i]f the risk of repeat offense is high and there exists a threat to the public safety." Section 168-l(5) directs the Board to develop guidelines assessing both "the risk of a repeat offense" and, separately, "the threat posed to the public safety." Two elements, joined by "and," not one.

Respondent does not dispute that he has a prior felony conviction for a sex crime, and he does not ask this Court to invalidate Override 1 as improperly triggered. That concession is deliberate, and it sharpens rather than weakens what follows: the entire live dispute in this proceeding is the statute's second, independent clause. Override 1's own text asks only whether "[t]he offender has a prior felony conviction for a sex crime" — nothing more. Once that fact is shown, People v. Gunter holds the override applies automatically, "irrespective of the points scored," with no discretion left to this Court. 217 A.D.3d at 789-90. But automatic application of the override answers only the "risk of repeat offense" clause. Nothing in the Guidelines, in the RAI's own text, or in the Case Summary's own reasoning supplies any separate finding on whether Respondent presently poses "a threat to the public safety." The Case Summary's own words collapse the two: the prior conviction, it says, "provides compelling evidence that he poses a serious risk to public safety that is not captured by scoring alone." That sentence treats the bare fact of a ten-year-old conviction as self-proving present dangerousness. Section 168-l(6)'s plain, conjunctive text does not permit that shortcut.

Respondent's own record makes the gap concrete, not abstract. The same 2012 conviction now triggering Override 1 was already litigated, on the merits, in a prior SORA proceeding — and that proceeding produced a Level Two classification, not Level Three. People v. Bernecky, 161 A.D.3d 1540 (4th Dep't 2018), lv denied, 32 N.Y.3d 901 (2018). The Fourth Department affirmed that Level Two classification and affirmed denial of further departure to Level One, expressly relying on the fact that Respondent "possessed an extraordinary number of pornographic images, including depictions of sexual acts involving children, violence, and bestiality." Id. at 1541. A court, after full adversarial process, on this exact conduct, found only a moderate risk. Override 1 now recodes that same conduct as automatically establishing both a high risk of repeat offense and a threat to public safety, with no new individualized finding bridging the gap. The obvious response — that the instant, second offense is itself new evidence of increased dangerousness — is fair, and Respondent addresses it directly: recency and repetition of digital, non-contact conduct may bear on the first clause, the risk of repeat offense. It does not, without more, supply the second, independent finding the statute separately requires.

#### B. The Override Mechanism Itself Is a Guidelines Creation That Displaces, Rather Than Implements, the Statute's Own Individualized Standard

The New York Constitution's separation-of-powers principle limits how far an agency may go in filling gaps a statute leaves open. Boreali v. Axelrod, 71 N.Y.2d 1 (1987), identifies the line: an agency exceeds its delegated authority when it stops "fill[ing] in the details of broad legislation" and instead "writ[es] on a clean slate, creating its own comprehensive set of rules without benefit of legislative guidance." Id. at 13. Override 1 does exactly that as to § 168-l(6)'s public-safety clause. The statute directs the Board to assess "the threat posed to the public safety" as an independent inquiry. Override 1 does not fill in how to make that assessment — it substitutes a single categorical fact for the assessment entirely, with zero discretion left to a SORA court once the predicate conviction is shown. Gunter, 217 A.D.3d at 789. Nor does the override reflect any exercise of the Board's own "subject matter expertise," the deference New York courts otherwise extend to the Board's guideline-making. See Moss, 43 N.Y.3d at 588. Override 1 applies no clinical or actuarial judgment at all; it is a records check for a qualifying conviction, in real contrast to the RAI's individually scored factors, which at least purport to embody a forensic risk assessment.

This is a matter of first impression in New York, and Respondent preserves it squarely, for this Court and for any appeal. No reported New York decision has presented or decided whether Override 1, standing alone, can substitute for § 168-l(6)'s independent public-safety finding. That silence cuts both ways — no adverse authority forecloses the argument, and none endorses it — and Respondent raises it candidly as exactly that: an unresolved question this record squarely presents.

#### C. New York's Own Closest Analogous Proceeding Forbids the Inference the Board Draws Here — And Arms the Respondent to Contest It

Mental Hygiene Law Article 10 is New York's own legislative answer to how a court should find present sexual dangerousness by clear and convincing evidence. It forbids exactly the inference the Case Summary draws: a factfinder "may not find solely on the basis of the respondent's commission of a sex offense" that the respondent presently suffers from a mental abnormality. MHL § 10.07(d). A diagnosed clinical condition, established through a psychiatric examiner's report, is a structural precondition to the proceeding even existing. MHL § 10.05(g). And Article 10 does not merely require the State to build its case on clinical evidence — it arms the respondent to contest that evidence. Any respondent under Article 10 may request court-ordered evaluation by a psychiatric examiner of his choosing, and "[i]f the respondent is financially unable to obtain an examiner, the court shall appoint an examiner of the respondent's choice to be paid within the limits prescribed by law." MHL § 10.06(e).

SORA supplies neither half of that structure. No provision of Correction Law Article 6-C gives a respondent any mechanism to obtain his own clinical evaluation to contest a threat-to-public-safety finding, funded or otherwise. The Board's own recommendation package here contains no psychiatric or psychological evaluation, no actuarial instrument beyond the RAI's own scoring, and no expert opinion of any kind on Respondent's current risk. The Case Summary is a narrative built entirely from conviction facts, with no clinical content whatsoever standing behind its "compelling evidence" conclusion.

Respondent is not without a remedy for that gap. County Law § 722-c, New York's general indigent-expert-funding statute, extends by its own text to "article six-c of the correction law" — SORA proceedings, directly, not by analogy. On a proper showing of necessity, a court may authorize funds for an expert. People v. Linton, 94 A.D.3d 962, 962-63 (2d Dep't 2012). Linton denied relief because the defendant there made only a generic request for departure assistance; it did not hold that no SORA respondent may ever obtain a funded expert. Respondent's necessity showing is not generic. Override 1 supplies the entire evidentiary basis for the "risk of repeat offense" element and supplies no evidence at all — not weak evidence, none — on the independent "threat to public safety" element. A forensic risk evaluation is the only mechanism available to Respondent to generate any evidence on the statutory element the Board's own package leaves silent. Respondent respectfully requests that this Court authorize funds under County Law § 722-c for a forensic risk-assessment evaluation before any final classification determination.

This request finds further support, offered candidly as the most distant of the analogies available, in Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68 (1985), which held that due process requires the State to provide an indigent defendant access to psychiatric assistance once his mental state becomes a significant issue the State itself has put in play. Ake is a criminal-trial and capital-sentencing doctrine, and Respondent does not present it as controlling a civil registration proceeding. But its underlying premise — that an indigent respondent cannot be asked to contest a clinical dangerousness finding with no clinical tools of his own — is the same premise Article 10 already codifies for New York's closest analogous civil-dangerousness proceeding, and SORA's total silence on the question, not merely its lower evidentiary bar, is what makes this record an appropriate one in which to grant the relief requested.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent respectfully requests that this Court grant a downward departure from the presumptive Level Three classification to Risk Level Two. In the alternative, Respondent requests that this Court require the People to independently establish, by clear and convincing evidence and through properly founded proof under Mingo, each disputed fact identified in this memorandum before relying on it, and that this Court authorize funds under County Law § 722-c for a forensic risk-assessment evaluation before any final classification determination is made.
