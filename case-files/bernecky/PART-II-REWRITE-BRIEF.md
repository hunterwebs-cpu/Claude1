## The client's objections, by provision (source material — argue these using independent judgment)

**SC ¶ 8 / ¶ 4 — material ban.** "I will not purchase, possess, view,
produce, or use sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind as defined by
my treatment staff." Companion ¶ 4 requires pre-approval before receiving
"sexually explicit images or narratives." The client's own insight, which
he considers important: don't treat this as fundamentally a pornography
argument. "Material(s) of any kind" is not confined to expressive content
on its face — it plausibly reaches ordinary legal adult items (sex toys,
condoms, lubricant) with no Judgment restriction and no connection to the
conduct of conviction. The Judgment restricts only child pornography
(Special Condition (j)) — never "legal adult" material of any kind. Known
authority: *Eaglin*, 913 F.3d 88 (2d Cir. 2019) (struck a legal-adult-
pornography ban tethered to a statute and judge-imposed with findings —
this term is untethered, provider-imposed, no findings); *Carlineo*, 998
F.3d 533, 536 (2d Cir. 2021), for vagueness. Adverse authority the Writer
should confirm and meet, not avoid: *Simmons*, 343 F.3d at 82, appears to
cite approvingly to *Phipps* and *Bee*, which upheld conditions using the
phrase "sexually stimulating" — these are unverified; read them and either
distinguish them honestly or account for them. Illustrative examples the
client offered, useful if the Writer finds them persuasive: a memoir he may
write describing the abuse he suffered; the PSR's own description of the
images in his possession; the Genesis account of Lot and his daughters;
the film *The Prince of Tides*.

**SC ¶ 5 — platform pre-approval.** Requires treatment-staff approval
before using "any platforms including but not limited to (social media,
dating sites, email...)" — expressly includes email. The Judgment's
Special Condition (a) authorizes only notice and monitoring by the U.S.
Probation Office. *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 514, found the same defect in its
own facts ("required the approval not only of the Probation Office, but
also of the treatment team").

**SC ¶ 2 — partner disclosure/consent.** Requires disclosing all intimate
partners and producing "acceptable proof of informed consent... which
includes disclosure of my offense history in full" before a sexual
relationship. The client considers this an end run around *Boles*, 914
F.3d 95, 111-12 (2d Cir. 2019), which invalidated a comparable
officer-discretion notification condition, and the district's own Mar. 22,
2019 standing order requiring the court, not an officer, to make any risk
finding — this provision reinstates that discarded structure with no
determination by anyone at all. *Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77, 82 (2d Cir. 2010),
vacated a near-identical partner-notification/offense-disclosure
condition, holding it causes "social isolation" and impairs
rehabilitation — real, binding authority for the client's isolation
concern (do not use an unverified clinical-literature isolation claim
instead of this). Also worth the Writer's judgment: no real clinical
protocol assigns the work of obtaining a third party's "informed consent"
to the patient producing documentary proof of it — that responsibility
belongs to the clinician (see ATSA sources below if useful).

**Consequences ¶ 2(vi) — undefined discharge trigger.** "[O]ther problem
behavior not outlined in this Treatment Contract, as determined in the
sole discretion of the staff of the Practice" triggers immediate
discharge. The client wants the real mechanism stated plainly: discharge
under this clause becomes a violation report, which can mean a warrant,
arrest, and revocation proceeding — on a standard nobody can read in
advance. *Matta*, 777 F.3d 116, 122 (2d Cir. 2015); *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d
at 538; *Jimenez*, No. 24-1661, slip op. at 34 (2d Cir. Feb. 25, 2026,
amended May 12, 2026) — likely the government's strongest available
authority, drawing its own line at exactly this kind of provision ("giving
[probation officers] the authority to decide whether [the defendant]
participates in treatment at all"). The Writer should meet this authority
head-on rather than avoid it.

**SC ¶ 9 — minor/vulnerable-population contact.** Requires reporting
"accidental, incidental, or intentional" contact with anyone under 18,
"potential or former victims," "vulnerable populations (as defined by
treatment staff)." The Judgment's Special Condition (c) reaches only
"deliberate contact." Prior research found that a private-document-versus-
judicial-order distinction (i.e., that this document shouldn't get the
benefit of a charitable construction because it isn't a court order) does
not hold up — *Kunz* applied its charitable construction to a Probation
form, not only judicial orders — so the Writer should build this argument
on *Browder*'s actual test instead: whether a term executes or contradicts
the Judgment. "Accidental" is the textual opposite of "deliberate." The
"vulnerable populations" language is independently vague and undefined,
graded by treatment staff, with no record support for Bernecky having any
issue with that category. Fifth Amendment concern: disclosing contact with
a "potential victim" would itself trigger mandatory reporting and create a
link in a chain of evidence — *Von Behren*, 822 F.3d 1139, 1146-47 (10th
Cir. 2016), discusses Federal Rules of Evidence 413 and 414 in this
context (not Rule 404 — 413/414 admit propensity evidence, which is the
stronger point for Bernecky); *Von Behren*'s own provider document had a
nearly identical "potential victims" police-reporting clause, which the
Tenth Circuit gave independent weight. Note *Von Behren* fn.9 excludes
questions relevant only to supervised-release status (uses
pornography-viewing as its own example) — the Writer should use judgment
about where the Fifth Amendment argument does and doesn't reach as a
result.

**Additional client point on SC ¶ 9, added after the rest of this brief —
verify and use.** The client's recollection: the Second Circuit has
consistently held that *incidental* contact with a minor can never itself
be a problem, and that minor-contact conditions in judgments have never
been construed to encompass ordinary incidental contact — this needs to be
verified against actual Second Circuit authority (check *Browder*,
*Carlineo*, or others already in the case's Drive folder; the Judgment's
own Special Condition (c) language reaching only "deliberate contact" is
consistent with this recollection and may itself be enough, but confirm
whether there is direct appellate authority saying so). SC ¶ 9 expressly
requires notification even for "accidental" and "incidental" contact —
going further than what the Judgment requires and, if the client's
recollection is confirmed, further than what controlling Second Circuit
authority would even permit a sentencing court to impose in the first
place. Separately, and independently of that point: whatever therapeutic
benefit disclosure of such contact might have, the client's view is that
his answer cannot be compelled by threat of revocation for a wide range of
reasons — the same category of concern discussed at length regarding the
polygraph condition in Part IV (worth a cross-reference rather than
repeating the analysis) — and this is a problem beyond what the Judgment
could even legally require, independent of the accidental/incidental
textual point above. Treat these as two separate, stackable arguments
against SC ¶ 9's incidental-contact notification requirement, not one.

**Requirements ¶ 1 / Code of Conduct ¶¶ 5-6 — compelled disclosure, no
privilege carve-out.** Requires "full acknowledgement," reports of "any
relapse in problematic behavior" within 24 hours, reports of "criminal
behaviors" beyond the offense of conviction — routed to Probation, with
law enforcement contact permitted. No carve-out anywhere for invoking the
Fifth Amendment. The client's position is unconditional: he will invoke
the privilege, and will not waive it under any circumstance regardless of
any claimed therapeutic benefit, because treatment providers are mandatory
reporters and no immunity — from any jurisdiction — has been offered.
Separately, "problematic behavior" and "relapse in problematic behavior"
are themselves vague and undefined — a distinct problem from the
self-incrimination one, arguable under *Carlineo*.

**Code of Conduct ¶ 6 — duplicative, no treatment purpose.** Requires
reporting residence/employment changes and a long list of "risk factors"
to treatment staff. Bernecky already owes comparable reporting to his
probation officer under Standard Conditions 5/7 and Special Conditions
(d)/(i) — the client's view is that this clause duplicates an existing
reporting relationship with no stated treatment purpose and no timeline.

**Consequences ¶ 3(a) — Probation on the Treatment Team.** This is the
provision the client feels most strongly about. Nothing in Special
Condition (b) or (h) puts Probation on a body that decides discharge —
those conditions authorize supervising "the details of participation,"
which *Matta* calls a minor detail a court may delegate; the client's view
(which the Writer should test rather than simply adopt) is that sitting on
the discharge-deciding body is not a detail but decision-making authority
the Judgment never gave away. The client's proposed relief: Probation is
not a member of any treatment team; provider reporting to Probation is
limited to attendance, participation/progress, and concerns grounded in
actual clinical findings, not to session notes, internal records, or raw
data absent a court order. If useful: ATSA's current guidelines state that
providers "do not attempt to assume the roles of supervision officers or
law enforcement agents," and require providers to warn clients in writing
that incriminating disclosures can lead to prosecution — this document
never gives that warning.
