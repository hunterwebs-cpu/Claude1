## 3. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1: What are the actual, defined elements of Override 1, and where do they come from?**

*Summary conclusion:* Override 1 requires (a) a **prior** conviction (b) that is a **felony** (c) for a "**sex crime**." The RAI form's one-line label is itself a fair paraphrase of the Guidelines' operative override language, which — per consistent, multiply-corroborated secondary-source characterization plus the Board's own boilerplate rationale reproduced verbatim in Bernecky's own Case Summary ("this provides compelling evidence that he poses a serious risk to public safety that is not captured by scoring alone") — treats a prior sex-crime felony as strong-enough evidence of dangerousness that the Board chose to make it an automatic override rather than a mere scored factor. "Sex crime," as used here and in RAI Factor 9 (which uses identical language), is **not** self-evidently identical to the narrower, closed-list "sex offense" / "sexually violent offense" definitions in Correction Law § 168-a(2)-(3) that govern *registration* eligibility; at least one Appellate Division opinion (*Davis*, 3d Dep't 2016) treats "sex crime" as a somewhat broader, conduct-focused Guidelines concept. This distinction does not end up mattering for Bernecky specifically — see Issue 3 — but it is a real, non-obvious wrinkle the Writer should know about rather than assume away.

**Issue 2: What standard governs the People's showing, and what must actually be in the record?**

*Summary conclusion:* Confirmed, across every source consulted without exception: **the People bear the burden of proving the applicability of a particular override by clear and convincing evidence**, and once that burden is met, the SORA court has **no discretion** to decline to apply the override — application is automatic, "irrespective of the points scored on the risk assessment instrument." As to what must be in the record: the case law I found (*People v. Korzeniowski*, 2d Dep't 2023) directly answers the sub-question the assignment posed — **a bare label is not what was tested, but a documented Case Summary narrative, without a certificate of conviction, has been held sufficient**; a defendant's argument that the override requires a certificate of conviction (not just a Board narrative) was rejected. Bernecky's Case Summary is considerably more detailed than a bare label — it recites the specific federal statute sections, the sentence, the district, and a narrative of the underlying offense conduct — which makes a Korzeniowski-style documentary-insufficiency challenge a weak one on this record specifically. See Section 5.B.

**Issue 3: Does Bernecky's 2012 federal § 2252A conviction qualify as "a prior felony conviction for a sex crime"?**

*Summary conclusion:* **Yes, and this does not appear to be a genuinely contestable point on this record.** Correction Law § 168-a(2)(d)(iii) — which I read directly, quoted verbatim in the Franczyk practice guide — expressly **enumerates 18 U.S.C. § 2252A by name** among the federal statutes whose convictions qualify as a New York "sex offense," "provided that the elements of such crime of conviction are substantially the same as those which are a part of such offense" (the Board's own Sex Offender Designation Form in Bernecky's file uses the identical "as of March 11, 2002" baseline-date formulation, and marked that exact box, for **both** his current and prior convictions). Because § 2252A(a)(1) (distribution) and § 2252A(a)(5)(B) (possession) are directly named — rather than needing a *North*/*Perez*-style essential-elements-plus-underlying-conduct comparison to some analogous New York offense — Bernecky's situation is more straightforward than the reported cases addressing out-of-state or non-enumerated federal convictions (e.g., *People v. Blue*, federal sex-trafficking; *People v. Perez*, New Jersey lewdness). I did not find, despite a genuinely diligent search using several different phrasings, any reported case specifically litigating whether a federal § 2252A child-pornography conviction qualifies for Override 1 — that appears to be because the statutory enumeration answers the question directly enough that it has not generated appellate litigation, not because the issue is unresolved. This absence is itself a reportable finding, and I flag the residual, unresolved "sex crime" vs. "sex offense" terminological question from Issue 1, though I do not think it changes the bottom line here. See Section 5.C.

**Issue 4: Has an override ever been successfully challenged on validity (not departure) grounds?**

*Summary conclusion:* I found the same pattern as the sibling memo found for departures: courts have entertained direct challenges to an override's proper establishment, but **every challenge on the substance of the override I located failed** — *People v. Moss* (Ct. App. 2025, arising from the Fourth Department and, notably, from **Monroe County Court** — the same court, same county, as Bernecky's case) rejected a challenge premised on the underlying conviction being constitutionally infirm, because the conviction had not been formally vacated; *Korzeniowski* rejected a documentary-sufficiency challenge. The one case I found where an Appellate Division actually **reversed** a Level 3 designation connected to an override (*People v. Ritchie*, 4th Dep't 2022) did so on a **procedural due-process/notice** theory (the court sua sponte revived the override issue after the parties had stipulated around it, without giving the defendant notice or a chance to be heard) — not on a substantive challenge to whether the override's elements were met. This is adverse authority for any strategy built around directly attacking Override 1's validity, and I surface it accordingly. See Section 5.D.

## 5. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### A. Override 1's elements, and the "sex crime" / "sex offense" wrinkle

**Rule.** The RAI form itself — a primary document in this case, which I have read directly — states: "If any override is circled, offender is presumptively a Level 3," and lists as override 1: "Offender has a prior felony conviction for a sex crime." Every secondary source I consulted, independently and without exception (CaseMine's commentary, multiple FindLaw-hosted Second Department opinions, and the Franczyk practice guide, the last of which I read directly), describes this as one of four Guidelines-created overrides, and attributes to the Guidelines and Commentary itself the rationale that these four circumstances were selected because each "provides compelling evidence that an offender poses a serious risk to public safety" not otherwise "captured by scoring alone" — language that, notably, **also appears verbatim in Bernecky's own Case Summary** ("this provides compelling evidence that he poses a serious risk to public safety that is not captured by scoring alone"), which I take as the Board itself echoing the Guidelines' own stated rationale for the override framework, increasing (without fully confirming) my confidence in that characterization of the Guidelines' content.

**The "sex crime" wrinkle.** Both Override 1 and RAI Factor 9 use the term "sex crime" rather than the defined terms "sex offense" or "sexually violent offense" that Correction Law § 168-a(2)-(3) uses to govern who must register at all. Correction Law § 168-a does not itself define "sex crime" as a freestanding term. *People v. Davis* (3d Dep't 2016) — per the lead described in Section 2 above, not a confirmed primary-source quotation — treats "sex crime" for RAI purposes as extending to endangering the welfare of a child specifically because that offense "generally involves sexual misconduct," even though endangering the welfare of a child is not, standing alone, always a registerable § 168-a "sex offense." If that characterization is accurate, "sex crime" for Override 1/Factor 9 purposes is a somewhat *broader*, more conduct-focused Guidelines concept than the closed statutory "sex offense" list — which would matter a great deal in a case where the prior conviction was for an offense *not* on the § 168-a enumerated list. It does not end up mattering for Bernecky, because his prior conviction offenses are enumerated on **both** the narrower § 168-a(2)(d)(iii) list (by statute name, not by conduct-comparison) **and** obviously satisfy the broader "generally involves sexual misconduct" conception. I flag this open question because the Writer should not assume the two terms are interchangeable in a case where the answer might matter more.

**Validation status:** The RAI form language is a primary document, read directly (high confidence). The Guidelines' stated override rationale is corroborated by two independent lines (secondary-source characterization plus Bernecky's own Case Summary boilerplate) but not independently confirmed against the Guidelines' own text (moderate confidence — not quotable). The *Davis* "sex crime" characterization is a lead only, not a confirmed quotation (low-to-moderate confidence — explicitly not to be quoted without independent verification).

### B. The governing burden of proof and what must be shown

**Rule, confirmed with unusual consistency.** Every case I examined that addressed Override 1 directly — *People v. Barr* (2d Dep't 2022), *People v. Gunter* (2d Dep't 2023), *People v. Butler* (2d Dep't 2024), *People v. McGuire* (2d Dep't 2025), *People v. Musmacker* (2d Dep't 2023) — states, in materially identical language traced back to *People v. McCurdy*, 198 A.D.3d 991 (2d Dep't 2021), and *People v. Lobello*, 123 A.D.3d 993 (2d Dep't 2014): **"The People bear the burden of proving the applicability of a particular override by clear and convincing evidence,"** and once that burden is met, **"a SORA court is not possessed of any discretion in determining whether to apply [an] override; the application of the override is automatic."** This is also exactly what the Franczyk guide states, and I read that statement directly in the primary-adjacent document itself (not merely through a case-law fetch), which gives me materially higher confidence in this specific proposition than in most others in this memo.

**Important caveat on sourcing of the case-law quotations above.** I obtained the Barr/Gunter/Butler/McGuire/Musmacker language through this session's web-fetch tool, which summarizes rather than returning raw opinion text verbatim. Per the brief's quotation-fidelity rule, I am **not** certifying the quoted strings above as verbatim primary-source text I personally verified character-for-character — I am reporting them as consistent, cross-corroborating characterizations from what purports to be FindLaw-hosted primary text, repeated near-identically across five independent case pages citing back to the same two originating cases (*McCurdy* and *Lobello*), which is a stronger evidentiary basis than a single source but still short of my own direct read. The Writer should re-pull at least *McCurdy* and *Lobello* from Westlaw/Lexis before quoting this language in a filing.

**What must actually be in the record.** This is the specific sub-question the assignment asked me to resolve: is a bare assertion sufficient, or must the underlying facts independently qualify? *People v. Korzeniowski* (2d Dep't 2023) is squarely on point: the defendant argued that the People had not proven the prior felony sex offense by clear and convincing evidence because they had not introduced a **certificate of conviction** — only the Board's Case Summary. The court rejected this argument, holding (per the consistent characterization across two independent search passes) that a Case Summary is sufficient documentary proof, citing back to *Barr* and *McCurdy*. **This means a bare RAI checkbox alone would likely not suffice, but a Case Summary that actually narrates the specific statute, offense conduct, and disposition — as Bernecky's does — has been held to meet the clear-and-convincing standard even without a certified judgment of conviction.** This is adverse authority to a documentary-insufficiency challenge on Bernecky's specific record, and I surface it as such rather than let the Writer discover it independently.

**Validation status:** Barr/Gunter/Butler/McGuire/Musmacker burden-of-proof language — reconstructed from secondary characterization of purported FindLaw-hosted primary text, cross-corroborated across five independent pages but not independently verified by me (moderate-high confidence as to substance, not quotable as verbatim). Korzeniowski — reconstructed from two independent WebSearch summarization passes, not read by me even through an intermediary full-text fetch (moderate confidence as to substance only). Franczyk guide's burden-of-proof statement — read directly by me in full (high confidence as to that document's content, though the guide itself is a secondary source one step removed from the Guidelines and case law it describes).

### C. Does Bernecky's federal § 2252A conviction qualify?

**The statutory hook.** Correction Law § 168-a(2)(d) — quoted verbatim in the Franczyk guide, which I read directly — defines a qualifying out-of-jurisdiction "sex offense" to include: "(i) an offense IN ANY OTHER JURISDICTION which includes ALL OF THE ESSENTIAL ELEMENTS of any such crime provided for in paragraphs (a), (b) or (c) [the enumerated NY Penal Law offenses] or (ii) a FELONY in ANY OTHER JURISDICTION FOR WHICH THE OFFENDER IS REQUIRED TO REGISTER AS A SEX OFFENDER IN THE JURISDICTION IN WHICH THE CONVICTION OCCURRED, or (iii) any of the provisions of 18 USC 2251, 2251A, 2252, **2252A**, 2260, 2422(b), [2423] or [2425], provided that the ELEMENTS OF SUCH CRIME OF CONVICTION ARE SUBSTANTIALLY THE SAME AS THOSE WHICH ARE A PART OF SUCH OFFENSE" — with the Board's own Designation Form (a primary document in this specific case) fixing that comparison date as March 11, 2002. This is a **direct statutory enumeration**, not a conduct-comparison test: § 2252A is named by number. Bernecky's prior conviction is explicitly for § 2252A(a)(1) and § 2252A(a)(5)(B).

**Elements stability since 2002.** The core prohibitions in § 2252A(a)(1) (distribution) and § 2252A(a)(5)(B) (possession) have not been substantively redefined since 2002; the amendments I was able to identify in that period (Pub. L. 107-273 in 2002 itself, and later additions of subsections (e)-(g)) affect penalty ranges and add affirmative-defense/enhancement provisions, not the core elements of the distribution and possession offenses themselves. I did not find, and would flag as a genuine gap if the Writer wanted to press this technically, a case specifically holding that § 2252A(a)(1)/(a)(5)(B)'s elements are "substantially the same" as of the March 11, 2002 baseline — but nothing I found suggests otherwise, and this does not appear to be a fruitful avenue for Bernecky.

**No case law specifically on federal CP convictions and Override 1.** I searched specifically and repeatedly (varying "federal," "child pornography," "2252A," "presumptive level three," and "override 1" in combination) and did not find a single reported case litigating whether a federal child-pornography conviction under § 2252A qualifies as a "sex crime" for Override 1 purposes. Every override case I found involved a prior *New York Penal Law* contact offense (rape, sodomy, sexual abuse) as the triggering conviction. I take the *absence* of litigation on this specific point, combined with the direct statutory enumeration of § 2252A by name (removing the need for an essential-elements comparison at all), as evidence that this is not a genuinely close question — not as evidence that the question has gone unaddressed because it is unresolved. The essential-elements-plus-underlying-conduct doctrine that *does* generate real litigation (*Matter of North v. Board of Examiners of Sex Offenders*, 8 N.Y.3d 745 (2007); *People v. Kennedy*, 7 N.Y.3d 87 (2006); *People v. Perez*, 2020 NY Slip Op 02096 (Ct. App. Mar. 26, 2020); *People v. Blue*, 2020 NY Slip Op 04689 (4th Dep't Aug. 20, 2020)) governs *non-enumerated* out-of-state or foreign offenses that must be compared conduct-by-conduct to an analogous New York Penal Law offense — a harder, more contestable inquiry Bernecky simply does not face, because his statute of conviction is named directly in the New York statute.

**Validation status:** The Correction Law § 168-a(2)(d) text — read directly by me, quoted in the Franczyk guide (high confidence as to that document's rendering of the statute; not independently cross-checked against McKinney's or the official NY Senate statutory text — flagged in Section 8). The Board's own Designation Form markings — primary document in this case, read directly (high confidence). *North*/*Kennedy*/*Perez*/*Blue* — reconstructed from secondary-source search characterization only, not read in any form by me (low-moderate confidence, not quotable). The federal statute's amendment history — reconstructed from a general web search of the U.S. Code's own history notes, not independently read against the full amendment text (moderate confidence).

### D. Has an override ever been successfully challenged on validity grounds?

**The closest and most important case: *People v. Moss*.** *People v. Moss*, 2025 NY Slip Op 01673 (Ct. App. 2025), is, on the facts I was able to reconstruct, the single most directly relevant precedent located in this entire research effort — not primarily for its holding (adverse to a validity challenge), but because it **arose from Monroe County Court itself**, on appeal through the **Fourth Department** (Appellate Division order entered December 22, 2023, in the same Judicial District as Bernecky's hearing), and squarely addresses a challenge to Override 1's proper establishment on facts involving a defendant who was also designated a "sexually violent offender and predicate sex offender" — the identical designation category marked on Bernecky's own Designation Form. Per the consistent, multiply-corroborated secondary characterization I was able to assemble (I could not obtain the raw opinion text, despite trying nycourts.gov directly, a reader-proxy, and several mirrors — all blocked or unavailable): Moss had a 2006 felony conviction for course of sexual conduct against a child; a later resentencing court found substantial evidence the underlying guilty plea had been unconstitutionally coerced and declined to use it as a sentencing predicate in a different (non-SORA) proceeding, but the 2006 conviction itself was never vacated or reversed through a direct challenge. The Court of Appeals held that, **absent a direct vacatur or reversal of the prior conviction**, the Guidelines' text controls and the override applies to an "undisturbed" prior conviction — rejecting the argument that a later court's sentencing-context doubts about the conviction's constitutionality were enough to defeat the override. **This is adverse authority for any strategy premised on attacking the validity of Bernecky's 2012 conviction indirectly** (through argument alone, rather than a direct vacatur), and — because there is no indication anywhere in Bernecky's file that the 2012 conviction has been vacated, appealed, or is under any collateral attack — Moss's reasoning, if it in fact holds what the sources consistently describe, would apply with full force against Bernecky on this record.

**A documentary-sufficiency challenge also failed.** *People v. Korzeniowski* — discussed in Section 5.B above — is the other example of a validity (not departure) challenge to an override, and it also failed.

**The one reversal was procedural, not substantive.** *People v. Ritchie*, 2022 NY Slip Op 01635 (4th Dep't Mar. 11, 2022) — a Fourth Department case, per the FindLaw-sourced characterization I obtained — involved a defendant whose Board-recommended override the parties' stipulation and the court's initial "so ordered" appeared to bypass entirely; the SORA court then, without notice to the defense, revisited the point score sua sponte and separately noted it would have found Level 3 "due to the override" in any event. The Fourth Department reversed and remitted for a new hearing, on the ground that the sua sponte point reassessment (and, per the characterization, the manner the override issue was handled) violated the defendant's due-process right to notice and an opportunity to be heard. This is a genuinely useful precedent for a **notice/procedural** argument if the Board's or the People's presentation at Bernecky's hearing tries to introduce or rely on anything beyond what was disclosed in the package already in the client's file — but it is not a precedent for challenging whether Override 1's substantive elements (prior, felony, sex crime) were met on a properly-noticed record, which is what Bernecky faces here.

**Assessment.** I looked specifically and diligently for a case where an override was defeated on the substantive ground that the prior conviction did not actually qualify as a "sex crime" or a "felony," and found none — for any offender, not just for one with a federal CP prior. Combined with the sibling memo's parallel finding on departures (no located example of a departure grant from Override 1), the consistent picture across both research efforts is that New York courts treat Override 1 as functioning almost exactly as designed: once a felony sex-crime conviction is documented, both direct validity challenges and departure requests face a genuinely uphill, disfavored path.

**Validation status:** *Moss* — reconstructed from consistent secondary-source characterization across four independent search results (including one describing it as "an appeal by permission of the Court of Appeals from an order of the Appellate Division of the Supreme Court in the Fourth Judicial Department," which is itself a specific, checkable procedural fact); the fact that it originated in **Monroe County** in particular is corroborated by that same search result and should be independently confirmed before being relied on as a "same-court" precedent in a filing (moderate confidence as to substance and origin; not quotable). *Korzeniowski* and *Ritchie* — reconstructed from secondary-source search characterization only (moderate confidence as to substance; not quotable).

## 6. CONCLUSION

On the research assembled here, **Override 1 appears to have been correctly and defensibly established against Bernecky as a matter of law**, and I do not see a strong, independent avenue for challenging its proper *application* (as opposed to seeking a *departure* from it, which is the separate question the sibling memo already addresses). The elements — prior, felony, sex crime — are all satisfied on the current record: the 2012 conviction is prior to the instant offense; it is unambiguously a federal felony; and it qualifies as a "sex crime" both under the direct statutory enumeration of 18 U.S.C. § 2252A in Correction Law § 168-a(2)(d)(iii) (which removes the need for any essential-elements comparison) and, more informally, under the broader "generally involves sexual misconduct" conception of "sex crime" that at least one Appellate Division opinion attributes to the Guidelines and Commentary itself. The Board's showing — a detailed Case Summary narrative specifying the exact statute sections, sentence, and offense conduct, corroborated by its own independently-generated Designation Form marking the identical federal statutes as qualifying — meets the *Korzeniowski* standard for sufficiency even without a certificate of conviction in the file. And the one body of case law addressing direct validity challenges to Override 1 (as opposed to departure requests) — most importantly *Moss*, arising from the same Monroe County Court — points toward such challenges failing absent an actual, formal vacatur of the predicate conviction, which nothing in this file suggests exists or is available here.
