# DEFENDANT'S MOTION TO MODIFY CONDITIONS OF SUPERVISED RELEASE
## PURSUANT TO 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2), FED. R. CRIM. P. 32.1(c), AND U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4

Defendant Jeffrey Bernecky, proceeding *pro se*, moves under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2) and Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32.1(c) to modify several conditions of supervised release imposed by the Judgment entered June 12, 2018. Dkt. No. 28.

## PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

A judgment's silence is not a blank space for someone else to fill in.

That is the whole of this motion. The conditions Mr. Bernecky is serving were written in 2018 for a man nobody would meet for another eight years, at a hearing where the question in the room was how long he would spend in prison. Nobody there was asked what a computer-monitoring clause would mean to a manufacturing company's HR department, or whether a man in an apartment complex may say hello to the neighbor two doors down. The words went onto the page with those questions unasked, and eight years later they are being read by whoever happens to be holding them.

Supervision began on or about July 30, 2026, and the pattern showed itself immediately. An employer that had offered Mr. Bernecky permanent work read Special Condition (a) and asked whether hiring him would open the company's own computer systems to federal monitoring. Nothing in the condition answers that question. He lost about two weeks of pay while it was sorted out informally, at the moment he was leaving Bureau of Prisons custody and taking on a one-year lease. That employer hired him anyway. The next one may not.

Probation also required him to sign a twenty-one-item Computer Monitoring Program Participant Agreement that no judge has ever reviewed, which forbids him the antivirus software his operating system installed at the factory, forbids the private-browsing mode that ships as a default on every major browser, and lets a probation officer decide, by no stated standard, whether he may own a device at all. His personal telephone, on his own carrier plan, now tells him on its lock screen that it belongs to an organization. And Special Condition (g) commands him to answer questions under polygraph, offering in exchange a promise that he may argue about the answer later — which is not what the Fifth Amendment provides.

Mr. Bernecky does not ask this Court to revisit his sentence. He asks it to say what its own order means, now, so that he serves the next ten years knowing what each condition requires rather than learning it one violation report at a time. Congress made that authority available "at any time," 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2), and Rule 32.1's drafters explained why: "the probationer should be able to obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it." Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's note to subdiv. (b) (1979).

## JURISDICTION AND AUTHORITY

This Court may "modify, reduce, or enlarge the conditions of supervised release, at any time prior to the expiration or termination of the term of supervised release." 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2). That authority exists "to account for new or unforeseen circumstances." *United States v. Lussier*, 104 F.3d 32, 36 (2d Cir. 1997).

The Second Circuit has already told a court in this District to use this precise mechanism at this precise moment. *United States v. Balon* involved a W.D.N.Y. defendant and computer-monitoring conditions materially identical to Special Condition (a). The panel held his challenges unripe because "it is currently impossible to predict the state of computer technology at the commencement of Balon's supervised release period," and instructed the district court "to reconsider, at the instance of the government or Balon and in light of *Lifshitz*, the special conditions regarding monitoring of Balon's computer at a time closer to Balon's term of supervised release." 384 F.3d 38, 40, 47 (2d Cir. 2004). It then held expressly "that changing computer technology is an appropriate factor to authorize a modification of supervised release conditions under Section 3583(e)." *Id.* at 47. *Balon* is the case that put Mr. Bernecky here, now.

*Balon* also marks the boundary of the rule that a condition's illegality is not itself a ground for modification. That rule, the panel explained, "held only that a condition of supervised release that was illegal *ab initio* could not be challenged in a Section 3583(e) proceeding and has no applicability to a case where fluid circumstances alter either the need for, or the efficacy of, certain conditions." *Id.* at 47 n.4; *accord United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748, 774 n.14 (2d Cir. 2023).

Circumstances change in law as well as in fact, and both have changed here. On the facts: a Participant Agreement Probation drafted and required eight years after sentencing, a category of consumer devices that did not exist in its current form, an employer dispute, an active state proceeding in which Mr. Bernecky's treatment statements are being used against him, and employment with a retirement benefit. On the law: the authority this motion relies on most heavily postdates his sentencing — *Kunz* in 2023, construing this District's own monitoring program on this identical text; *United States v. Boles*, 914 F.3d 95 (2d Cir. 2019), and this District's standing order implementing it, both from 2019; *United States v. Brown*, 173 F.4th 45 (2d Cir. 2026); and *Hunter v. United States*, 608 U.S. \_\_\_, No. 24-1063 (June 18, 2026). Judge Larimer could not have applied in 2018 authority that did not exist until years later.

The Sentencing Commission's 2025 amendment is itself such a change, and it was written for this motion's exact posture. Effective November 1, 2025, U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a) invites courts to conduct "an individualized assessment of the appropriateness of existing conditions ... in consultation with the probation officer after the defendant's release from imprisonment," because conditions "are imposed at original sentencing, often years before the defendant begins supervision," and circumstances "may change in and after prison, such that the original term and conditions may no longer be appropriate."

Where Probation has read a condition to reach further than this Court's own text authorizes, this Court supplies the remedy. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68 (citing *United States v. Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d 147, 153 (2d Cir. 2020), for the rule that where Probation "does overstep [its] authority," the sentencing court may provide relief).

## ARGUMENT

### I. Special Condition (a) — The Computer and Internet Monitoring Program

The Judgment tells Mr. Bernecky to notify his probation officer of the devices he will use. The Participant Agreement tells him he may use only the devices his probation officer permits. Those are different instructions, and only one of them is his sentence.

Special Condition (a) bars him from using or possessing "any computer, data storage device, or any internet capable device unless the defendant participates in the Computer and Internet Monitoring Program ..., or unless authorized by the Court or the U.S. Probation Office." It requires him to "provide the U.S. Probation Office advance notification of any computer(s), automated service(s), or connected device(s) that will be used during the term of supervision." It authorizes Probation "to install any application as necessary to surveil all activity on computer(s) or connected device(s) owned or operated by the defendant," directs that Probation "shall be notified via electronic transmission of impermissible/suspicious activity," and permits unannounced examinations of equipment "owned or used by the defendant," but only "[a]s triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity." It then limits all of it: monitoring and examinations "shall be designed to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information or any private material that is not illegal or reasonably likely to lead to illegal material or evidence related to illegal activity." Dkt. No. 28.

At intake Probation required Mr. Bernecky to sign the twenty-one-item Computer Monitoring Program Participant Agreement attached as Exhibit A. No judge has ever reviewed it. Its terms appear nowhere in the Judgment, and it closes by warning him that "any violation may be reported to the Court and may result in adverse action." Ex. A.

Since supervision began, the condition has been administered as a system of permissions. An employer that had offered Mr. Bernecky permanent work read Special Condition (a), asked whether hiring him would subject the company's own systems to federal monitoring, and told him not to come back until the question was answered. Certification ¶ \_\_. His probation officer told him the company's monitoring capability would have to satisfy a federal standard she did not identify. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. About two weeks of pay later, Probation accepted the arrangement the employer had proposed on its own — an internal account with internet access disabled — and the matter closed informally, nothing decided and nothing written down. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. Probation has approved a television model that has no web browser, then set a PIN code on it so no application can be installed without an officer's consent. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. And Mr. Bernecky has been told he may not work anywhere that employs people under eighteen — an instruction that appears in no condition of his sentence. *Id.* ¶ \_\_.

#### A. The Judgment requires notice of devices; Exhibit A ¶ 3 requires permission, which the Second Circuit has already held Probation may not enforce.

Exhibit A ¶ 3 provides: "I shall not use, own, or access any device which allows internet access other than what I have been authorized to use by the Probation Office." The Judgment says he "must provide the U.S. Probation Office advance notification of any computer(s), automated service(s), or connected device(s) that will be used." Dkt. No. 28. Notice plus silence leaves a device permitted. Permission plus silence leaves it forbidden. The Agreement does not add a detail to the Judgment; it reverses its default.

*United States v. Kunz* construed this District's identical CIMP boilerplate against a special condition whose operative sentences are word for word the ones in Mr. Bernecky's Judgment. 68 F.4th 748 (2d Cir. 2023). The panel read the notification passage to do exactly what it says: it "simply orders Kunz to proactively notify Probation of any device, or devices, that he intends to use while under supervision so that they may be outfitted with surveillance software." *Id.* at 767-68. From that the court drew the conclusion that decides this issue: "the court's language implies that it is Kunz himself, not Probation, who is empowered to decide how many internet-connected devices he wishes to use." *Id.*

The CIMP form before the *Kunz* panel contained a term providing that the supervisee "may be limited to ... one Internet-capable device." The Second Circuit held that term "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court, and therefore may not be enforced as written," and affirmed the judgment only "on the understanding that the Probation Department may not enforce any CIMP term that purports to allow it to limit Kunz to a single internet-connected device." *Id.* at 767-68. A restriction of that weight "would need to be specifically imposed by the district court and justified by an express, case-specific rationale for imposing it," and in *Kunz*, "[n]one of that happened here." *Id.* at 767-68.

Item 3 is the same defect without the ceiling. The term *Kunz* struck reserved to Probation the power to cut a supervisee down to one device. Item 3 reserves the power to decide which devices, how many, and whether any at all — a power stated in no standard and exercised by no judge. If a term that might reduce Mr. Bernecky to one device exceeds Probation's delegated authority, a term permitting Probation to leave him with none does not survive the same test. And none of that happened here either: Judge Larimer imposed a notification requirement in 2018 and made no finding about how many internet-capable devices Mr. Bernecky may own, because nobody asked him to.

That allocation of authority is not a technicality. A court "may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *United States v. Matta*, 777 F.3d 116, 122 (2d Cir. 2015). The power to impose "significantly greater restrictions on [a supervisee's] liberty" is "reserved exclusively for the district court." *United States v. Carlineo*, 998 F.3d 533, 537-38 (2d Cir. 2021). And the liberty is real: computers and internet access "have become virtually indispensable in the modern world of communications and information gathering," *United States v. Peterson*, 248 F.3d 79, 83 (2d Cir. 2001) — a decision from this District — and a computer and a telephone alike are "indispensable to participation in modern society," *United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d 88, 98 (2d Cir. 2019), quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68.

The government's best answer is the Judgment's own word "authorized," and it will not carry the weight. The opening sentence forbids use of a computer "unless the defendant participates in the [CIMP], or unless authorized by the Court or the U.S. Probation Office." That clause is an alternative route to permitted use for a supervisee outside the program — a second door, not a lock on the first. Read the other way, it makes the next sentence surplusage: there would be no reason to command advance notice of devices "that will be used" if no device could be used without advance approval. The *Kunz* panel read the same two sentences together and found the choice of devices left to the supervisee. 68 F.4th at 767-68.

#### B. Eight more terms of Exhibit A reach past the Judgment or cannot be obeyed as written.

Three of them the Second Circuit has already fixed, and Mr. Bernecky asks only that the fix be stated on the record here. Items 6, 9, and 17 require advance notice of "any alterations to computer(s)/connected device(s) or passwords/screen names prior to executing change," forbid "any encrypted email accounts," and forbid "any type of encryption unless pre-approved." Ex. A ¶¶ 6, 9, 17. *Kunz* construed the same terms to reach only what the supervisee himself does: they are "intended to preclude a defendant's active attempts to avoid monitoring, not to punish a passive defendant for innocuous day-to-day activities that trigger changes initiated by forces outside of his control," and the encryption restrictions "allow the reasonable use of services and resources that happen to employ encryption in some way." 68 F.4th at 763-64. Without that construction the terms are unusable. The major consumer email services encrypt by default, and every address beginning "https" signifies a site that encrypts data as it is transferred; read literally, the terms forbid Mr. Bernecky the ordinary internet and "demand the impossible" of him. *Id.* at 764-65 (quoting *United States v. Johnson*, 446 F.3d 272, 281 (2d Cir. 2006)).

Item 5 belongs in a different place, and Mr. Bernecky says so plainly. It provides that "[w]hile using my monitored device, I shall not use any form of 'in-private' browsing at any time" — a restriction with an evident purpose and a limit stated in its own first clause. Ex. A ¶ 5. He asks only that it be confirmed to mean what it says: it governs the devices Probation monitors and reaches nothing else.

Item 16 the Second Circuit has not addressed, and it does the most practical damage: "I shall not purchase, download, possess and/or install any anti-virus, anti-spyware, firewall, or internet security application without advanced authorization by the U.S. Probation Office and my probation officer." Ex. A ¶ 16. Current consumer operating systems ship with a firewall and antivirus enabled at the factory. Read as written, the term makes possession of an ordinary new computer a violation on the day it is taken out of the box, and it directs a man whose devices are monitored to strip them of the defenses that protect the monitored data. The Judgment authorizes Probation to *add* an application. Dkt. No. 28. It says nothing about removing the ones already there.

Three others reach subjects the Judgment does not touch. Item 19 requires Mr. Bernecky to "notify all individuals who have access to my computer system/internet accessible device that it is subject to monitoring and/or search/seizure" — a third-party notification duty. Ex. A ¶ 19. The only notification obligation in his sentence is Standard Condition 12, whose language the Second Circuit vacated and this District ordered removed from every judgment seven years ago. Whether Mr. Bernecky must tell anyone anything is for this Court to determine, not for a probation officer to require and not for a form to supply. *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 86 (a court "may not simply leave the issues of employer notification to the probation officer's unfettered discretion"); *see* Part VII, *infra*. Item 21 permits seizure of his hardware on reasonable suspicion "that a violation of a condition of supervision or unlawful conduct *by any person* has or is about to occur." Ex. A ¶ 21 (emphasis added). The Judgment ties examination to "impermissible/suspicious activity" on his own devices and authorizes nothing on suspicion of what somebody else may be about to do. And Item 8 forbids booting "any computer" from removable media, Ex. A ¶ 8, while the Judgment's reach stops at equipment "owned or operated by the defendant." Dkt. No. 28.

#### C. The condition should be construed to reach the devices, and to read the material, it was written to reach.

Special Condition (a) begins with a capability qualifier — "any internet capable device" — and then drops it, authorizing installation on "connected device(s)" and examination of "computer equipment." Dkt. No. 28. Restoring the condition's own drafting logic, and calibrating it to this offense, resolves most of what is unworkable about it.

The category the qualifier now describes has outrun anything a 2018 sentencing could have contemplated. The Second Circuit has itself noted "the proliferation of the 'Internet of Things,' which has exploded the category of devices that could qualify as internet-connected." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68. A thermostat, a wireless printer, a washing machine, a garage-door opener, and a fitness tracker are each an internet-capable device. None can browse the web or display a library of images. *Peterson* answered this kind of overinclusion in the case that governs this District: "Although a defendant might use the telephone to commit fraud, this would not justify a condition of probation that includes an absolute bar on the use of telephones. Nor would defendant's proclivity toward pornography justify a ban on all books, magazines, and newspapers." 248 F.3d at 83.

The Second Circuit has supplied the workable line, in a child-pornography case and in this exact procedural posture. Vacating an employment condition, the panel in *United States v. Ruff* noted that the district court retained authority "pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2) to modify [the] conditions of supervised release to require that Ruff advise his probation officer whether his employment entails access to *a computer that could be used to access child pornography over the internet*." No. 18-2325-cr (2d Cir. Nov. 14, 2019) (summary order) (emphasis added). That is the standard Mr. Bernecky asks this Court to apply to the whole condition. It is also what the Fourth Amendment requires of the monitoring: it "must be narrowly tailored, and not sweep so broadly as to draw a wide swath of extraneous material into its net," *United States v. Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d 173, 190 (2d Cir. 2004), and must be "precisely targeted to" the government's actual objective, *United States v. Brown*, 173 F.4th 45 (2d Cir. 2026), slip op. at 10 (quoting *Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d at 190).

The word "operated" needs the same treatment. If a device is "operated by the defendant" whenever he touches it, the ownership limit in the sentence disappears, and the condition reaches a shared workstation, a self-checkout terminal, and an ATM — systems over which Mr. Bernecky has no dominion and whose owners have never been heard. The clause should be read to mean what its neighbors mean: devices within his ownership or control.

That leaves the material. Special Condition (a)'s last sentence is a constraint on Probation, not a courtesy, and *Kunz* held as much: it "constrains the supervisors in their surveillance ... by requiring them not to abuse their power and access." 68 F.4th at 766-67. The Judgment's architecture matches that limit. Probation is to be "notified via electronic transmission of *impermissible/suspicious* activity," and examinations follow "[a]s triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity." Dkt. No. 28 (emphasis added). That is an exception-reporting design, and it is the design *Lifshitz* preferred — software alerting an officer to impermissible activity "would bear much greater resemblance to screening a probationer's urine for particular drugs," while constant inspection of what a supervisee writes "might be more like searching his diary or inspecting his closets." 369 F.3d at 191-92.

Nothing in Exhibit A implements that sentence, and Mr. Bernecky has no way to know whether the monitoring on his devices honors it. The stakes are present, not projected. He is litigating two matters — this motion, and a New York Sex Offender Registration Act proceeding in Monroe County Court in which he has requested assigned counsel — and the drafts, notes, and correspondence for both sit on the monitored device. Certification ¶ \_\_. His personal telephone, on his own carrier plan, now displays on its lock screen the message "This device belongs to your organization." *Id.* ¶ \_\_. Whatever configuration produces that message, the Judgment authorized installing a monitoring application, not handing the device over to somebody else's management. He asks the Court to give the final sentence operative content.

#### D. The Judgment imposes no occupational restriction. One is nevertheless being applied.

Federal law permits a sentencing court to restrict a defendant's employment, but only on findings. A court may restrict his ability to engage in an occupation or profession "bearing a reasonably direct relationship to the conduct constituting the offense," 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(5), and may do so only if it determines both that "a reasonably direct relationship existed between the defendant's occupation, business, or profession and the conduct relevant to the offense of conviction" and that "imposition of such a restriction is reasonably necessary to protect the public because there is reason to believe that, absent such restriction, the defendant will continue to engage in unlawful conduct similar to that for which the defendant was convicted." U.S.S.G. § 5F1.5(a); *see Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 83.

Neither finding appears in Mr. Bernecky's Judgment, and no occupational restriction appears in it either. Nor could those findings have been made on this record: neither of his convictions involved an employer's equipment, account, or workplace. That is the same gap that produced vacatur in *Ruff*, where the panel found "no evidence that he has used a workplace computer to access child pornography and therefore no evidence that any 'relationship existed between [Ruff's] occupation' and the conduct relevant to the offense at issue." *Ruff*, No. 18-2325-cr.

The restriction is being applied anyway, in two forms. Mr. Bernecky has been told he may not work anywhere that employs people under eighteen — an exclusion reaching whole sectors in which employing sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds is routine, stated in no condition of his sentence and supported by no finding anyone has identified. And Special Condition (a) itself was used to gate his employment: he was told not to come back to work, and lost roughly two weeks of pay, while Probation considered whether an employer's internal systems measured up to a standard it never named.

*Brown* marks the boundary, and this case sits on the far side of it. A computer-monitoring condition is not an occupational restriction, that panel held, where it "does not bar [the defendant] from a specific occupation, business, or profession, restrict him from using a computer or computer equipment such that he would be functionally barred from occupations requiring such use, ... or compel [him] to notify his employers of the nature of his conviction." *Brown*, slip op. at 12 (citing *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 83). As administered, Special Condition (a) does the first two. And *Peterson* supplies the converse: a computer condition limiting the terms on which a defendant may participate in a field is an occupational restriction even where it does not bar him from the field outright. 248 F.3d at 84 n.2.

The remedy is a sentence of clarification, and it costs the government nothing. The word "employer" does not appear in Special Condition (a). The Judgment's silence on employment is not an ambiguity for Probation to resolve; it is the absence of an authority nobody ever granted. Mr. Bernecky's current employer wants that stated as much as he does, and the next one will not wait two weeks to find out.

**Relief requested (Part I):** an order (1) construing Special Condition (a) to require advance notice of the devices Mr. Bernecky will use rather than Probation's authorization, and providing that Exhibit A ¶ 3 may not be enforced to require Probation's permission to acquire, own, or use an internet-capable device; (2) providing that Exhibit A ¶¶ 6, 9, and 17 are construed as *Kunz* construed the same terms, reaching only encryption and alterations Mr. Bernecky himself initiates; that ¶ 16 may not be enforced to require him to forgo or remove antivirus, anti-malware, or firewall protection supplied by his device or operating system; that ¶ 5 reaches only devices on which monitoring software is installed and ¶ 8 only devices within his ownership or control; that ¶ 19 imposes no notification obligation, that question remaining for this Court under Standard Condition 12 as addressed in Part VII, *infra*; and that ¶ 21 authorizes seizure only upon reasonable suspicion concerning Mr. Bernecky's own conduct; (3) construing "internet capable device," and the devices subject to installation and examination, to reach devices within Mr. Bernecky's ownership or control that are capable of accessing, storing, or displaying visual images over the internet, and not devices or systems belonging to an employer or other third party; (4) directing that the monitoring installed on his devices be configured to give effect to Special Condition (a)'s final sentence, including a protocol excluding materials prepared for or exchanged in connection with pending legal proceedings, and that Probation state for the record what the monitoring captures and what it transmits, with an evidentiary hearing on feasibility if Probation contests it; and (5) confirming that no condition of his supervision restricts his choice of occupation or employer, including any restriction based on whether an employer employs or serves persons under eighteen.

### II. Special Conditions (b) and (h) — The R.K.K.S. Treatment Contract

A private counseling practice cannot write conditions of supervised release. Mr. Bernecky asks this Court to say so now, and to say what his own conditions require, rather than have the question reach this courtroom in the form of an arrest warrant. He is not asking to be excused from treatment. He is asking not to be left with two bad choices — go to prison for refusing a private company's terms, or surrender liberty that no judge has taken from him because a private company demanded it and no one asked a court whether that was warranted.

Special Condition (b) orders him to "participate in a sex offense-specific treatment program and follow the rules and regulations of that program," leaves to the probation officer "the details of the defendant's participation in the program, including the selection of a provider and schedule," and directs that he "is not to leave treatment until complete or as ordered by the Court." Special Condition (h) orders him to "participate in a mental health treatment program, including a mental health evaluation and any treatment recommended," on materially the same terms, and contains no rules-and-regulations clause at all. Dkt. No. 28. Before R.K.K.S. Mental Health Counseling PLLC would treat him, it required him to sign the three-page instrument attached as Exhibit B, captioned "Treatment Contract." He signed it on August 19, 2026, and he signed it for a reason: the defendant in the Second Circuit case that controls this one refused to sign a document like it and was arrested for refusing. Certification ¶ \_\_. Mr. Bernecky is in treatment, he is attending, and he intends to stay. He signed, and then he came here — rather than refusing and litigating the same question from a detention cell. Much of what that document commands appears nowhere in his Judgment.

This District has already worked through what happens next, in a case with the same kind of document and nearly the same words in the judgment. Brian Browder was serving a W.D.N.Y. term of supervised release that required him to enroll in and "comply with the mandates of" a sex-offender treatment program. His provider — the Probation Office's chosen provider — required a treatment agreement containing a term barring contact with anyone under seventeen without permission, while his Judgment expressly excluded his own children from its no-contact condition. He appeared for at least two sessions, refused to sign, and put his objection in a letter to his probation officer. The provider terminated him. The Probation Office petitioned, the government moved successfully to detain him, and after a hearing the district court found the violation proven and sentenced him. *United States v. Browder*, 866 F.3d 504, 506-08 (2d Cir. 2017).

The Second Circuit reversed, holding that "it was reasonable for Browder to object to signing a treatment agreement that conflicted with his actual sentence," and that nothing showed he "otherwise acted unreasonably with respect to participating in such treatment." *Id.* at 505-06. The panel rejected the district court's reasoning that permission would have been granted for the asking: "The key question is not whether permission, if sought, would be granted; it is whether Browder can be required, by the Probation Office or its designee, to ask in the first place. Because such a requirement as to Browder's children would contradict, rather than execute, Browder's sentence, the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose it." *Id.* at 514. The principle appears in a single line of a footnote: "the treatment cannot be implemented in a way that directly conflicts with other, specific conditions of his supervised release." *Id.* at 513 n.37.

On remand, Judge Vilardo stated the rule in a sentence: "As the Second Circuit's decision on the treatment condition teaches, Browder cannot be forced to sign or abide by agreements that reach beyond the Court's ordered conditions." Decision and Order, *United States v. Browder*, No. 10-CR-263 (W.D.N.Y. Mar. 14, 2018) (Vilardo, J.) [*Browder* Remand Order]. Judge Vilardo held that although the treatment condition remained, "such treatment may not be contingent on his signing any agreement that forces him to obtain permission to have contact with his own children" — and, more broadly, that Browder "need not sign any treatment agreement requiring him to obtain such permission." *Id.*

The same order did one more thing, and it is why *Browder* is not merely analogous authority. Judge Vilardo re-imposed Browder's special conditions "as revised to reflect guidance from the Second Circuit since those conditions were first imposed in 2011." The sex offense-specific treatment condition Judge Vilardo entered is Mr. Bernecky's Special Condition (b) word for word, but for one sentence about inpatient treatment; the mental-health condition entered in that same order is Special Condition (h) sentence for sentence, down to its alcohol clause. *Browder* Remand Order (re-imposed special conditions). That order issued March 14, 2018. Mr. Bernecky was sentenced three months later, on June 12, 2018. The words "follow the rules and regulations of that program" are what replaced "comply with the mandates of the treatment program" when this District revised the condition to reflect the Second Circuit's guidance. *Compare Browder*, 866 F.3d at 507 n.4, *with Browder* Remand Order. That phrase is the product of the correction *Browder* required. It is not a grant of the power that correction took away.

Read against the Judgment, the delegation is narrow and its boundary is marked. Special Condition (b) hands the probation officer "the details of the defendant's participation in the program, including the selection of a provider and schedule." That is the precise and entire category the Second Circuit permits: a court "may delegate to a probation officer decisionmaking authority over certain minor details of supervised release — for example, the selection of a therapy provider or treatment schedule," but "may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *United States v. Matta*, 777 F.3d 116, 122 (2d Cir. 2015). The "supervision mission" of a probation officer "includes execut[ing] the sentence, ... but not imposing it." *Id.* Or, as this Circuit has since put it, "the Probation Office may supervise and execute a sentence but may not fashion a sentence's terms." *United States v. Carlineo*, 998 F.3d 533, 538 (2d Cir. 2021). A program term that cannot be squared with the court's own condition "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court, and therefore may not be enforced as written." *United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748, 767-68 (2d Cir. 2023).

Here the chain runs one link further than in any of those cases. The terms were not written by a probation officer; they were written by a private professional limited liability company. And the document then folds Probation back in on the provider's own terms: it defines a "Treatment Team ... comprised of all therapists in the Practice, the Probation Officer, and the Probation Supervisor," and gives that body the decision whether to discharge Mr. Bernecky from the treatment his sentence requires him to complete. Ex. B, Consequences ¶¶ 3, 3(a). A probation officer is not a clinician. Special Condition (b) hands him the administrative details of treatment — which provider, which schedule — and the Judgment gives him no part in the clinical judgment whether a patient should remain in treatment or be put out of it. Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (b). The document seats him on the panel that makes that judgment anyway, and seats his supervisor beside him.[^ii-2]

Two provisions show what the document adds, and they are illustrations rather than an inventory. Special Conditions ¶ 8 provides: "I will not purchase, possess, view, produce, or use sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind as defined by my treatment staff." On its face, that paragraph reaches all manner of sexually stimulating material — material of any kind, purchased, possessed, viewed, produced, or used. The problems with a term written that broadly are myriad. The first is that it exceeds this Court's Judgment.

The Judgment restricts what Mr. Bernecky may possess in exactly three places, and each names a category the law itself defines: controlled substances he may not lawfully possess, firearms and dangerous weapons, and child pornography. Dkt. No. 28, Mandatory Condition 2, Standard Condition 10, Special Condition (j). Special Conditions ¶ 8 adds a fourth kind of contraband to a federal sentence, and it is the only one of the four whose contents no statute, no regulation, and no order of this Court defines. Its boundary is fixed by its own last five words — "as defined by my treatment staff" — which is to say, by the people who report him.

The Second Circuit requires the opposite. A subjective element in a condition is permissible only "if tethered to objective criteria." *United States v. Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77, 81 (2d Cir. 2010). *Reeves* vacated a condition because "[n]o source provides anyone — courts, probation officers, prosecutors, law enforcement officers, or Reeves himself — with guidance" as to what it covered, and because a supervisee's "continued freedom during supervised release should not hinge on the accuracy of his prediction" of how the person judging him would apply it. *Id.* "A condition is too vague if it requires a reasonable person to speculate as to its meaning." *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 536. Special Conditions ¶ 8 does worse than leave the line unmarked. It assigns the drawing of the line to a private party, after the fact, so that whether an ordinary household purchase was contraband is answered only once it has been made.

The third problem is of a different order. A vague term can be fixed by a more careful definition; this one cannot be drafted around, and it would remain even if this Court had imposed Special Conditions ¶ 8 itself, word for word. The paragraph reaches material the First Amendment protects. Nonobscene sexually explicit material involving adults is protected by the Constitution in the hands of ordinary citizens, without regard to its social worth. *United States v. Voelker*, 489 F.3d 139, 151 n.15 (3d Cir. 2007). A condition reaching that material is reviewed accordingly. The Second Circuit "carefully scrutinize[s] unusual and severe conditions," and has said that a condition "prohibiting access to ... legal adult pornography" is one of them and "merit[s] our close examination." *United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d 88, 94-95 (2d Cir. 2019). Such material "receive[s] full First Amendment protection when in the possession of ordinary adults, but may be regulated in the hands of parolees to a much greater extent" — which is why bans of this kind "must be supported on the record by detailed factual findings establishing that the proposed ban is reasonably related to the sentencing factors set forth in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) and that it is reasonably necessary to accomplish their objectives," and why "[a] general reference to 'the public interest' ordinarily will not suffice." *Id.* at 99 (quoting *Farrell v. Burke*, 449 F.3d 470, 497 (2d Cir. 2006)).

Two courts have vacated conditions considerably narrower than this one. The Third Circuit struck a supervised-release condition barring possession of material depicting "sexually explicit conduct" as defined by 18 U.S.C. § 2256(2) — a term anchored to a federal statute, and so not vague — because "there are First Amendment implications for a ban that extends to explicit material involving adults," because the prohibition "still sweeps within its reach some legal adult pornography as well as illegal child pornography," and because nothing in that record suggested adult material "contributed in any way to Voelker's offense." *Voelker*, 489 F.3d at 151, 153. Although *Voelker* is a Third Circuit decision, the principle it states is applicable here. The Second Circuit reached the same result on the same reasoning in *Eaglin*, striking a ban tethered to that identical statutory definition, holding "that a blanket ban on possessing or accessing adult pornography cannot be imposed unless such a ban on adult pornography is reasonably related to the sentencing factors and reasonably necessary to accomplish the goals of sentencing," and faulting the district court for treating adult pornography "as reprehensible in itself, rather than as an otherwise lawful action that violated Eaglin's conditions of supervised release." 913 F.3d at 100. Where such a condition has been sustained, the term was anchored outside the decisionmaker: *United States v. Simmons* upheld one because "federal law provides considerable guidance as to the meaning of the term pornography ... [that] avoids reference to subjective standards." 343 F.3d 72, 81-82 (2d Cir. 2003), quoted in *Reeves*, 591 F.3d at 81. Special Conditions ¶ 8 has neither the statutory anchor *Simmons* described nor the findings *Eaglin* requires, because no court made any. Mr. Bernecky's Judgment forbids him to possess child pornography and says nothing about lawful adult material. Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (j).

*Eaglin* also identified how a clinical judgment of this kind properly enters a sentence: a wholesale ban "might be justified ... where a mental health professional testified that viewing pornography would be detrimental to the defendant's rehabilitation." 913 F.3d at 100. The route runs through testimony a court hears and findings a court makes — not through a paragraph in a form a patient signs at intake. R.K.K.S. is free to hold whatever clinical view it likes about sexually stimulating material. Converting that view into a term of a federal sentence is this Court's work.

The examples make that reach concrete. "Material(s) of any kind" is a word for things as much as for images. A sex toy is a material. So is a condom, a bottle of lubricant, an item of clothing. Each is lawful to buy in any pharmacy in this district, and each is capable of being called sexually stimulating by somebody. The paragraph reaches purchase and possession as well as viewing, which is the grammar of a restriction on objects. No court has ever found that a condom or a bottle of lubricant bears on Mr. Bernecky's risk of reoffending, because no court was ever asked. And a man cannot speculate his way to safety when the meaning is supplied only after the purchase is made.

Special Conditions ¶ 2 is the second of the two, and it is the one the Second Circuit has already vacated in substance: "I understand that I am required to disclose all my intimate partners to treatment staff. I understand that I need to provide acceptable proof of informed consent to treatment staff which includes disclosure of my offense history in full prior to engaging in a sexual relationship." *Reeves* struck a supervised-release condition obligating a defendant convicted of possessing child pornography, on entering a significant romantic relationship, to notify Probation and disclose his sex-offense history to the other party. The panel was "hard-pressed to see how the notification requirement is reasonably necessary to protect someone with whom Reeves might choose to associate," and found it would work against the purpose it claimed to serve: it "would almost certainly adversely affect, and could very well prematurely end, any intimate relationship he might develop, placing him at a greater risk of social isolation and thus impair, rather than enhance, his rehabilitation." 591 F.3d at 81-82. That is not a prediction here. Mr. Bernecky goes to work and goes home, and forms no relationships, because he will not face that disclosure and what follows it. Certification ¶ \_\_. The paragraph also demands something the law of consent does not produce: "acceptable proof," graded by staff, that another adult gave "informed consent" to a relationship. And it assigns to staff a decision the Second Circuit has placed with the judge. The only notification duty in Mr. Bernecky's sentence is Standard Condition 12, and the Second Circuit vacated that language in *United States v. Boles* as "vague and afford[ing] too much discretion to the probation officer," holding that "the [district] court must determine" whether notification is required. 914 F.3d 95, 111-12 (2d Cir. 2019) (quoting *United States v. Peterson*, 248 F.3d 79, 86 (2d Cir. 2001)); *see* Part VII, *infra*. He owes no one a disclosure about himself until this Court makes that determination, and a form signed at intake cannot make it in advance.

The pattern repeats, and the Court need not resolve each instance to grant the relief sought. Immediate discharge follows from "other problem behavior not outlined in this Treatment Contract, as determined in the sole discretion of the staff of the Practice," and from bringing "any item that may be used as a weapon." Ex. B, Consequences ¶ 2. He must report "any relapse in problematic behavior" within twenty-four hours and disclose changes in "criminal behaviors," while Consequences ¶ 1 routes what he says to his supervising officer and, if staff think it necessary, to law enforcement — the compelled-disclosure problem addressed in Part IV, *infra*. Ex. B, Code of Conduct ¶¶ 5-6; Consequences ¶ 1.[^ii-3] The document also makes "computer based sexual interest assessments" a term of treatment, though nothing in the Judgment mentions them; the profession that supplies this treatment treats such testing as requiring the client's specific informed consent, obtained for the procedure itself.[^ii-1] Mr. Bernecky will not submit to that testing.[^ii-4]

The rule that keeps this power in judicial hands exists because a sentence is built for the person who has to serve it. "A district court is required to make an individualized assessment when determining whether to impose a special condition of supervised release, and to state on the record the reason for imposing it; the failure to do so is error." *United States v. Betts*, 886 F.3d 198, 202 (2d Cir. 2018); *accord Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 536. The face of this document is the reason that rule exists. Its individualization blank reads: "I agree to abide by the following special conditions: No special conditions designated." Ex. B. The section containing that blank opens with a term written for somebody else entirely — a person "under pretrial supervision or pretrial diversion," a status Mr. Bernecky left behind at his conviction in 2018. *Id.*, Special Conditions ¶ 1. Nothing in the space reserved for this patient, and a leftover clause from a stranger's case at the top of it. That is not the execution of an individualized order this Court entered about this defendant.

None of this asks the Court to run another party's business. R.K.K.S. is a private practice and may condition admission to its program on whatever terms it likes; if it will not treat a patient who declines to sign, that is its right. What follows from that right is an obligation on Probation. Special Condition (b) makes the selection of a provider Probation's job, and the provider Probation selects should be one willing to treat Mr. Bernecky within the scaffolding of this Court's Judgment. Mr. Bernecky does not ask this Court to edit a clinician's paperwork or to tell a therapist how to run a group. He asks for a ruling on what that paperwork is not, and for a direction that the treatment his sentence orders be delivered inside the boundaries that sentence set.

That boundary serves the treatment as well as the Judgment. A provider holding a supervisee's liberty in reserve never has to persuade him of anything; the rules enforce themselves, and an arrest stands behind each of them. This document adopts that model wholesale. It makes a private company's discretion — over what counts as a "sexually stimulating material," over what counts as "problem behavior," over whether a patient stays or goes — the engine of compliance, and leaves the clinician nothing to do but announce the rule. The profession that supplies this treatment builds its guidance the other way, directing that a written treatment plan specify the "[t]herapeutic styles that can optimize motivation, engagement, and therapeutic alliance." *ATSA Best Practice Guidelines for Men* Guideline 10.02, at 52-53 (Ex. C). Mr. Bernecky is asking to be treated by a clinician whose working leverage is a therapeutic one, and to leave the coercive leverage where the law placed it — in a judgment, entered by a judge, after a hearing.

The reason to say it now is written into the document. Consequences ¶ 1 provides that "my supervising officer will be notified of any violation of this Treatment Contract," and Consequences ¶ 3 makes "violation of the terms of this Treatment Contract" a ground for discharge. Discharge is where the harm lands, and it lands on the Judgment directly: Special Condition (b) directs that Mr. Bernecky "is not to leave treatment until complete or as ordered by the Court," and Special Condition (h) that he is "not to leave such treatment until completion or as ordered by the Court." Dkt. No. 28. A patient the Practice puts out has left treatment neither on completion nor on this Court's order. A private company's definition of a breach is thus the first step of a federal violation proceeding, and a petition is the second.

That sequence is not a forecast. It is what happened to Browder. His provider terminated him because he objected to a term of its agreement; the Probation Office petitioned, charging that he had refused to sign the agreement that would have let him receive the counseling the court had ordered; the government moved successfully to detain him; and after a hearing the district court found the violation proven. *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 508; *Browder* Remand Order. The Second Circuit then held that Browder had been right to refuse. 866 F.3d at 505-06, 514. Rule 32.1's drafters addressed that sequence: "the probationer should be able to obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it," and "in cases of neglect, overwork, or simply unreasonableness on the part of the probation officer, the probationer should have recourse to the sentencing court when a condition needs clarification or modification." Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's note to subdiv. (b) (1979). Where Probation or its designee "does overstep [its] authority," the supervisee may "seek recourse before the sentencing court." *United States v. Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d 147, 153 (2d Cir. 2020), quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68. Browder took the other route. He was vindicated — after he had been detained, found in violation, sentenced, and taken an appeal. Mr. Bernecky is asking for the ruling first.

**Relief requested (Part II):** an order (1) declaring that the R.K.K.S. "Treatment Contract," and any comparable document a provider requires as a condition of admission, is not a condition of supervised release, that no term of it is enforceable against Mr. Bernecky as such, and that no term of it may support a violation petition except to the extent it restates a condition this Court imposed; (2) construing the participation requirement of Special Conditions (b) and (h) to mean that Mr. Bernecky must attend the sessions scheduled for him and engage in the treatment offered, so that a violation of that requirement occurs only upon a knowing failure to attend without sufficient cause or excuse; (3) providing that if a provider discharges him on the provider's own initiative, the Probation Office shall arrange an alternative provider willing to treat him on terms consistent with this Court's Judgment and shall report the discharge to the Court, and that the discharge is not itself a violation of supervision; (4) confirming that he is not required to disclose his offense history to any person, or to obtain or document any third party's consent, unless and until this Court so determines under Standard Condition 12 as amended per Part VII, *infra*; and (5) confirming that no condition of his supervision requires him to submit to computer-based sexual interest testing, or to any physiological testing other than the polygraph and computerized voice stress analyzer examinations Special Condition (g) addresses.

### III. Special Conditions (d) and (i) — Financial Monitoring and Disclosure

Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to limit Special Condition (d) to the records capable of showing what it was written to detect, and to vacate Special Condition (i).

The changed circumstance is the point. Before his incarceration he had nothing, and an objection to a financial-disclosure condition would have been unripe — there were no finances to disclose. He is now in his thirties, employed full time, and his employer offers a 401(k). Certification ¶ \_\_. He wants to save for retirement and to invest, and should not have to seek advance approval to do it or produce brokerage and retirement records to a probation officer.

Special Condition (d) states its own purpose: to monitor compliance "with not buying or subscribing to online services that provide child pornography." No one has ever alleged that Mr. Bernecky purchased child pornography; his convictions are for possession, and nothing in the record suggests his access involved payment. Even taking the stated purpose at face value, the records capable of showing such a payment are a specific set — credit and debit card statements, PayPal or comparable electronic transaction records, and cryptocurrency wallet or exchange records. A 401(k) statement cannot show a payment to a child-pornography vendor. Neither can a brokerage confirmation. And detecting such a payment requires nothing beyond the payee's name: the purpose is served in full once Mr. Bernecky discloses to whom a payment was made, without the amount, the account number, or anything else.

*United States v. Jenkins* struck a condition forbidding a child-pornography defendant from incurring any credit card charge or opening any line of credit without prior approval, holding that "[n]othing in the record suggests these restrictions ... are 'reasonably necessary' ... to protect the public or to deter [him] from continuing to engage in the conduct for which he was convicted — possession of child pornography," and asking why he "should be prohibited from buying a drink on an airplane or taking an Uber ride or making a purchase on Amazon unless the transaction is pre-approved by a probation officer." 854 F.3d 181, 194 (2d Cir. 2017). Special Condition (d) requires disclosure rather than permission, and the two are not the same. But *Jenkins*'s point survives the distinction: a condition responding to a possession offense with no financial element cannot reach further than that offense gives Probation reason to look.

Special Condition (i) is spent. It ties Probation's access to Mr. Bernecky's finances, and its control over his assets, to "the collection of any unpaid fine or restitution." The $500 restitution and the special penalty assessment are paid in full. Certification ¶ \_\_. Nothing remains to collect, and the guideline the condition implements authorizes a condition of this kind only "if the court imposes an order of restitution, forfeiture, or notice to victims, or orders the defendant to pay a fine," U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(3), with the companion debt-obligation condition applying only where the defendant is not "in compliance with the payment schedule," *id.* § 5D1.3(d)(2). The condition should be vacated.

**Relief requested (Part III):** an order (1) clarifying that Special Condition (d) requires disclosure only of the payee's name for transactions made by credit card, debit card, PayPal or comparable electronic payment service, or cryptocurrency, and does not reach the amount of any transaction, any account number, or any brokerage, investment, or retirement account record; (2) confirming that neither condition requires advance approval for Mr. Bernecky to participate in an employer-sponsored retirement plan, to open or fund a brokerage account, or to select investments; and (3) vacating Special Condition (i).

### IV. Special Condition (g) — Polygraph and Computerized Voice Stress Analyzer Testing

The Fifth Amendment protects the right to refuse to answer an incriminating question at the moment the question is asked. Special Condition (g) takes that right away and hands back a receipt. It commands that Mr. Bernecky "shall answer the questions posed during the examination," and offers in exchange a preserved right to argue, later and somewhere unspecified, about what may be done with the answer. The Court should strike the command.

Three sentences should go: "The defendant shall answer the questions posed during the examination, subject to the defendant's right to challenge in a court of law the use of such statements as violations of the defendant's Fifth Amendment rights"; "In this regard, the defendant shall be deemed not to have waived the defendant's Fifth Amendment rights by making any such statements"; and "The results of any polygraph pre-tests and polygraph examinations may be disclosed to the US Probation office and the Court, but shall not be further disclosed without a court order." The third is surplusage once the first two are gone; it exists only to govern what becomes of compelled statements. Everything else stands, including the command that Mr. Bernecky *submit to* testing on the schedule the condition sets.

The condition reaches both instruments it names. It authorizes "examinations using a polygraph, computerized voice stress analyzer, or other similar device," and the compelled-answer sentence applies to all of them without distinction. The constitutional injury is the command to answer, and a voice stress analyzer extracts the answer the same way a polygraph does. Everything said below applies with identical force to CVSA examinations and to any successor device the condition's "other similar device" language would reach.

**The governing rule.** Compulsion exists where the government, "either expressly or by implication, asserts that invocation of the privilege would lead to revocation" or another substantial penalty. *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 435 (1984). Murphy lost, and the two reasons he lost are the two reasons this condition is different. Murphy's condition said nothing about his freedom to decline to answer — it required truthfulness if he spoke, not an answer to every question put to him — and nothing in his case, express or implied, threatened revocation for invoking. Special Condition (g) is the mirror image on both. It speaks directly to Mr. Bernecky's freedom to decline, and commands that he "shall answer." And it is a condition of supervised release, so failing to comply is by definition a violation. The threatened penalty is not something an officer would have to add in the room. It is in the condition's own grammar.

That matters because of what invoking has always required. A witness invokes by declining to answer, with nothing further required of him at that moment; the validity of the claim is for a court to assess afterward if challenged. *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951). Special Condition (g) tells Mr. Bernecky that the single act the Constitution has always said suffices to protect him is itself a violation of his supervision. That is *Murphy*'s penalty situation reached by no analogy at all.

**The savings clause is not immunity.** *Von Behren* held that "a threat to revoke one's probation for properly invoking his Fifth Amendment privilege is the type of compulsion the state may not constitutionally impose," and identified in footnote 9 the three circumstances that would change the outcome: if the defendant "had refused to answer a question relevant only to his supervised release status ..., or if he had refused to answer a question that did not involve the risk of self-incrimination, or if he were given immunity and still refused to answer any questions." 822 F.3d at 1149 n.9, 1150.

Special Condition (g) supplies none of the three, and the third is where the difference lives. Immunity is a promise that an answer and its fruits may not be used against the speaker — what *Murphy* itself contemplated when it said the government may "insist on answers to even incriminating questions and hence sensibly administer its probation system, as long as it recognizes that the required answers may not be used in a criminal proceeding." 465 U.S. at 435 n.7. What Special Condition (g) supplies instead is a preserved right to litigate. A defendant given immunity has lost nothing by answering. A defendant given a right to litigate has lost the disclosure itself and keeps only an argument about what may be done with it.

**And immunity, to substitute for the privilege, has to be as large as the privilege it displaces.** The Supreme Court's rule is one of coextension: "such immunity from use and derivative use is coextensive with the scope of the privilege against self-incrimination, and therefore is sufficient to compel testimony over a claim of the privilege." *Kastigar v. United States*, 406 U.S. 441, 462 (1972). The measure is the privilege itself — and the privilege is not bounded by sovereign. It "protects a state witness against incrimination under federal as well as state law and a federal witness against incrimination under state as well as federal law." *Murphy v. Waterfront Comm'n of N.Y. Harbor*, 378 U.S. 52, 77-78 (1964) (*Waterfront Commission*). A promise that binds one prosecutor, or one sovereign, or one proceeding does not remove the hazard; it relocates it. Whatever the United States Attorney for this District may promise about a federal prosecution leaves a New York prosecutor free to use the same words.

That is not hypothetical here. This Judgment itself directs that Mr. Bernecky's presentence report be released to the New York State Board of Examiners of Sex Offenders and to a New York county court. Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (e). Material generated in this federal supervision is built to travel to a state forum. A savings clause preserving a right to argue "in a court of law" — without identifying which court, or which sovereign's — is not coextensive with anything.

**The threshold that triggers the privilege is low.** A statement is incriminating if it would furnish "a lead or a link in the chain of evidence that could lead to prosecution." *Von Behren*, 822 F.3d at 1144 (quoting *Maness v. Meyers*, 419 U.S. 449, 461 (1975)). No active investigation is required. *United States v. McDonald*, No. 1:11-CR-01075-MV-1, slip op. at 6 (D.N.M. Jan. 6, 2026). Once a person has a valid basis to invoke, the government may not compel the answer at all — not now, on the promise that a court will decide later whether it should have. A later ruling that a statement is inadmissible cannot make an investigator un-know what the answer told him or stop him from following where it leads.

**The two decisions closest to this question turn on a verb this condition does not use.** In *United States v. Richards*, the Tenth Circuit upheld a condition requiring the defendant "to submit to periodic polygraph testing," on a record where nothing showed the government had threatened revocation for a valid invocation, and where the condition itself barred use of polygraph results "for purposes of instituting or instigating criminal charges." 958 F.3d 961, 964, 966-68 (10th Cir. 2020). Judge Vázquez reached the same result as to a condition requiring the defendant to "submit to clinical polygraph examinations," in terms that decide this case: a defendant "may be required to 'submit to clinical polygraph examinations,' but that does not necessarily translate to 'truthfully answer even incriminating questions during mandatory clinical polygraph examinations.'" *McDonald*, slip op. at 11. Special Condition (g) closes that gap by its own terms. It does not tell Mr. Bernecky to submit to an examination; it tells him to answer the questions posed during one. There is no reading of "shall answer" that leaves open the room to decline that "submit to" leaves open. Both courts also left the door open to exactly this motion: *Richards* invited a renewed challenge if the government's position changed, 958 F.3d at 968, and *McDonald* said the court "would consider adding language akin to that approved in *Richards*" on a motion to modify, slip op. at 13.

The Second Circuit has not decided this question. Its one polygraph decision, *United States v. Johnson*, upheld language close enough to Special Condition (g) that the resemblance is unlikely to be coincidental. 446 F.3d 272 (2d Cir. 2006). But the theory presented there was narrower: Johnson argued that, absent formal use and derivative-use immunity, he faced an unconstitutional choice between confessing to past conduct and standing in violation for silence. Nobody in *Johnson* argued that compelling the answer itself, at the moment it is demanded, is the constitutional violation. That is Mr. Bernecky's theory, and *Johnson* never reached it.

**What the Supreme Court said this June about constitutionally infirm supervised-release conditions.** In *Hunter v. United States*, decided June 18, 2026, the Court "approve[d] the majority view among the courts of appeals that an appeal waiver is unenforceable when it would result in a miscarriage of justice," a standard it defined by saying that a "waiver may be set aside only if the sentence is marred by the kind of egregious error that would bring the judicial system into disrepute." 608 U.S. \_\_\_, No. 24-1063, slip op. at 11 (2026). The Court then gave three illustrations of the error it meant. The second: "a defendant may appeal a sentence that is infected with a blatant constitutional error, such as when a judge takes account of a constitutionally impermissible factor (like race) or imposes a constitutionally infirm condition of supervised release." *Id.* at 12. Justice Gorsuch, concurring, read that category to reach "a condition of release that violates his right to be free from forced medication, or a condition that violates his right to speak or worship freely, or any other condition that violates one of his recognized constitutional rights." *Id.* at 10 (Gorsuch, J., concurring).

The Court set a high bar and said so, and Justice Kavanaugh wrote separately, joined by two colleagues, to underscore it. Mr. Bernecky asks for considerably less than *Hunter* gave. He asks no court to disregard a waiver, a default, or any procedural bar; he asks this Court to exercise authority Congress conferred by statute on a motion Congress said may be made "at any time." What *Hunter* supplies is the Supreme Court's own placement of a constitutionally infirm supervised-release condition — alongside racial bias in sentencing — in the small category of errors the judicial system cannot leave standing. A district court holding express statutory power to fix such a condition, before anyone has been arrested over it, is the least costly moment in the system to do so.

**Why now.** Nothing about waiver doctrine, and nothing this Court can say about timing, will change what Mr. Bernecky intends to do: he will invoke the privilege question by question, at the moment each question is asked, and he will not answer under compulsion. What makes this urgent is not a scheduled examination. It is that his treatment statements are already being used against him, in a proceeding under way today.

Mr. Bernecky is a respondent in a New York Sex Offender Registration Act risk-level proceeding in Monroe County Court. Certification ¶ \_\_. Statements he made in treatment are discussed at length in the materials before that court and are being used to argue for a higher risk classification. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. That is present-tense, documented use of his treatment disclosures against him in another forum, by another sovereign — the hazard *Waterfront Commission* holds the privilege reaches, and the one a savings clause preserving a right to argue "in a court of law" does nothing to prevent. A man watching his treatment statements be used against him in one proceeding, while a condition of his supervision commands him to make more of them, has every reason to stop talking and every right to.

**The warning nobody gave him.** The profession that supplies sex offense-specific treatment has confronted this exact dilemma in the polygraph setting and prescribed a remedy this condition does not contain. Its practice standards direct that members "must ensure that limits of confidentiality are fully disclosed to clients prior to polygraph testing, and that clients are afforded the opportunity for informed consent, specifically with respect to the ways in which the findings will be used and to whom the findings will be provided," because "[c]lient disclosures of potentially incriminating information to mandated reporters can ... lead to future prosecution." ATSA *Practice Guidelines* App. B (2014); *see* Part II, *supra*, n.1 (full citation). What follows is a writing requirement: "Members inform clients, in writing, of this potential dilemma and how it is addressed in their jurisdiction and program." *Id.* The current edition adds that "it is not the role of treatment providers to attempt to determine or verify a client's legal guilt or innocence or to secure confessions of unreported or undetected sexually abusive behaviors," and that the influence of denial and minimization "in sexual recidivism risk has not yet been clearly established." ATSA *Best Practice Guidelines for Men* Guidelines 12.05, at 56, and 12.04, at 55 (6th ed. 2025) (Ex. C). Mr. Bernecky has never been given that warning, in writing or otherwise, by anyone. He does not ask this Court to enforce a professional guideline against anybody, and nothing in this record establishes that any clinician treating him belongs to that association. He offers it for a narrower purpose. The profession that administers these examinations regards a written warning about criminal exposure as the least a man is owed before he answers. Special Condition (g) supplies no warning and commands the answer.

**The counsel problem.** Whether a particular invocation of the privilege is valid is a question of law. *Hoffman*, 341 U.S. at 486. Mr. Bernecky is not a lawyer, and neither is the examiner across the table. He cannot make that determination in real time, and the cost of getting it wrong is a violation of supervision. So when any question is put to him under this condition — by an examiner, a treatment provider, or a probation officer — he will move at that moment for appointment of counsel under the Criminal Justice Act. Absent counsel actually available to him, he will invoke as to every question, because a layperson forced to decide question by question which answers are safe has no accurate way to do it and no obligation to guess. Congress has already made proceedings of this kind a mandatory-appointment category: representation "shall be provided for any financially eligible person who ... faces modification, reduction, or enlargement of a condition, or extension or revocation of a term of supervised release." 18 U.S.C. § 3006A(a)(1)(E). Mr. Bernecky requests appointment of counsel for this motion on that basis.

**What happens if this is not resolved now.** If the government or the Probation Office responds to a refusal with a violation report, an arrest, or a revocation hearing, Mr. Bernecky will raise this identical argument at that point, and this Court will decide this identical question — after a warrant, after a detention, and after the case has become a revocation proceeding. Rule 32.1's drafters wrote the answer to that sequence: a supervisee "should be able to obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it." Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's note to subdiv. (b) (1979). The Judiciary's own policy directs the officer administering this condition the same way: "[i]f the person on supervision refuses to answer a specific question on the grounds that it is self-incriminating, the officer should not compel (e.g., through threat of revocation) the person to answer the question," and where "there is uncertainty about whether the invocation of the privilege against self-incrimination is valid ... the probation officer should refer the matter to the court to make this determination." Guide to Judiciary Policy, Vol. 8E, § 250(b). Special Condition (g)'s "shall answer" does not merely omit that safeguard. It instructs the officer to do what the policy tells him not to do.

Two points bear on cost rather than entitlement. Whatever verification interest polygraph testing serves, it is not answering any demonstrated problem here: the record contains no instance of dishonesty by Mr. Bernecky toward a probation officer, a treatment provider, or this Court. And his record raises the stakes rather than lowering them — a compelled answer from a defendant with a prior conviction of this kind carries materially greater consequences than the same answer from a first offender, which is precisely why the protection has to operate at the moment of the question rather than after it.

**Relief requested (Part IV):** an order striking, in full and without replacement, Special Condition (g)'s compelled-answer sentence, its non-waiver sentence, and the now-surplus disclosure sentence, leaving Mr. Bernecky's obligation to *submit to* polygraph and CVSA testing intact; in the alternative, an order providing that he may invoke the privilege question by question without penalty, that no violation report may be filed based on a refusal to answer, and that any dispute about the validity of a particular invocation be referred to this Court; and appointment of counsel under 18 U.S.C. § 3006A(a)(1)(E).

### V. Special Condition (h) — Alcohol Abstinence

Special Condition (h) requires abstinence from alcohol "[w]hile in treatment or taking psychotropic medication." The abstinence requirement should attach when a physician says it should, and not otherwise.

The clause is live right now, and nobody is administering it. Mr. Bernecky is in treatment and is taking prescribed medication, so the condition's disjunctive trigger is satisfied twice over. Certification ¶ \_\_. No one — not his probation officer, not his treatment provider, not his prescriber — has told him he may not have any alcohol. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. Read literally, a total ban is in force and he is violating it whenever he has a beer. Read as anyone administering it appears to read it, nothing is in force at all. That gap between text and practice is the same defect running through the rest of this motion, and it is present, not projected.

Nothing in this record ties alcohol to Mr. Bernecky's offense, to his history, or to any violation. His offense involved images on a device. The Second Circuit vacated a comparable blanket restriction for exactly that gap. *United States v. Betts*, 886 F.3d 198, 202-03 (2d Cir. 2018). It has since applied the same rule to a nearly identical "during treatment" abstinence clause. *United States v. Rodriguez*, No. 22-1820-cr (2d Cir. Dec. 7, 2023) (summary order).

The wording compounds the problem. Because the trigger is disjunctive, talk therapy alone requires abstinence even though no interaction risk can exist without a medication. And treating "psychotropic medication" as one uniform category ignores that alcohol-interaction risk varies sharply by drug class. This clause is this District's standard template, not a finding made about Mr. Bernecky.

**Relief requested (Part V):** an order amending Special Condition (h) so that the alcohol-abstinence requirement applies only upon, and only for as long as, a treating physician determines that alcohol is contraindicated for a specific medication or treatment Mr. Bernecky is actually receiving.

### VI. Standard Conditions 5 and 8 — Residence Approval and Contact with Persons Convicted of Felonies

Standard Condition 5 requires Mr. Bernecky to live at a place "approved by the probation officer." Standard Condition 8 forbids him to knowingly communicate or interact with anyone he knows has a felony conviction without advance permission. Each tells him permission is required. Neither says what earns it.

**The residence condition needs a stated standard.** The changed circumstance is straightforward: supervision began in July 2026, so this problem could not have arisen, let alone been raised, in 2018. Since it began, no one has told Mr. Bernecky what standard governs approval of a residence, and he has looked for one himself and been unable to find it. Certification ¶ \_\_. Because nothing defines what satisfies "approved," an officer may condition approval on anything, and he has no way to show he has met a requirement nobody has stated. "A condition is too vague if it requires a reasonable person to speculate as to its meaning." *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 536. It is also a delegation problem: a court "may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122. The only articulated basis Probation has offered anywhere is that "the probation officer assesses whether the change is appropriate to meet the defendant's risk and needs and whether it poses any risk to the defendant or to others" — broad enough to justify any outcome and too vague to guide any. U.S. Courts, *Overview of Probation and Supervised Release Conditions*, ch. 2, § V (July 2024). Probation knows how to write a real standard when it wants one: its own guidelines require a written plan, supervisory approval, and a defined reasonable-suspicion showing before a search of Mr. Bernecky's home. Guide to Judiciary Policy, Vol. 8E, Appx. 3A. Nothing comparable governs a decision that can put him out of it.

**The felon-contact condition should require disclosure, not approval.** Mr. Bernecky raises this condition because he does not know what it requires of him, and the answer should come from the Court rather than from an accumulation of guesses made one encounter at a time.

The conditions of his life since release make that concrete. He lives in an apartment complex with numerous neighbors who have felony convictions. He is required to attend a counseling and support group composed of people convicted of felonies. He works, shops, and rides public transit in a society in which roughly eight percent of the adult population has a felony conviction. Sarah K.S. Shannon et al., *The Growth, Scope, and Spatial Distribution of People With Felony Records in the United States, 1948-2010*, 54 Demography 1795 (2017). Read as written, Standard Condition 8 requires him to obtain advance permission before saying good morning in a hallway.

The condition's premise also does not describe this offense or this defendant. Advance-approval gating of associations fits crimes committed by groups, in concert, or in conspiracy, where association with other offenders is itself the mechanism of the offense. Possession of child pornography is not that crime. Mr. Bernecky committed it alone, in his own home, and his own account of how he came to commit it twice is that he was isolated from other people rather than connected to the wrong ones. Certification ¶ \_\_. The Court then placed him for roughly eight years in a Bureau of Prisons facility where every person around him had a felony conviction, and he formed friendships there with people who encourage him toward a law-abiding life. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. Cutting him off from those people reproduces the circumstance that preceded the offense.

Probation's legitimate interest is in knowing who Mr. Bernecky spends time with and being able to ask about it. Disclosure serves that interest completely.

**Relief requested (Part VI):** an order (1) directing that Mr. Bernecky be provided a written standard governing what will satisfy "approved" under Standard Condition 5, with leave to challenge the substance of any standard Probation proposes; and (2) amending Standard Condition 8 so that he must disclose any ongoing association with a person he knows to have a felony conviction, rather than obtain advance permission for it, with the probation officer retaining the ability to inquire about any such association.

### VII. Standard Condition 12 — Risk Notification

Standard Condition 12, as it appears in Mr. Bernecky's Judgment, provides that "[i]f the probation officer determines that you pose a risk to another person (including an organization), the probation officer may require you to notify the person about the risk and you must comply with that instruction." That is the exact condition the Second Circuit held invalid, agreeing that it "is vague and affords too much discretion to the probation officer" and vacating it as indistinguishable from an employer-notification condition already struck for leaving the obligation to "the probation officer's unfettered discretion." *United States v. Boles*, 914 F.3d 95, 111-12 (2d Cir. 2019) (quoting *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 86).

Within two months of *Boles*, this District entered a standing order directing that this language be removed from every judgment imposing supervision and replaced with a version requiring the court itself to determine, based on "criminal record, personal history and characteristics, and the nature and circumstances of [the] offense," that a defendant poses a risk before any notification requirement attaches. *Amended Standing Order, In re United States v. Boles* (W.D.N.Y. Mar. 22, 2019) (Geraci, C.J.). That order directs amendment of "the Judgment and Commitment order in all criminal cases in which a term of probation or supervised release is imposed." Mr. Bernecky's Judgment was entered nine months earlier and was never conformed to it. At a pre-supervision meeting his probation officer presented him with this exact, superseded language and required him to sign an acknowledgment of it. Certification ¶ \_\_. He asks the Court to apply to him the correction this District has already ordered for everyone else.

He objects separately to the structure the corrected condition creates, and this motion is his first opportunity to do so. *Boles* was decided in January 2019 and the standing order implementing it was entered March 22, 2019. Mr. Bernecky's conviction became final in June 2018. The condition he is objecting to did not exist while any avenue of direct review was open to him; he could not have challenged in 2018 a replacement condition no court had yet written. The objection is raised at the first moment it could be raised, and it is not waived.

Under the standing order, a risk determination is made between the probation officer and the Court. Mr. Bernecky would have no notice of it, no opportunity to be heard, no ability to present evidence, and no ability to contest any assertion made about him — while the consequence is that he must go tell a third party that he is dangerous. Where Probation believes a risk notification is warranted, it should proceed by petition and summons, on notice. And because such a proceeding would determine whether Mr. Bernecky must disclose himself as a danger to a named person, counsel should be appointed for any hearing on it.

**Relief requested (Part VII):** an order (1) amending Standard Condition 12 to match this District's March 22, 2019 standing order following *Boles*; and (2) providing that any risk determination under the amended condition be initiated by petition and summons, with notice to Mr. Bernecky, an opportunity to be heard, and appointment of counsel for any hearing.
