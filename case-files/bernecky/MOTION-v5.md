# DEFENDANT'S MOTION TO MODIFY CONDITIONS OF SUPERVISED RELEASE
## PURSUANT TO 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2), FED. R. CRIM. P. 32.1(c), AND U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4

Defendant Jeffrey Bernecky, proceeding *pro se*, moves under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2) and Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32.1(c) to modify several conditions of supervised release imposed by the Judgment entered June 12, 2018. Dkt. No. 28.

---

## PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

A judgment's silence is not a blank space for someone else to fill in.

That is the whole of this motion. The conditions Mr. Bernecky is serving were written in 2018 for a man nobody would meet for another eight years, at a hearing where the question in the room was how long he would spend in prison. Nobody there was asked what a computer-monitoring clause would mean to a manufacturing company's HR department, or whether a man in an apartment complex may say hello to the neighbor two doors down. The words went onto the page with those questions unasked, and eight years later they are being read by whoever happens to be holding them.

Supervision began on or about July 30, 2026, and the pattern showed itself immediately. An employer that had offered Mr. Bernecky permanent work read Special Condition (a) and asked whether hiring him would open the company's own computer systems to federal monitoring. Nothing in the condition answers that question. He lost about two weeks of pay while it was sorted out informally, at the moment he was leaving Bureau of Prisons custody and taking on a one-year lease. That employer hired him anyway. The next one may not.

Probation also required him to sign a twenty-one-item Computer Monitoring Program Participant Agreement that no judge has ever reviewed, which forbids him the antivirus software his operating system installed at the factory, forbids the private-browsing mode that ships as a default on every major browser, and lets a probation officer decide, by no stated standard, whether he may own a device at all. His personal telephone, on his own carrier plan, now tells him on its lock screen that it belongs to an organization. And Special Condition (g) commands him to answer questions under polygraph, offering in exchange a promise that he may argue about the answer later — which is not what the Fifth Amendment provides.

Mr. Bernecky does not ask this Court to revisit his sentence. He asks it to say what its own order means, now, so that he serves the next ten years knowing what each condition requires rather than learning it one violation report at a time. Congress made that authority available "at any time," 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2), and Rule 32.1's drafters explained why: "the probationer should be able to obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it." Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's note to subdiv. (b) (1979).

## JURISDICTION AND AUTHORITY

This Court may "modify, reduce, or enlarge the conditions of supervised release, at any time prior to the expiration or termination of the term of supervised release." 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2). That authority exists "to account for new or unforeseen circumstances." *United States v. Lussier*, 104 F.3d 32, 36 (2d Cir. 1997).

The Second Circuit has already told a court in this District to use this precise mechanism at this precise moment. *United States v. Balon* involved a W.D.N.Y. defendant and computer-monitoring conditions materially identical to Special Condition (a). The panel held his challenges unripe because "it is currently impossible to predict the state of computer technology at the commencement of Balon's supervised release period," and instructed the district court "to reconsider, at the instance of the government or Balon and in light of *Lifshitz*, the special conditions regarding monitoring of Balon's computer at a time closer to Balon's term of supervised release." 384 F.3d 38, 40, 47 (2d Cir. 2004). It then held expressly "that changing computer technology is an appropriate factor to authorize a modification of supervised release conditions under Section 3583(e)." *Id.* at 47. *Balon* is the case that put Mr. Bernecky here, now.

*Balon* also marks the boundary of the rule that a condition's illegality is not itself a ground for modification. That rule, the panel explained, "held only that a condition of supervised release that was illegal *ab initio* could not be challenged in a Section 3583(e) proceeding and has no applicability to a case where fluid circumstances alter either the need for, or the efficacy of, certain conditions." *Id.* at 47 n.4; *accord United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748, 774 n.14 (2d Cir. 2023).

Circumstances change in law as well as in fact, and both have changed here. On the facts: a Participant Agreement Probation drafted and required eight years after sentencing, a category of consumer devices that did not exist in its current form, an employer dispute, an active state proceeding in which Mr. Bernecky's treatment statements are being used against him, and employment with a retirement benefit. On the law: the authority this motion relies on most heavily postdates his sentencing — *Kunz* in 2023, construing this District's own monitoring program on this identical text; *United States v. Boles*, 914 F.3d 95 (2d Cir. 2019), and this District's standing order implementing it, both from 2019; *United States v. Brown*, 173 F.4th 45 (2d Cir. 2026); and *Hunter v. United States*, 608 U.S. \_\_\_, No. 24-1063 (June 18, 2026). Judge Larimer could not have applied in 2018 authority that did not exist until years later.

The Sentencing Commission's 2025 amendment is itself such a change, and it was written for this motion's exact posture. Effective November 1, 2025, U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a) invites courts to conduct "an individualized assessment of the appropriateness of existing conditions ... in consultation with the probation officer after the defendant's release from imprisonment," because conditions "are imposed at original sentencing, often years before the defendant begins supervision," and circumstances "may change in and after prison, such that the original term and conditions may no longer be appropriate."

Where Probation has read a condition to reach further than this Court's own text authorizes, this Court supplies the remedy. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68 (citing *United States v. Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d 147, 153 (2d Cir. 2020), for the rule that where Probation "does overstep [its] authority," the sentencing court may provide relief).

## ARGUMENT

### I. Special Condition (a) — The Computer and Internet Monitoring Program

Special Condition (a) requires "advance notification of any computer(s), automated service(s), or connected device(s) that will be used during the term of supervision"; conditions Mr. Bernecky's use of any "internet capable device" on participation in CIMP; authorizes Probation to "install any application as necessary to surveil all activity" on devices he "own[s] or operate[s]"; authorizes examination of equipment he "own[s] or use[s]" when triggered by "impermissible/suspicious activity"; and directs that monitoring "be designed to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information." Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (a). This is the same text the Second Circuit construed on appeal from this District in *Kunz*. 68 F.4th at 754-55.

#### A. The Judgment requires Mr. Bernecky to hold a job and report it. It restricts no employment, and Probation may not add what the Court left out.

While housed at the Rochester Residential Reentry Center, Mr. Bernecky took a temporary placement with a contact-lens manufacturer, which invited him to apply for permanent work at the end of its customary ninety-to-one-hundred-twenty-day period. Certification ¶ \_\_. He disclosed his conviction and his conditions, and handed over the Judgment itself when a general description did not answer the company's questions. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. Human resources asked whether Special Condition (a) obligated the company to open its own computer systems to federal monitoring because Mr. Bernecky was on its payroll. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. He asked his probation officer. She answered that whatever the company put in place would have to satisfy an unspecified federal standard, and told him the company owed a duty to monitor him beyond its own internal procedures. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. Mr. Bernecky was not present for whatever the officer said to the company directly, and he does not purport to report it. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. The company told him not to report until the matter was resolved. A week later the officer said an account without internet access, paired with the company's own monitoring, would satisfy her. He was hired, roughly two weeks of pay later. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. None of that appears in any order of this Court or in any document Mr. Bernecky or his employer has ever been shown. *Id.* ¶ \_\_.

Congress permitted a sentencing court to order a defendant to "refrain, in the case of an individual, from engaging in a specified occupation, business, or profession bearing a reasonably direct relationship to the conduct constituting the offense," or to engage in one "only to a stated degree or under stated circumstances." 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(5); *see* § 3583(d). The implementing Guideline states the findings the statute presupposes: a "reasonably direct relationship" between the occupation and "the conduct relevant to the offense of conviction," and that the restriction "is reasonably necessary to protect the public because there is reason to believe that, absent such restriction, the defendant will continue to engage in unlawful conduct similar to that for which the defendant was convicted." U.S.S.G. § 5F1.5(a). Those are not Guidelines formalities layered onto a statute. They are the statutory standard, written out.

Nobody asked for an occupational restriction at sentencing. Neither the government nor the Probation Office requested one, so this Court was never called on to make either finding, and neither appears in the Judgment, in any Statement of Reasons, or in any filing by either party. Mr. Bernecky's offense had no occupational element of any sort — the images sat on devices he bought in his own name and kept in his own home, and no employer, workplace, or work account appears anywhere in the conduct of conviction. Certification ¶ \_\_.

The Judgment is not silent about employment. It addresses employment twice, and each time imposes a reporting duty on Mr. Bernecky rather than a restriction on his job or an obligation on anyone who hires him. Standard Condition 7 requires him to work full time "(at least 30 hours per week) at a lawful type of employment" and to notify his probation officer before changing where he works or what he does there. Special Condition (e) requires him to register in any state where he "is employed." Dkt. No. 28. Standard Condition 12, the closest the Judgment comes to an employer-facing provision, runs the same way: the duty it creates is Mr. Bernecky's. *Id.*; *see* Part VII, *infra*.

That is *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*, a rule for reading legal texts rather than a statutes-only instrument. Scalia and Garner collect it among the principles governing constitutional, statutory, and contractual interpretation alike. Antonin Scalia & Bryan A. Garner, *Reading Law: The Interpretation of Legal Texts* \_\_ (2012) [VERIFY pincite]. The Second Circuit applies it to private agreements as a matter of course. Asked whether "a Collective Bargaining Agreement [that] enumerates those matters subject to arbitration" impliedly excludes the matters it does not enumerate, the court found the answer "in the Latin maxim expressio unius est exclusio alterius — specific inclusion of one thing is the exclusion of another." *Cornell Univ. v. UAW Local 2300*, 942 F.2d 138, 139 (2d Cir. 1991).

If the canon carries that weight in a bargained agreement, it carries at least as much in a criminal judgment. A contract is written by two parties at arm's length, and a court construing one is reconstructing a shared intention from a document both sides wrote. A judgment has no second author. It is an enumeration by the only actor with power to impose any item on it, and every item had to be separately justified before it could be written down. A statute, for its part, is a general grant of authority drafted for everyone; a sentence is a list of specific deprivations one court found warranted, one at a time, for one person. The more justification an instrument demands for each item it contains, the less likely it is that an item absent from it is absent by accident.

The canon does require a closed series carrying no sign it was meant to be open, which is why the Supreme Court declined to apply it to a statutory list introduced by "may include." *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Echazabal*, 536 U.S. 73, 80-81 (2002). This Judgment is the opposite kind of document — Special Conditions (a) through (j), each separately lettered and separately drafted, with no "including," no "such as," and nothing else inviting additions. An occupational restriction is missing from that list because none was imposed.

What the Court did not impose, Probation may not add. "A district court may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *United States v. Matta*, 777 F.3d 116, 122 (2d Cir. 2015), quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 762-63. Program terms reaching past what the court's condition says "exceed[] the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court, and therefore may not be enforced as written," *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68, and a term that "would contradict, rather than execute" the sentence pronounced is one "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose," *United States v. Browder*, 866 F.3d 504, 514 (2d Cir. 2017). On the precise question here — what an employer may be told — the sentencing court "must determine" the answer and "may not simply leave" it to a probation officer's "unfettered discretion." *United States v. Peterson*, 248 F.3d 79, 85-86 (2d Cir. 2001) (per curiam).

The officer went past even that. She told Mr. Bernecky that his employer owed the Probation Office a duty to monitor him beyond the company's own internal procedures. Certification ¶ \_\_. This Court's power over that company is zero. It is not a party, it was never heard, and it holds Fourth Amendment rights of its own in its own premises and systems: the Warrant Clause "protects commercial buildings as well as private homes." *Marshall v. Barlow's, Inc.*, 436 U.S. 307, 311 (1978). No wording in a criminal judgment against Mr. Bernecky could authorize a probation officer to direct it, because a court cannot strip a company of its constitutional protections in a case it is not part of.

*Brown* will not carry the weight the government may put on it. There the Second Circuit reviewed a condition that expressly subjected devices Brown could use "in the course of employment" to "monitoring restrictions as permitted by the employer," and held it was not an occupational restriction under § 5F1.5(a) because it did not "target 'a specified occupation, business, or profession.'" 173 F.4th at 47, 50-51. *Brown* tells a court what to make of a condition that speaks to employment. It does not turn a condition that never mentions employment into one that does.

The Tenth Circuit has already run this problem to its end. Reviewing monitoring conditions whose warning clause reached "any such computer the defendant may have access," it held in *United States v. Mike* that "the probation officer could reasonably believe that the computer monitoring conditions apply not only to computers that Mike owns, but also to those owned by others, including Mike's employer." It remanded "to clarify their scope," instructing that if the district court meant that reach, "it must make the necessary findings." 632 F.3d 686, 694-95, 699 (10th Cir. 2011). On remand the district court entered an amended judgment "to clarify [the] computer monitoring condition of supervision pursuant to remand," tied every monitoring authorization to the defendant's own equipment, and deleted that clause. Amended Judgment, *United States v. Mike*, No. 1:07-cr-01334-WJ (D.N.M. Mar. 18, 2011), ECF No. 99, at 1, 6-7 (Johnson, J.). It made no finding extending the conditions to employment.

That belief — that a monitoring condition reaches computers "owned by others, including [the defendant's] employer" — is the one Mr. Bernecky's probation officer acted on here. Disposing of it took the Tenth Circuit a remand and the district court an amended judgment. This Court can dispose of it in a single order, and it should do so generally rather than patch the problem for one company, because the next employer will ask what this one asked.

**Relief requested (Part I.A):** an order (1) disclaiming the interpretation *Mike* identified — confirming that Special Condition (a) does not apply to Mr. Bernecky's employment in any respect, reaches no computer, account, or system owned by an employer, and imposes no obligation on any employer; (2) directing the Probation Office to advise his employer in writing that it owes the Probation Office nothing under this Judgment; and (3) confirming that the Judgment's silence on a subject creates no restriction on that subject, and that Probation may not treat such silence as authorization.

#### B. The Judgment requires notice of devices. The CIMP Agreement converts notice into permission, and adds four more restrictions the Judgment never authorized.

Begin with what the Judgment actually commands. Mr. Bernecky "must provide the U.S. Probation Office advance notification of any computer(s), automated service(s), or connected device(s) that will be used during the term of supervision." Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (a). Notice is the obligation the sentencing court imposed, and notice gives Probation complete visibility into what he has. Everything at issue below is what Probation added on top of it.

Exhibit A is a form Probation drafted for its entire CIMP caseload and required Mr. Bernecky to sign at intake in 2026. Whether or not some version existed in 2018, it was not entered into evidence and not attached to the presentence report; it was never before the sentencing court in any form. The objection is doctrinal: an agreement built for blanket use cannot enlarge conditions that were supposed to be individualized.

*Kunz* supplies the framework, on this identical form. Where a CIMP provision is ambiguous, or would demand something absurd read with total literalism, the court construes it narrowly rather than striking it. 68 F.4th at 762-66. Where a provision cannot be reconciled with the special condition's own text, it "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court" and "may not be enforced as written." *Id.* at 767-68. Five terms need attention.

**Item 3 — device authorization.** Item 2 requires the advance notice the Judgment already requires. Ex. A ¶ 2. Item 3 goes further: it forbids Mr. Bernecky to "use, own, or access any device which allows internet access other than what I have been authorized to use by the Probation Office," listing "internet appliances, mobile telephones, tablets, electronic games, gaming consoles, blu ray players, and SMART TV's." Ex. A ¶ 3. It states no standard for what earns authorization, no limit on what may be withheld, and it sorts devices by product label rather than by what they do. "Tablet" covers a general-purpose computer and equally covers an e-reader that can retrieve nothing but books from one vendor's store, or a child's device with no browser at all. Item 2 already achieves everything Special Condition (a) authorizes. Item 3 converts notice into permission.

*Kunz* struck down the same defect in this same program. It confronted a CIMP clause under which Probation could have limited the defendant to one internet-capable device and held that the clause exceeded the authority the district court delegated, because such a restriction "would pose a significant burden on his liberty, and therefore would need to be imposed by the court and justified by particularized on-the-record findings." 68 F.4th at 767-68. Item 3 is the broader form of that vice: rather than capping Mr. Bernecky at one device, it lets Probation decide, device by device, whether he may have any.

Two of Mr. Bernecky's own devices show what that discretion produces.

The first is a television. He owns a Roku set, which he reported to Probation at the outset and which the manufacturer states "does not provide the ability to browse the internet on your television." Certification ¶ \_\_. Probation put a parental lock on it anyway. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. A lock is not monitoring. Special Condition (a) authorizes applications installed "as necessary to surveil all activity," and directs that monitoring "be designed to avoid ... reading any privileged information" — language presupposing that something is being watched. Blocking is a different act. It is active prohibition: it restricts what Mr. Bernecky may do rather than recording what he does, and the condition authorizes it nowhere. The lock also does not reach the television's casting function, the one path by which outside content actually arrives on it. *Id.* ¶ \_\_.

The second is a telephone. As instructed, Mr. Bernecky notified Probation of his Android phone, on a US Mobile plan he pays for, and contacted the CIMP vendor to arrange installation of the monitoring software. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. The phone now displays on its lock screen: "This device belongs to your organization." *Id.* ¶ \_\_. That is not something a vendor wrote. It is Android's own system text, generated automatically when an application is granted Device Owner status — the most privileged management tier the operating system offers, carrying the power to erase the device remotely, to prevent its own removal, and to control what other software may be installed. Google defines that tier as a device "entirely managed by the user's organization, intended for company-owned devices that are used exclusively for work purposes." Google, *Android Enterprise Terminology*, https://developers.google.com/android/work/terminology. Mr. Bernecky bought the phone and pays for the service. Special Condition (a) authorizes surveilling a device's activity, and that does not require the power to wipe it. Probation may not take the broader tier merely because the platform makes it available.

Mr. Bernecky asks that pre-authorization attach only to a device that has general-purpose web-browsing capability, or an application-installation mechanism through which an ordinary user could add web-browsing, anonymization, or peer-to-peer file-sharing capability, and that is compatible with the monitoring software Probation uses — and that whatever device-management privilege is used to enroll an authorized device be no broader than surveilling that device's activity requires. In exchange he offers Probation something the Judgment does not give it. Special Condition (a) conditions physical examination on a trigger: "[a]s triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity." Mr. Bernecky consents to physical inspection of any of his personal devices, on request, at any time, with no prior suspicion required — as to devices within his own authority to consent to. *See United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164, 171 (1974).

**Item 21 — seizure authority.** Item 21 requires Mr. Bernecky to "permit the seizure and removal of hardware and data storage media for further analysis by law enforcement or the probation officer based upon reasonable suspicion that a violation of a condition of supervision or unlawful conduct by any person has or is about to occur." Ex. A ¶ 21. Special Condition (a) is narrower in three respects. It authorizes only "the U.S. Probation Office" to install applications and examine devices; Item 21 adds "law enforcement," empowered to seize hardware independently — and Congress made that broader model available without the sentencing court selecting it. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(23) (search "by any law enforcement or probation officer"). Special Condition (a) ties examination to activity on equipment "owned or used by the defendant"; Item 21 substitutes "unlawful conduct by any person," so that a visitor's unrelated suspected crime becomes grounds to seize Mr. Bernecky's hardware, though every model Congress or the Commission has written ties the trigger to the defendant. *See* U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(7) ("unlawful conduct by the defendant"), quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 758-59. And Special Condition (a) speaks of activity "occurring," while Item 21 adds seizure where conduct "is about to occur," converting an after-the-fact tool into a predictive one. Each addition exceeds what the district court delegated and should be declared unenforceable and stricken. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68.

**Item 16 — security software.** Item 16 forbids Mr. Bernecky to "purchase, download, possess and/or install any anti-virus, anti-spyware, firewall, or internet security application without advanced authorization." Ex. A ¶ 16. Every modern operating system ships with antivirus and firewall software already installed and running, and a user does not "purchase, download, possess and/or install" what the manufacturer installed before the device reached him. Read literally, Item 16 requires Mr. Bernecky to seek authorization to leave his operating system's factory defaults running, or else disable them and leave the very device Probation monitors open to compromise. Nothing in Special Condition (a) mentions security software. Another arm of the same federal government instructs every American to do what Item 16 gates: the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency "recommends that all computers and mobile devices on your home network run antivirus software," alongside its instruction to "[i]nstall a network firewall." CISA, *Home Network Security*, https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/home-network-security. Probation's interest is in knowing what security software runs on a monitored device, and notice serves that interest completely.

**Item 5 — private browsing.** Item 5 provides that "[w]hile using my monitored device, I shall not use any form of 'in-private' browsing at any time." Ex. A ¶ 5. Private browsing defeats third-party tracking and clears local cache and cookies at the end of a session. It is not built to evade a monitoring agent with device-level access, and it does not: Google's own documentation states that "organizations that manage your network, like your school, employer, or internet service provider, may be able to observe your activity in Incognito." Google, *Browse in Incognito Mode*, Chrome Help, https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/95464. The feature ships as a default on effectively every browser, and some browsers do not offer it as a mode at all because tracker-blocking is the only mode they have — DuckDuckGo is one. DuckDuckGo, *Web Tracking Protections*, https://duckduckgo.com/duckduckgo-help-pages/privacy/web-tracking-protections. Item 5 reaches an ordinary privacy feature used by ordinary people, on a rationale Special Condition (a) never adopted. If Probation's monitoring captures activity regardless of browser mode — which is what the authorization to "surveil all activity" presumes — Item 5 adds nothing. If it does not, that is a fact about the method Probation chose, addressed in Part I.C.

**Items 9 and 17 — encryption.** Item 9 provides that Mr. Bernecky "will not use any encrypted email accounts." Item 17 provides that he "shall not use any type of encryption unless pre-approved by my probation officer." Ex. A ¶¶ 9, 17. Both are challenged, and for the same threshold reason: Special Condition (a) says nothing about encryption, so both are Probation's additions to a sentence rather than its execution of one. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68; *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 514.

*Kunz* construed CIMP paragraph 18 — word for word the same text as Item 17 — and held it "would be absurd to interpret Probation as purporting to require Kunz to ask permission every time he attempts to access a website whose URL happens to begin with the characters 'https.'" 68 F.4th at 764. It construed the restriction narrowly: "so long as no encryption is initiated by or at the behest of Kunz, and so long as any encryption initiated by external forces does not meaningfully impair Probation's ability to monitor Kunz's behavior ... we construe the encryption restrictions to allow the reasonable use of services and resources that happen to employ encryption in some way." *Id.*

That construction was calibrated to the absurdity in front of the panel — transport-layer encryption on ordinary web traffic — and it does not clear the clause. "[A]ny type of encryption" also reaches at-rest encryption of a device's own storage, end-to-end messaging, signed and encrypted email, virtual private networks, and encrypted backups. Each of those is encryption a user affirmatively selects rather than encryption that "happens" to him, so *Kunz*'s narrowing does not cover them, and each is a routine feature of ordinary digital life. Item 17 leaves them all behind a pre-approval gate.

That gate is unworkable on its own terms. It requires advance permission for things ordinary life gives no advance warning of — a bank that delivers statements through a secure message portal, a medical provider that sends records through an encrypted link, a court that requires filings through an encrypted system. A term demanding a telephone call before an ordinary transaction can be completed is not a monitoring measure. It is a permission slip for daily life, and the Judgment does not authorize one.

Item 9 is worse in one respect: it bans encrypted email outright, with no approval route at all. And the ban accomplishes nothing, because Probation's monitoring captures Mr. Bernecky's screen. For him to read a message he must have it in front of him in plaintext — true of a routine Gmail message and equally true of a PGP-encrypted one. Encryption hides nothing from a monitoring agent sitting on the device. What it does is protect him from everyone else. Federal law assumes it in the most sensitive category there is: an entity transmitting a patient's own medical records to that patient must "[i]mplement technical security measures to guard against unauthorized access to electronic protected health information that is being transmitted over an electronic communications network," and the regulation names encryption as the mechanism. 45 C.F.R. § 164.312(e). Item 9, read literally, tells Mr. Bernecky he must be the one American who receives his own medical records over an unprotected channel.

The right line for both Items is conduct, not a catalogue of approved uses and not a pre-approval gate. Mr. Bernecky may need whatever encryption ordinary life requires, and neither he nor his probation officer can enumerate that list in advance. What Probation is entitled to forbid is what it actually cares about: knowingly attempting to circumvent or defeat the monitoring regime, and actively suppressing or destroying data.

**Relief requested (Part I.B):** an order (1) replacing Item 3's device-authorization gate with the capability-based standard described above, coupled with Mr. Bernecky's consent to suspicion-free physical inspection of any device within his authority to consent to, and confining any device-management privilege used to enroll a device to no more than surveilling its activity requires; (2) declaring unenforceable and striking from Item 21 "by law enforcement," "or unlawful conduct by any person," and "or is about to occur"; (3) narrowing Item 16 to require notice rather than prior authorization before installing or running any antivirus, anti-spyware, firewall, or other security software, whether factory-installed or added; (4) striking Item 5, or construing it to reach only conduct undertaken to defeat monitoring; and (5) striking Items 9 and 17 and replacing them with a single term prohibiting Mr. Bernecky from knowingly attempting to circumvent or defeat the monitoring required by Special Condition (a), and from suppressing, destroying, or concealing data subject to monitoring.

#### C. Special Condition (a) never says what monitoring consists of, and the method should be limited to the file types Mr. Bernecky's offenses actually involve.

Special Condition (a)'s promise that monitoring "shall be designed to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information" is a real constraint on Probation rather than hortatory text. The Second Circuit has held that this exact language "constrains the supervisors in their surveillance ... by requiring them not to abuse their power and access." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 766-67. A method that captures everything has nothing left to avoid, and the reading has already happened by the time any design would operate.

That promise is currently unadministrable. Mr. Bernecky served an earlier term of supervision under a materially similar condition, and Probation's administration of it produced continuous keystroke logging. Certification ¶ \_\_. This time he has been given no instruction — by Probation, by the vendor, or by anyone — on whether his probation officer has unfettered access to captured data or sees it only on a trigger, on what constitutes a report to Probation, or on privilege in any respect. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. A privilege the law protects even against a prison's walls cannot survive on an unwritten promise of good faith. The process must answer three questions it has never answered: whether Mr. Bernecky may designate privileged material by folder or label so that it is excluded at capture; whether privileged material must be submitted for *in camera* review before anyone at Probation or the vendor reads it; and whether privileged material captured inadvertently must be suppressed, destroyed, and never disclosed.

The stakes are not abstract. Indiscriminate capture cannot distinguish an ordinary message from a communication with an attorney, "the oldest of the privileges for confidential communications known to the common law," *Upjohn Co. v. United States*, 449 U.S. 383, 389 (1981); a session with a psychotherapist, *Jaffee v. Redmond*, 518 U.S. 1, 10-15 (1996); or a private communication between spouses, *Trammel v. United States*, 445 U.S. 40, 51 (1980). Nor does it stop at formal privilege. A modern device holds "the sum of an individual's private life." *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373, 393-97 (2014). And because internet-connected devices are of "nearly essential" importance to modern life, *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 773 (quoting *United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d 88, 96 (2d Cir. 2019)), a monitoring choice made once at the outset governs a very large share of a decade.

The Second Circuit has already mapped the spectrum of methods, and the ends are not close. "[C]onstant inspection of the documents that Lifshitz creates on his computer might be more like searching his diary or inspecting his closets than it is like the highly targeted diagnosis accomplished by drug testing," while "software that alerted a probation officer only when Lifshitz was engaging in impermissible communications over e-mail or the Internet would bear much greater resemblance to screening a probationer's urine for particular drugs." *United States v. Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d 173, 192 (2d Cir. 2004). Section 3583(d)(2) permits only the deprivation of liberty that is "reasonably necessary."

Applying that comparison here produces a specific line, and it is not the testing method — it is the file type. Mr. Bernecky's conduct in both convictions was possession of images, and he has never been alleged to have communicated with anyone about committing an offense. Certification ¶ \_\_. A drug test screens for particular drugs; the equivalent here screens for particular file types. He does not object to monitoring and does not ask to go unmonitored. He asks that monitoring be limited to inspecting photograph and video files on his devices, and that the vendor be prohibited from disclosing to Probation or anyone else anything other than image and video file content. The Second Circuit has already identified a comparably focused tool as adequate for this offense category: "a more focused restriction, limited to pornography sites and images, can be enforced by unannounced inspections of [a probationer's] premises and examination of material stored on his hard drive or removable disks." *United States v. Sofsky*, 287 F.3d 122, 127 (2d Cir. 2002) (addressing a total access ban), quoted in *Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d at 189.

*Balon* anticipated the objection and invited the answer. Considering a proposal to limit monitoring to particular categories of data, the panel observed that a user could disguise a contraband file "as a 'word' or 'excel' document" by changing "the letters at the end of the file name," found no device then capable of what the defendant proposed, and said expressly: "Perhaps our analysis has missed something that Balon can clarify, but we offer this note so that Balon will address the issue when the time comes." 384 F.3d at 48. The time has come, and twenty-two years of technology have answered the note: content-based file-type identification does not depend on a filename or an extension. Mr. Bernecky does not ask this Court to select a product or resolve an engineering question on the papers. He asks it to set the legal boundary — image and video files, nothing else disclosed — and to direct the Probation Office to implement it. If Probation contends no available technology can hold that line, that is a factual dispute, and Mr. Bernecky requests an evidentiary hearing on it.

**Relief requested (Part I.C):** an order (1) limiting monitoring under Special Condition (a) to inspection of image and video files, and prohibiting the provider from disclosing to the Probation Office or anyone else any captured material other than image and video file content; (2) directing the Probation Office to implement that limitation and report to the Court on the method selected; (3) directing that Mr. Bernecky be given written notice of what data is captured, who may access it, and what triggers a report; and (4) establishing a privilege protocol providing for designation, *in camera* review before any review by Probation or the vendor, and suppression and destruction of any privileged material captured inadvertently.

#### D. "Internet capable device," "operated," and "used" no longer describe a workable class of devices.

Special Condition (a)'s reach turns on three words that have not aged well. It conditions Mr. Bernecky's use of any "internet capable device" on CIMP participation, authorizes monitoring of devices "owned or operated" by him, and authorizes examination of equipment "owned or used" by him. Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (a).

Federal law defines a "computer" as "an electronic, magnetic, optical, electrochemical, or other high speed data processing device performing logical, arithmetic, or storage functions." 18 U.S.C. § 1030(e)(1). A graphing calculator satisfies that definition, and so, at the other extreme, do a car and the touchscreen fare systems on modern mass transit — each of which is, in ordinary English, a thing a person "operates." Read literally, the condition tells Mr. Bernecky he may not board a bus without enrolling it in CIMP. The line that does real work is not connectivity and not the verb; it is capability — whether a device can access, search for, or display child pornography.

*Kunz* confirms that this Circuit reads this program's text sensibly rather than literally when a literal reading would sweep too far. 68 F.4th at 764-65. But *Kunz*'s facts were milder in one decisive respect: no one there had acted on the expansive reading, so the panel needed only say that nobody would. Here someone did — twice. An employer held Mr. Bernecky's job for two weeks over what "operated" and "used" might require of its own systems. And Probation, having locked down the browser-less Roku, told him that YouTube presented a problem — a service carrying network news, congressional proceedings, instructional material, and nearly the full range of ordinary media, with no connection to the conduct of conviction. Certification ¶ \_\_. That is not an officer reading an ambiguous text one plausible way. It is an officer treating the condition as a general license to decide what Mr. Bernecky may watch. The Second Circuit has held that a sentencing court "must determine" such questions itself and "may not simply leave" them to a probation officer's "unfettered discretion." *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 85-86; *accord Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122. An interpretive canon premised on everyone recognizing the same obvious meaning cannot resolve a dispute in which the officer administering the condition has already read it as conferring exactly the discretion the Circuit says she cannot have.

*United States v. Leone* turned aside a vagueness challenge to a monitoring condition premised on "[m]icrowaves and modern refrigerators." No. 19-1670, 2020 WL 2610536 (2d Cir. May 20, 2020) (summary order). That challenge was facial and hypothetical, on a record containing no officer who had actually read the condition expansively — the problem this record does contain. Mr. Bernecky asks this Court to exercise its own § 3583(e)(2) authority at the moment *Balon* identified, and *Balon* is the case *Leone* relies on.

**Relief requested (Part I.D):** an order striking "or operated" and "or used," leaving "owned by the defendant" as the operative standard, and defining "internet capable device" by the capability-based standard set out in Part I.B above — together with a provision that, excepting employer devices necessary for work performance, Mr. Bernecky shall not personally and privately possess any unreported device meeting that definition.

### II. Special Conditions (b) and (h) — The R.K.K.S. Treatment Contract

Mr. Bernecky is in treatment and intends to stay in it. He asks only that the treatment this Court ordered not be made contingent on his agreement to terms this Court never imposed.

Before R.K.K.S. Mental Health Counseling PLLC would treat him, it required him to execute a document titled "Treatment Contract," attached as Exhibit B and dated August 19, 2026. Certification ¶ \_\_. Nothing in it was written for Mr. Bernecky. Its first Special Condition governs a person "under pretrial supervision or pretrial diversion" who may not "talk about the offense for which I am currently being investigated" — Mr. Bernecky pleaded guilty in 2018 and has been serving the resulting sentence for eight years. Ex. B, Special Conditions ¶ 1. No check box, initial, or marking of any kind separates the paragraphs that apply to him from those that do not, and the form's operative sentence is "I agree to abide by the following special conditions" — not the following *checked* conditions. The one place reserved for what this patient individually requires reads, in full: "No special conditions designated."[^ii-1]

The Judgment is more particular than that. Special Condition (b) requires Mr. Bernecky to "participate in a sex offense-specific treatment program and follow the rules and regulations of that program," gives the probation officer authority to "supervise the details of the defendant's participation in the program, including the selection of a provider and schedule," and then reserves one thing: "The defendant is not to leave treatment until complete or as ordered by the Court." Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (b). Special Condition (h), which orders mental health treatment, uses the same supervise-the-details language and the same exit clause, and contains no rules-compliance clause at all. *Id.*, Special Condition (h). Which of the two R.K.K.S. serves under does not change the analysis; if it serves under (h), the Judgment supplies no textual hook for its terms whatever.

Those words have a history, and it is this one. On March 14, 2018 — three months before Mr. Bernecky was sentenced — Judge Vilardo entered an order on remand in *United States v. Browder* re-imposing that defendant's special conditions "as revised to reflect guidance from the Second Circuit since those conditions were first imposed in 2011 by Judge Arcara." No. 10-CR-263 (W.D.N.Y. Mar. 14, 2018). The conditions in that order are the conditions Mr. Bernecky serves — (b), (c), (d), (f), and (h), word for word. And the occasion for writing them was this exact problem. The Second Circuit had just held that Browder was "well within his rights to object" to signing a treatment agreement whose minor-contact term "would have subjected him to a more punitive condition governing contact with minors than did the condition to which Browder was actually sentenced by the District Court." *United States v. Browder*, 866 F.3d 504, 513 (2d Cir. 2017). Judge Vilardo's order states the rule in one sentence: "As the Second Circuit's decision on the treatment condition teaches, Browder cannot be forced to sign or abide by agreements that reach beyond the Court's ordered conditions."

The clause the government will rely on — "follow the rules and regulations of that program" — was written by that judge, in that order, on that page.

It could not have meant what the government will need it to mean. A program term that "would contradict, rather than execute," the sentence is one "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose." *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 514. A court "may delegate to a probation officer decisionmaking authority over certain minor details of supervised release — for example, the selection of a therapy provider or treatment schedule," but "may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122. Stated at its shortest: the Probation Office "may supervise and execute a sentence but may not fashion a sentence's terms." *United States v. Carlineo*, 998 F.3d 533, 538 (2d Cir. 2021).

Nothing turns on the document's title. Confronting a materially identical instrument in *United States v. Von Behren*, a district court held that because "compliance with the terms of the [provider's] agreement was required for participation in and successful completion of the ... program, the requirements of the ... agreement were, in effect, conditions of Mr. Von Behren's supervised release." 822 F.3d 1139, 1142 (10th Cir. 2016) (describing the district court's ruling). The Second Circuit noted that the *Browder* agreement "even refers to its terms as 'conditions.'" 866 F.3d at 513 n.38. Exhibit B closes with nearly the same acknowledgment — that Mr. Bernecky is "required to follow all the conditions listed above regarding my treatment and behavior."

*Browder* also disposes of the answer that no one would actually enforce these terms against Mr. Bernecky. The district court there had found that permission for Browder to contact his own daughters would certainly have been granted. That was not the question: "approvals from the Probation Office are insufficient to remedy the issue raised by Browder," because "[t]he key question is not whether permission, if sought, would be granted; it is whether Browder can be required, by the Probation Office or its designee, to ask in the first place." *Id.* at 514. And the panel flagged what decides much of what follows — the agreement required approval "not only of the Probation Office, but also of 'the treatment team' — something the District Court did not address and over which it had no direct oversight." *Id.*

#### A. Probation sits on the body that can end the treatment this Judgment orders Mr. Bernecky to finish.

Exhibit B defines that body: "The Treatment Team is comprised of all therapists in the Practice, the Probation Officer, and the Probation Supervisor." Ex. B, Consequences ¶ 3(a). Any "violation of the terms of this Treatment Contract," and any three unexcused absences, result in "CONSIDERATION FOR DISCHARGE from the Practice by the Treatment Team." *Id.* ¶ 3. A separate list requires "IMMEDIATE DISCHARGE from the Practice," and its final item is "other problem behavior not outlined in this Treatment Contract, as determined in the sole discretion of the staff of the Practice." *Id.* ¶ 2(vi). And every discharge is a report: "my supervising officer will be notified of any violation of this Treatment Contract," and "law enforcement may be contacted." *Id.* ¶ 1.

Follow that to its end. If the Treatment Team discharges Mr. Bernecky, he has left treatment before completion and without an order of this Court — a violation of Special Condition (b) on the face of the Judgment, reported by an officer who sat on the panel that produced it. A violation report begins a petition, and a petition can end in a warrant, an arrest, and a revocation hearing. The document does not describe that sequence. It sets it in motion.

The Judgment does not distribute the decision that starts it. It reserves it. "The defendant is not to leave treatment until complete or as ordered by the Court." Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (b). Two exits, and this Court holds both — completion, or its own order. A committee that includes Mr. Bernecky's probation officer and her supervisor is neither.

The Second Circuit decided this question in January. Reviewing treatment conditions requiring participation only "if deemed necessary" by the Probation Department, it held that they "improperly delegate judicial authority," vacated them, and directed the district court "to make clear ... that the district court itself will make the determination whether Harris participates in treatment, based on appropriate on-the-record findings." *United States v. Harris*, 164 F.4th 181, 197 (2d Cir. 2026). The rule is settled here: the "details of" a condition "can be left to the probation officer," but "whether a defendant is required to attend [certain programs] at all must be decided by the district court, since the court alone can impose such a condition." *Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d at 152-53, quoted in *Harris*, 164 F.4th at 197. Whether Mr. Bernecky continues to attend at all is that same question, asked later.

The government's best authority marks the same line. In *United States v. Jimenez* the Second Circuit upheld a treatment condition against a delegation challenge and said precisely why: the condition "does not entitle probation officers to impose additional burdens on Jimenez, such as giving them the authority to decide whether Jimenez participates in treatment at all." No. 24-1661, slip op. at 33-34 (2d Cir. Feb. 25, 2026, amended May 12, 2026). Mr. Bernecky does not dispute that a sentencing court may write such an arrangement into a judgment. The condition in *Jimenez* ran until the defendant was "released from the program by the probation officer" — and a district judge put that there, on the record, subject to review. Judge Larimer wrote the opposite into this one.

One ground for ending his treatment has no content at all: Consequences ¶ 2(vi) defines the forbidden conduct as "other problem behavior not outlined in this Treatment Contract." A supervisee has a "due process right to conditions of supervised release that are sufficiently clear to inform him of what conduct will result in his being returned to prison," *United States v. Simmons*, 343 F.3d 72, 81 (2d Cir. 2003), quoted in *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 537, and "[a] condition is too vague if it requires a reasonable person to speculate as to its meaning," *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 536. Mr. Bernecky cannot comply with ¶ 2(vi) by trying harder.

The profession that supplies this treatment draws the same line, for reasons of its own. Its current standards direct that "[p]robation and parole officers should not assume specialized clinical responsibilities within treatment programs ... with clients for whom they have supervision responsibility," and treat even an officer's presence at a session as something that can "inhibit client participation and disclosure" and "blur clients' perceptions of officers' and therapist roles." ATSA *Best Practice Guidelines for Men* (6th ed. 2025) (Risk Management). Consequences ¶ 3(a) goes a good deal further than presence. Mr. Bernecky does not ask this Court to enforce a private association's standards against anyone. He offers them to show that Probation's real interest in his treatment — whether he attends, participates, and progresses — is served without a seat on the panel.

#### B. The Judgment prohibits child pornography. The contract prohibits whatever treatment staff decides is stimulating, and requires permission before Mr. Bernecky uses email.

The Judgment's contraband provision is one sentence: "The defendant shall not possess or have in his control any child pornography." Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (j). Exhibit B adds two more prohibitions and a licensing scheme.

**"Sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind."** Special Conditions ¶ 8 provides that Mr. Bernecky "will not purchase, possess, view, produce, or use sexually stimulating material(s) of any kind as defined by my treatment staff," and requires him to report "any incidents" within twenty-four hours. Read as written, the paragraph is not confined to expression. Condoms, lubricant, and other lawful adult goods sold in ordinary stores are materials, they are sexually stimulating, and they are of a kind. No provision of this Judgment restricts them, and neither offense of conviction — possession of images, on devices Mr. Bernecky bought and kept at home — has anything to do with them.

Confined to expression, the term still does not survive *Eaglin*, where the Second Circuit vacated a ban on legal adult pornography that was defined by express reference to 18 U.S.C. § 2256(2) and imposed by a district judge who gave a reason for it. Such bans "must be supported on the record by detailed factual findings establishing that the proposed ban is reasonably related to the sentencing factors set forth in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) and that it is reasonably necessary to accomplish their objectives," and the district court had erred by treating "the possession of adult pornography as reprehensible in itself, rather than as an otherwise lawful action." *United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d 88, 99, 100 (2d Cir. 2019). *Eaglin* also names the route a clinical judgment takes into a sentence: such a ban "might be justified ... where a mental health professional testified that viewing pornography would be detrimental to the defendant's rehabilitation." *Id.* at 100. Testimony, weighed by a court, which then makes findings. This record contains no clinical opinion in any form that lawful adult material is contraindicated for Mr. Bernecky, and no finding by any court. Certification ¶ \_\_. R.K.K.S. may hold whatever clinical view it likes. What it may not do is convert that view into a term of a federal sentence enforceable by revocation.

The paragraph is also unreadable, and for the reason this Circuit has identified. A subjective element in a release condition "may be sufficiently specific if tethered to objective criteria," and the condition vacated in *Reeves* failed because it had "no objective baseline." *United States v. Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77, 81 (2d Cir. 2010). Paragraph 8 supplies a baseline and names it: "as defined by my treatment staff." That is the enforcer, not a standard.

The government will answer with *Simmons*, and *Simmons* answers it. The Second Circuit upheld a "pornography" condition there because 18 U.S.C. § 2256 supplies a "definition of pornography [that] avoids reference to subjective standards and is sufficiently specific to give adequate notice as to what conduct violates a prohibition on pornographic material." 343 F.3d at 82. In the same passage it cited two out-of-circuit decisions sustaining conditions that used the phrase "sexually stimulating," and each turned on something paragraph 8 lacks. *United States v. Phipps* was reviewed for plain error, and the Fifth Circuit sustained the term only by reading a companion clause as a limit — "[w]e therefore read this second condition to narrow the first condition to the kind of sexually explicit materials available at such places," meaning strip clubs and adult bookstores — which is how it concluded the term did not reach lingerie advertisements or the "Song of Solomon." 319 F.3d 177, 192-93 (5th Cir. 2003). *United States v. Bee* sustained a condition reaching material "deemed inappropriate by [his] probation officer and/or treatment staff" on abuse-of-discretion review, resting on a case-specific rationale the sentencing court had adopted: that the restriction was needed to address that defendant's "deviant sexual behavior triggered by his abuse of alcohol," in a case involving the sexual abuse of a six-year-old child. 162 F.3d 1232, 1234-35 (9th Cir. 1998). Paragraph 8 has no narrowing companion, no sentencing court, and no stated connection to anything Mr. Bernecky did. *Simmons* itself urged sentencing courts to write the statutory definition into such conditions "in future cases, particularly since in other contexts the term is inherently vague." 343 F.3d at 82.

**Sexually explicit images and narratives.** Special Conditions ¶ 4 forbids Mr. Bernecky to send or receive "sexually explicit images or narratives" without "prior approval by treatment staff." "Narratives" reaches text, and the reach is not hypothetical. A memoir of the abuse Mr. Bernecky suffered as a child, if he writes one, would be a sexually explicit narrative. So would his own presentence report, which describes the images found in his possession — and which this Judgment directs the Probation Office to release to a state board and a county court. Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (e).

**Platform pre-approval.** Special Conditions ¶ 5 provides that "the use of any platforms including but not limited to (social media, dating sites, email, blog/discussion forums, gaming services, text apps, photo sharing sites, chat rooms, etc.) should be approved by treatment staff prior to use." Email is named. What the Judgment requires as to Mr. Bernecky's use of connected services is advance notification to "the U.S. Probation Office," and it authorizes that office to monitor what he does. Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (a). Notice to a federal officer is not permission from a private clinician, and that is the difference *Browder* identified in the identical posture. 866 F.3d at 514.

#### C. The Second Circuit vacated a partner-notification requirement in *Reeves*, and *Boles* forbade a probation officer to impose the weaker version of it. This contract imposes it on no determination at all.

Special Conditions ¶ 2 provides that Mr. Bernecky is "required to disclose all my intimate partners to treatment staff," and that he "need[s] to provide acceptable proof of informed consent to treatment staff which includes disclosure of my offense history in full prior to engaging in a sexual relationship."

*Reeves* vacated a condition requiring a supervisee to "notify the Probation Department when he establishes a significant romantic relationship and ... inform the other party of his prior criminal history concerning his sex offenses." 591 F.3d at 80. Three of its holdings apply here without adjustment. Who may impose such a requirement: "in the appropriate circumstance a court, on the recommendation of the Probation Department, could require a defendant to notify third-parties of risks arising from the defendant's criminal record, personal history, or characteristics." *Id.* at 82. What it takes, because the requirement burdens a protected associational interest: the deprivation must be "narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest." *Id.* at 82-83 (quoting *United States v. Myers*, 426 F.3d 117, 126 (2d Cir. 2005)). And what it costs: such a requirement "would almost certainly adversely affect, and could very well prematurely end, any intimate relationship he might develop, placing him at a greater risk of social isolation and thus impair, rather than enhance, his rehabilitation." *Id.*

This Circuit has since rejected the weaker, officer-driven version of the same thing. *Boles* held a condition invalid because it let a probation officer determine that a supervisee "pose[s] a risk to another person" and require him to say so, and this District ordered that language replaced within two months with a version requiring the court itself to make the risk finding. *Boles*, 914 F.3d at 111-12; *see* Part VII, *infra*. Paragraph 2 reinstates the structure both decisions rejected, then removes the determination altogether. No officer finds that a particular partner faces a risk; no court does either. The duty attaches to every partner, before every sexual relationship, by operation of a form on which the space for individualized conditions is blank.

"[A]cceptable proof of informed consent" is also a phrase Mr. Bernecky cannot act on. Nothing states what proof is acceptable, in what form, or on what showing, and the graders are the treatment staff whose satisfaction determines whether his treatment continues. A subjective term unmoored from objective criteria is not enforceable, *Reeves*, 591 F.3d at 81, and this one goes further still: it assigns Mr. Bernecky the work of documenting another adult's state of mind.

#### D. Special Condition (c) reaches deliberate contact. The contract reaches accidental contact.

Special Conditions ¶ 9 provides that Mr. Bernecky's "contact with individuals under the age of 18, potential or former victims, codefendants, vulnerable populations (as defined by treatment staff) will be reviewed for appropriateness by treatment staff," and that he must "notify treatment staff within 24 hours of any accidental, incidental, or intentional contact with any of the above."

Two things are wrong with that paragraph, and they are independent of each other. The first is textual. Special Condition (c) prohibits "deliberate contact with any child under 18 years of age, excluding his biological or adopted children, unless approved by the probation officer or by the Court." Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (c). Judge Vilardo entered that sentence in the same remand order that produced Special Condition (b), carrying forward the limitation to "deliberate contact" that Judge Arcara had written in 2011. *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 507 n.5. "Accidental" is not a narrower reading of "deliberate." It is the opposite word, and no construction reconciles the two. A term standing in that relation to a sentence "would contradict, rather than execute" it, and neither the Probation Office nor a provider it designates had power to impose it. *Id.* at 514. The remaining categories compound the problem: "vulnerable populations (as defined by treatment staff)" names its definer for the second time in this document, nothing in this record connects Mr. Bernecky to any such category, Certification ¶ \_\_, and a condition with "no objective baseline" cannot be enforced, *Reeves*, 591 F.3d at 81.

The second problem is that one of these disclosures cannot be compelled at all. Identifying someone as a "potential ... victim" is not a neutral fact about a third party; it is a statement about Mr. Bernecky, and the contract routes it to his probation officer and permits law enforcement to be contacted. Ex. B, Confidentiality ¶ 2; *id.*, Consequences ¶ 1. The Tenth Circuit gave independent weight to a nearly identical clause in *Von Behren*, where the provider's agreement instructed it to report "any occurrence or potential occurrence of any sexual offense" to authorities expressly including "victims or potential victims" — a provision that, because it "specifically authorizes his examiner to report his admissions to the police, undoubtedly adds to Mr. Von Behren's apprehension." 822 F.3d at 1142, 1147. An answer of that kind is admissible against the speaker as propensity evidence in the very prosecutions he would face. *Id.* at 1146 (citing Fed. R. Evid. 413, 414). For the reasons set out in Part IV, Mr. Bernecky will invoke the privilege rather than answer, and no clinical benefit a provider attaches to the answer converts a compelled disclosure into a voluntary one.

#### E. The contract commands disclosure of criminal conduct, makes no allowance for the privilege, and duplicates reporting Mr. Bernecky already owes his probation officer.

Requirements ¶ 1 conditions treatment on "full acknowledgement of my actions and thinking patterns." Code of Conduct ¶ 5 requires Mr. Bernecky to "notify treatment staff within 24 hours of any relapse in problematic behavior." Code of Conduct ¶ 6 requires notice of changes in "relevant risk factors," a list that expressly includes "criminal behaviors \[outside of or in addition to Instant Offense or pending charges\]." Confidentiality ¶ 2 routes what he says to the Probation Office and to unnamed "other individuals and agencies involved in my treatment," and Consequences ¶ 1 permits law enforcement to be contacted. Nowhere does the document reserve his right to decline to answer.

The privilege analysis is in Part IV and is not repeated here. It applies to a question put by a treatment provider exactly as it applies to one put by a polygraph examiner: no sovereign has offered Mr. Bernecky immunity, and his treatment statements are being used against him in a New York proceeding today. The request here is narrower — that a document nobody negotiated not be enforced as though he had surrendered a right no one asked him about.

"[P]roblematic behavior" is separately unreadable. It is the document's organizing term, appearing in Requirements ¶ 1 and in Code of Conduct ¶¶ 5 and 6, and the document never defines it. Neither does it define "relapse," a word that presupposes the definition. A man required to self-report within twenty-four hours has to be able to tell, within twenty-four hours, what he is reporting. *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 536.

Paragraph 6's residence and employment reporting is owed already. Mr. Bernecky must report both to his probation officer, on stated timelines, under Standard Conditions 5 and 7, and must give the same officer financial disclosure under Special Conditions (d) and (i). Dkt. No. 28. The contract states no treatment purpose for a second channel and sets no deadline for using it. What it adds is a further set of obligations whose breach is a "violation of this Treatment Contract" reportable to the officer who already had the information. Ex. B, Consequences ¶¶ 1, 3.

### III. Special Conditions (d) and (i) — Financial Monitoring and Disclosure

Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to limit Special Condition (d) to the records capable of showing what it was written to detect, and to vacate Special Condition (i).

The changed circumstance is the point. Before his incarceration he had nothing, and an objection to a financial-disclosure condition would have been unripe — there were no finances to disclose. He is now in his thirties, employed full time, and his employer offers a 401(k). Certification ¶ \_\_. He wants to save for retirement and to invest, and should not have to seek advance approval to do it or produce brokerage and retirement records to a probation officer.

Special Condition (d) states its own purpose: to monitor compliance "with not buying or subscribing to online services that provide child pornography." No one has ever alleged that Mr. Bernecky purchased child pornography; his convictions are for possession, and nothing in the record suggests his access involved payment. Even taking the stated purpose at face value, the records capable of showing such a payment are a specific set — credit and debit card statements, PayPal or comparable electronic transaction records, and cryptocurrency wallet or exchange records. A 401(k) statement cannot show a payment to a child-pornography vendor. Neither can a brokerage confirmation. And detecting such a payment requires nothing beyond the payee's name: the purpose is served in full once Mr. Bernecky discloses to whom a payment was made, without the amount, the account number, or anything else.

*United States v. Jenkins* struck a condition forbidding a child-pornography defendant from incurring any credit card charge or opening any line of credit without prior approval, holding that "[n]othing in the record suggests these restrictions ... are 'reasonably necessary' ... to protect the public or to deter [him] from continuing to engage in the conduct for which he was convicted — possession of child pornography," and asking why he "should be prohibited from buying a drink on an airplane or taking an Uber ride or making a purchase on Amazon unless the transaction is pre-approved by a probation officer." 854 F.3d 181, 194 (2d Cir. 2017). Special Condition (d) requires disclosure rather than permission, and the two are not the same. But *Jenkins*'s point survives the distinction: a condition responding to a possession offense with no financial element cannot reach further than that offense gives Probation reason to look.

Special Condition (i) is spent. It ties Probation's access to Mr. Bernecky's finances, and its control over his assets, to "the collection of any unpaid fine or restitution." The $500 restitution and the special penalty assessment are paid in full. Certification ¶ \_\_. Nothing remains to collect, and the guideline the condition implements authorizes a condition of this kind only "if the court imposes an order of restitution, forfeiture, or notice to victims, or orders the defendant to pay a fine," U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(3), with the companion debt-obligation condition applying only where the defendant is not "in compliance with the payment schedule," *id.* § 5D1.3(d)(2). The condition should be vacated.

**Relief requested (Part III):** an order (1) clarifying that Special Condition (d) requires disclosure only of the payee's name for transactions made by credit card, debit card, PayPal or comparable electronic payment service, or cryptocurrency, and does not reach the amount of any transaction, any account number, or any brokerage, investment, or retirement account record; (2) confirming that neither condition requires advance approval for Mr. Bernecky to participate in an employer-sponsored retirement plan, to open or fund a brokerage account, or to select investments; and (3) vacating Special Condition (i).

### IV. Special Condition (g) — Polygraph and Computerized Voice Stress Analyzer Testing

The Fifth Amendment protects the right to refuse to answer an incriminating question at the moment the question is asked. Special Condition (g) takes that right away and hands back a receipt. It commands that Mr. Bernecky "shall answer the questions posed during the examination," and offers in exchange a preserved right to argue, later and somewhere unspecified, about what may be done with the answer. The Court should strike the command.

Three sentences should go: "The defendant shall answer the questions posed during the examination, subject to the defendant's right to challenge in a court of law the use of such statements as violations of the defendant's Fifth Amendment rights"; "In this regard, the defendant shall be deemed not to have waived the defendant's Fifth Amendment rights by making any such statements"; and "The results of any polygraph pre-tests and polygraph examinations may be disclosed to the US Probation office and the Court, but shall not be further disclosed without a court order." The third is surplusage once the first two are gone; it exists only to govern what becomes of compelled statements. Everything else stands, including the command that Mr. Bernecky *submit to* testing on the schedule the condition sets.

The condition reaches both instruments it names. It authorizes "examinations using a polygraph, computerized voice stress analyzer, or other similar device," and the compelled-answer sentence applies to all of them without distinction. The constitutional injury is the command to answer, and a voice stress analyzer extracts the answer the same way a polygraph does. Everything said below applies with identical force to CVSA examinations and to any successor device the condition's "other similar device" language would reach.

**The governing rule.** Compulsion exists where the government, "either expressly or by implication, asserts that invocation of the privilege would lead to revocation" or another substantial penalty. *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 435 (1984). Murphy lost, and the two reasons he lost are the two reasons this condition is different. Murphy's condition said nothing about his freedom to decline to answer — it required truthfulness if he spoke, not an answer to every question put to him — and nothing in his case, express or implied, threatened revocation for invoking. Special Condition (g) is the mirror image on both. It speaks directly to Mr. Bernecky's freedom to decline, and commands that he "shall answer." And it is a condition of supervised release, so failing to comply is by definition a violation. The threatened penalty is not something an officer would have to add in the room. It is in the condition's own grammar.

That matters because of what invoking has always required. A witness invokes by declining to answer, with nothing further required of him at that moment; the validity of the claim is for a court to assess afterward if challenged. *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951). Special Condition (g) tells Mr. Bernecky that the single act the Constitution has always said suffices to protect him is itself a violation of his supervision. That is *Murphy*'s penalty situation reached by no analogy at all.

**The savings clause is not immunity.** *Von Behren* held that "a threat to revoke one's probation for properly invoking his Fifth Amendment privilege is the type of compulsion the state may not constitutionally impose," and identified in footnote 9 the three circumstances that would change the outcome: if the defendant "had refused to answer a question relevant only to his supervised release status ..., or if he had refused to answer a question that did not involve the risk of self-incrimination, or if he were given immunity and still refused to answer any questions." 822 F.3d at 1149 n.9, 1150.

Special Condition (g) supplies none of the three, and the third is where the difference lives. Immunity is a promise that an answer and its fruits may not be used against the speaker — what *Murphy* itself contemplated when it said the government may "insist on answers to even incriminating questions and hence sensibly administer its probation system, as long as it recognizes that the required answers may not be used in a criminal proceeding." 465 U.S. at 435 n.7. What Special Condition (g) supplies instead is a preserved right to litigate. A defendant given immunity has lost nothing by answering. A defendant given a right to litigate has lost the disclosure itself and keeps only an argument about what may be done with it.

**And immunity, to substitute for the privilege, has to be as large as the privilege it displaces.** The Supreme Court's rule is one of coextension: "such immunity from use and derivative use is coextensive with the scope of the privilege against self-incrimination, and therefore is sufficient to compel testimony over a claim of the privilege." *Kastigar v. United States*, 406 U.S. 441, 462 (1972). The measure is the privilege itself — and the privilege is not bounded by sovereign. It "protects a state witness against incrimination under federal as well as state law and a federal witness against incrimination under state as well as federal law." *Murphy v. Waterfront Comm'n of N.Y. Harbor*, 378 U.S. 52, 77-78 (1964) (*Waterfront Commission*). A promise that binds one prosecutor, or one sovereign, or one proceeding does not remove the hazard; it relocates it. Whatever the United States Attorney for this District may promise about a federal prosecution leaves a New York prosecutor free to use the same words.

That is not hypothetical here. This Judgment itself directs that Mr. Bernecky's presentence report be released to the New York State Board of Examiners of Sex Offenders and to a New York county court. Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (e). Material generated in this federal supervision is built to travel to a state forum. A savings clause preserving a right to argue "in a court of law" — without identifying which court, or which sovereign's — is not coextensive with anything.

**The threshold that triggers the privilege is low.** A statement is incriminating if it would furnish "a lead or a link in the chain of evidence that could lead to prosecution." *Von Behren*, 822 F.3d at 1144 (quoting *Maness v. Meyers*, 419 U.S. 449, 461 (1975)). No active investigation is required. *United States v. McDonald*, No. 1:11-CR-01075-MV-1, slip op. at 6 (D.N.M. Jan. 6, 2026). Once a person has a valid basis to invoke, the government may not compel the answer at all — not now, on the promise that a court will decide later whether it should have. A later ruling that a statement is inadmissible cannot make an investigator un-know what the answer told him or stop him from following where it leads.

**The two decisions closest to this question turn on a verb this condition does not use.** In *United States v. Richards*, the Tenth Circuit upheld a condition requiring the defendant "to submit to periodic polygraph testing," on a record where nothing showed the government had threatened revocation for a valid invocation, and where the condition itself barred use of polygraph results "for purposes of instituting or instigating criminal charges." 958 F.3d 961, 964, 966-68 (10th Cir. 2020). Judge Vázquez reached the same result as to a condition requiring the defendant to "submit to clinical polygraph examinations," in terms that decide this case: a defendant "may be required to 'submit to clinical polygraph examinations,' but that does not necessarily translate to 'truthfully answer even incriminating questions during mandatory clinical polygraph examinations.'" *McDonald*, slip op. at 11. Special Condition (g) closes that gap by its own terms. It does not tell Mr. Bernecky to submit to an examination; it tells him to answer the questions posed during one. There is no reading of "shall answer" that leaves open the room to decline that "submit to" leaves open. Both courts also left the door open to exactly this motion: *Richards* invited a renewed challenge if the government's position changed, 958 F.3d at 968, and *McDonald* said the court "would consider adding language akin to that approved in *Richards*" on a motion to modify, slip op. at 13.

The Second Circuit has not decided this question. Its one polygraph decision, *United States v. Johnson*, upheld language close enough to Special Condition (g) that the resemblance is unlikely to be coincidental. 446 F.3d 272 (2d Cir. 2006). But the theory presented there was narrower: Johnson argued that, absent formal use and derivative-use immunity, he faced an unconstitutional choice between confessing to past conduct and standing in violation for silence. Nobody in *Johnson* argued that compelling the answer itself, at the moment it is demanded, is the constitutional violation. That is Mr. Bernecky's theory, and *Johnson* never reached it.

**What the Supreme Court said this June about constitutionally infirm supervised-release conditions.** In *Hunter v. United States*, decided June 18, 2026, the Court "approve[d] the majority view among the courts of appeals that an appeal waiver is unenforceable when it would result in a miscarriage of justice," a standard it defined by saying that a "waiver may be set aside only if the sentence is marred by the kind of egregious error that would bring the judicial system into disrepute." 608 U.S. \_\_\_, No. 24-1063, slip op. at 11 (2026). The Court then gave three illustrations of the error it meant. The second: "a defendant may appeal a sentence that is infected with a blatant constitutional error, such as when a judge takes account of a constitutionally impermissible factor (like race) or imposes a constitutionally infirm condition of supervised release." *Id.* at 12. Justice Gorsuch, concurring, read that category to reach "a condition of release that violates his right to be free from forced medication, or a condition that violates his right to speak or worship freely, or any other condition that violates one of his recognized constitutional rights." *Id.* at 10 (Gorsuch, J., concurring).

The Court set a high bar and said so, and Justice Kavanaugh wrote separately, joined by two colleagues, to underscore it. Mr. Bernecky asks for considerably less than *Hunter* gave. He asks no court to disregard a waiver, a default, or any procedural bar; he asks this Court to exercise authority Congress conferred by statute on a motion Congress said may be made "at any time." What *Hunter* supplies is the Supreme Court's own placement of a constitutionally infirm supervised-release condition — alongside racial bias in sentencing — in the small category of errors the judicial system cannot leave standing. A district court holding express statutory power to fix such a condition, before anyone has been arrested over it, is the least costly moment in the system to do so.

**Why now.** Nothing about waiver doctrine, and nothing this Court can say about timing, will change what Mr. Bernecky intends to do: he will invoke the privilege question by question, at the moment each question is asked, and he will not answer under compulsion. What makes this urgent is not a scheduled examination. It is that his treatment statements are already being used against him, in a proceeding under way today.

Mr. Bernecky is a respondent in a New York Sex Offender Registration Act risk-level proceeding in Monroe County Court. Certification ¶ \_\_. Statements he made in treatment are discussed at length in the materials before that court and are being used to argue for a higher risk classification. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. That is present-tense, documented use of his treatment disclosures against him in another forum, by another sovereign — the hazard *Waterfront Commission* holds the privilege reaches, and the one a savings clause preserving a right to argue "in a court of law" does nothing to prevent. A man watching his treatment statements be used against him in one proceeding, while a condition of his supervision commands him to make more of them, has every reason to stop talking and every right to.

**The warning nobody gave him.** The profession that supplies sex offense-specific treatment has confronted this exact dilemma in the polygraph setting and prescribed a remedy this condition does not contain. Its practice standards direct that members "must ensure that limits of confidentiality are fully disclosed to clients prior to polygraph testing, and that clients are afforded the opportunity for informed consent, specifically with respect to the ways in which the findings will be used and to whom the findings will be provided," because "[c]lient disclosures of potentially incriminating information to mandated reporters can ... lead to future prosecution." ATSA *Practice Guidelines* App. B (2014); *see* Part II, *supra*, n.1 (full citation). What follows is a writing requirement: "Members inform clients, in writing, of this potential dilemma and how it is addressed in their jurisdiction and program." *Id.* The current edition adds that "it is not the role of treatment providers to attempt to determine or verify a client's legal guilt or innocence or to secure confessions of unreported or undetected sexually abusive behaviors," and that the influence of denial and minimization on recidivism risk "has not yet been clearly established." ATSA *Best Practice Guidelines for Men* Guidelines 12.05, 12.06 (6th ed. 2025). Mr. Bernecky has never been given that warning, in writing or otherwise, by anyone. He does not ask this Court to enforce a professional guideline against anybody, and nothing in this record establishes that any clinician treating him belongs to that association. He offers it for a narrower purpose. The profession that administers these examinations regards a written warning about criminal exposure as the least a man is owed before he answers. Special Condition (g) supplies no warning and commands the answer.

**The counsel problem.** Whether a particular invocation of the privilege is valid is a question of law. *Hoffman*, 341 U.S. at 486. Mr. Bernecky is not a lawyer, and neither is the examiner across the table. He cannot make that determination in real time, and the cost of getting it wrong is a violation of supervision. So when any question is put to him under this condition — by an examiner, a treatment provider, or a probation officer — he will move at that moment for appointment of counsel under the Criminal Justice Act. Absent counsel actually available to him, he will invoke as to every question, because a layperson forced to decide question by question which answers are safe has no accurate way to do it and no obligation to guess. Congress has already made proceedings of this kind a mandatory-appointment category: representation "shall be provided for any financially eligible person who ... faces modification, reduction, or enlargement of a condition, or extension or revocation of a term of supervised release." 18 U.S.C. § 3006A(a)(1)(E). Mr. Bernecky requests appointment of counsel for this motion on that basis.

**What happens if this is not resolved now.** If the government or the Probation Office responds to a refusal with a violation report, an arrest, or a revocation hearing, Mr. Bernecky will raise this identical argument at that point, and this Court will decide this identical question — after a warrant, after a detention, and after the case has become a revocation proceeding. Rule 32.1's drafters wrote the answer to that sequence: a supervisee "should be able to obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it." Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's note to subdiv. (b) (1979). The Judiciary's own policy directs the officer administering this condition the same way: "[i]f the person on supervision refuses to answer a specific question on the grounds that it is self-incriminating, the officer should not compel (e.g., through threat of revocation) the person to answer the question," and where "there is uncertainty about whether the invocation of the privilege against self-incrimination is valid ... the probation officer should refer the matter to the court to make this determination." Guide to Judiciary Policy, Vol. 8E, § 250(b). Special Condition (g)'s "shall answer" does not merely omit that safeguard. It instructs the officer to do what the policy tells him not to do.

Two points bear on cost rather than entitlement. Whatever verification interest polygraph testing serves, it is not answering any demonstrated problem here: the record contains no instance of dishonesty by Mr. Bernecky toward a probation officer, a treatment provider, or this Court. And his record raises the stakes rather than lowering them — a compelled answer from a defendant with a prior conviction of this kind carries materially greater consequences than the same answer from a first offender, which is precisely why the protection has to operate at the moment of the question rather than after it.

**Relief requested (Part IV):** an order striking, in full and without replacement, Special Condition (g)'s compelled-answer sentence, its non-waiver sentence, and the now-surplus disclosure sentence, leaving Mr. Bernecky's obligation to *submit to* polygraph and CVSA testing intact; in the alternative, an order providing that he may invoke the privilege question by question without penalty, that no violation report may be filed based on a refusal to answer, and that any dispute about the validity of a particular invocation be referred to this Court; and appointment of counsel under 18 U.S.C. § 3006A(a)(1)(E).

### V. Special Condition (h) — Alcohol Abstinence

Special Condition (h) requires abstinence from alcohol "[w]hile in treatment or taking psychotropic medication." The abstinence requirement should attach when a physician says it should, and not otherwise.

The clause is live right now, and nobody is administering it. Mr. Bernecky is in treatment and is taking prescribed medication, so the condition's disjunctive trigger is satisfied twice over. Certification ¶ \_\_. No one — not his probation officer, not his treatment provider, not his prescriber — has told him he may not have any alcohol. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. Read literally, a total ban is in force and he is violating it whenever he has a beer. Read as anyone administering it appears to read it, nothing is in force at all. That gap between text and practice is the same defect running through the rest of this motion, and it is present, not projected.

Nothing in this record ties alcohol to Mr. Bernecky's offense, to his history, or to any violation. His offense involved images on a device. The Second Circuit vacated a comparable blanket restriction for exactly that gap. *United States v. Betts*, 886 F.3d 198, 202-03 (2d Cir. 2018). It has since applied the same rule to a nearly identical "during treatment" abstinence clause. *United States v. Rodriguez*, No. 22-1820-cr (2d Cir. Dec. 7, 2023) (summary order).

The wording compounds the problem. Because the trigger is disjunctive, talk therapy alone requires abstinence even though no interaction risk can exist without a medication. And treating "psychotropic medication" as one uniform category ignores that alcohol-interaction risk varies sharply by drug class. This clause is this District's standard template, not a finding made about Mr. Bernecky.

**Relief requested (Part V):** an order amending Special Condition (h) so that the alcohol-abstinence requirement applies only upon, and only for as long as, a treating physician determines that alcohol is contraindicated for a specific medication or treatment Mr. Bernecky is actually receiving.

### VI. Standard Conditions 5 and 8 — Residence Approval and Contact with Persons Convicted of Felonies

Standard Condition 5 requires Mr. Bernecky to live at a place "approved by the probation officer." Standard Condition 8 forbids him to knowingly communicate or interact with anyone he knows has a felony conviction without advance permission. Each tells him permission is required. Neither says what earns it.

**The residence condition needs a stated standard.** The changed circumstance is straightforward: supervision began in July 2026, so this problem could not have arisen, let alone been raised, in 2018. Since it began, no one has told Mr. Bernecky what standard governs approval of a residence, and he has looked for one himself and been unable to find it. Certification ¶ \_\_. Because nothing defines what satisfies "approved," an officer may condition approval on anything, and he has no way to show he has met a requirement nobody has stated. "A condition is too vague if it requires a reasonable person to speculate as to its meaning." *Carlineo*, 998 F.3d at 536. It is also a delegation problem: a court "may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122. The only articulated basis Probation has offered anywhere is that "the probation officer assesses whether the change is appropriate to meet the defendant's risk and needs and whether it poses any risk to the defendant or to others" — broad enough to justify any outcome and too vague to guide any. U.S. Courts, *Overview of Probation and Supervised Release Conditions*, ch. 2, § V (July 2024). Probation knows how to write a real standard when it wants one: its own guidelines require a written plan, supervisory approval, and a defined reasonable-suspicion showing before a search of Mr. Bernecky's home. Guide to Judiciary Policy, Vol. 8E, Appx. 3A. Nothing comparable governs a decision that can put him out of it.

**The felon-contact condition should require disclosure, not approval.** Mr. Bernecky raises this condition because he does not know what it requires of him, and the answer should come from the Court rather than from an accumulation of guesses made one encounter at a time.

The conditions of his life since release make that concrete. He lives in an apartment complex with numerous neighbors who have felony convictions. He is required to attend a counseling and support group composed of people convicted of felonies. He works, shops, and rides public transit in a society in which roughly eight percent of the adult population has a felony conviction. Sarah K.S. Shannon et al., *The Growth, Scope, and Spatial Distribution of People With Felony Records in the United States, 1948-2010*, 54 Demography 1795 (2017). Read as written, Standard Condition 8 requires him to obtain advance permission before saying good morning in a hallway.

The condition's premise also does not describe this offense or this defendant. Advance-approval gating of associations fits crimes committed by groups, in concert, or in conspiracy, where association with other offenders is itself the mechanism of the offense. Possession of child pornography is not that crime. Mr. Bernecky committed it alone, in his own home, and his own account of how he came to commit it twice is that he was isolated from other people rather than connected to the wrong ones. Certification ¶ \_\_. The Court then placed him for roughly eight years in a Bureau of Prisons facility where every person around him had a felony conviction, and he formed friendships there with people who encourage him toward a law-abiding life. *Id.* ¶ \_\_. Cutting him off from those people reproduces the circumstance that preceded the offense.

Probation's legitimate interest is in knowing who Mr. Bernecky spends time with and being able to ask about it. Disclosure serves that interest completely.

**Relief requested (Part VI):** an order (1) directing that Mr. Bernecky be provided a written standard governing what will satisfy "approved" under Standard Condition 5, with leave to challenge the substance of any standard Probation proposes; and (2) amending Standard Condition 8 so that he must disclose any ongoing association with a person he knows to have a felony conviction, rather than obtain advance permission for it, with the probation officer retaining the ability to inquire about any such association.

### VII. Standard Condition 12 — Risk Notification

Standard Condition 12, as it appears in Mr. Bernecky's Judgment, provides that "[i]f the probation officer determines that you pose a risk to another person (including an organization), the probation officer may require you to notify the person about the risk and you must comply with that instruction." That is the exact condition the Second Circuit held invalid, agreeing that it "is vague and affords too much discretion to the probation officer" and vacating it as indistinguishable from an employer-notification condition already struck for leaving the obligation to "the probation officer's unfettered discretion." *United States v. Boles*, 914 F.3d 95, 111-12 (2d Cir. 2019) (quoting *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 86).

Within two months of *Boles*, this District entered a standing order directing that this language be removed from every judgment imposing supervision and replaced with a version requiring the court itself to determine, based on "criminal record, personal history and characteristics, and the nature and circumstances of [the] offense," that a defendant poses a risk before any notification requirement attaches. *Amended Standing Order, In re United States v. Boles* (W.D.N.Y. Mar. 22, 2019) (Geraci, C.J.). That order directs amendment of "the Judgment and Commitment order in all criminal cases in which a term of probation or supervised release is imposed." Mr. Bernecky's Judgment was entered nine months earlier and was never conformed to it. At a pre-supervision meeting his probation officer presented him with this exact, superseded language and required him to sign an acknowledgment of it. Certification ¶ \_\_. He asks the Court to apply to him the correction this District has already ordered for everyone else.

He objects separately to the structure the corrected condition creates, and this motion is his first opportunity to do so. *Boles* was decided in January 2019 and the standing order implementing it was entered March 22, 2019. Mr. Bernecky's conviction became final in June 2018. The condition he is objecting to did not exist while any avenue of direct review was open to him; he could not have challenged in 2018 a replacement condition no court had yet written. The objection is raised at the first moment it could be raised, and it is not waived.

Under the standing order, a risk determination is made between the probation officer and the Court. Mr. Bernecky would have no notice of it, no opportunity to be heard, no ability to present evidence, and no ability to contest any assertion made about him — while the consequence is that he must go tell a third party that he is dangerous. Where Probation believes a risk notification is warranted, it should proceed by petition and summons, on notice. And because such a proceeding would determine whether Mr. Bernecky must disclose himself as a danger to a named person, counsel should be appointed for any hearing on it.

**Relief requested (Part VII):** an order (1) amending Standard Condition 12 to match this District's March 22, 2019 standing order following *Boles*; and (2) providing that any risk determination under the amended condition be initiated by petition and summons, with notice to Mr. Bernecky, an opportunity to be heard, and appointment of counsel for any hearing.

## CONCLUSION

Every condition addressed above shares the same defect. Each was written broadly enough to cover a worst case, at a moment when nobody had to test its limits, and each is now being read as broadly as its words can be stretched by whoever happens to be applying it. Three have already been stretched that far — the employer dispute, the browser-less television locked down over YouTube, and the risk-notification language this District ordered removed seven years ago and a probation officer asked Mr. Bernecky to sign anyway. The rest have not yet had the occasion.

None of this requires the 2018 Judgment to have been wrong. It requires only that someone say what it means. Doing that now costs the government nothing it is entitled to. Not doing it means the answers get supplied one at a time, by people without the authority to supply them, and reviewed by this Court only after Mr. Bernecky has paid for the guess.

For the foregoing reasons, Mr. Bernecky respectfully requests that the Court grant the relief specified at the end of each Part above, namely:

1. **Special Condition (a) — employment.** The relief requested in Part I.A.

2. **Special Condition (a) — CIMP Participant Agreement.** The relief requested in Part I.B as to Items 3, 5, 9, 16, 17, and 21.

3. **Special Condition (a) — monitoring method.** The relief requested in Part I.C, with an evidentiary hearing on technical feasibility if Probation contests it.
