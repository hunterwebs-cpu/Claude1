## 2. ISSUES AND SUMMARY CONCLUSIONS

**Issue 1:** What does New York case law actually require to establish "necessity" for County Law § 722-c expert funding?

**Conclusion:** The governing formulation, as stated in the NYSDA practice guide and attributed to *People v. Lowery*, 7 Misc 3d 1032(A) (White Plains City Ct. 2005) (tier (b) — guide's characterization, not independently read), is that the application must provide "specific factual details which show to a reasonable probability that the forensic services would aid in the defense or produce relevant evidence." That single sentence is doing the real work; everything else in the case law is elaboration on its three components — relevance (is the issue significant to the case), materiality (are there challengeable conclusions the expert would actually address), and purpose (what, specifically, will the expert do). "Bare bones" assertions that an expert "would be helpful" fail as a matter of course. Financial inability is a separate, independent element with its own body of case law. Denials are reviewed only for abuse of discretion, which makes getting the written application right the first time far more important than it would be under de novo review.

**Issue 2:** Is 18 U.S.C. § 3006A(e) a useful federal analog?

**Conclusion:** Yes, candidly and specifically useful — more so than a generic "federal courts do this too" citation, because New York's own § 722-c case law has *already* imported *Ake v. Oklahoma* directly into a § 722-c necessity analysis (*People v. Vale*, 133 A.D.2d 297, 299–300 (1st Dep't 1987)), which gives the Writer a domestic vehicle for the federal standard rather than a freestanding constitutional argument. The actual § 3006A(e) test — read directly by me from *United States v. Alden*, 767 F.2d 314, 318–19 (7th Cir. 1984) — is the "private attorney" standard (would a reasonable retained attorney, for a paying client, have engaged this service), tempered by a requirement that the request not be a "fishing expedition" and that the defendant show a "plausible defense" the service would support. This maps onto New York's relevance/materiality/purpose framework closely enough to be persuasively cited by analogy, with the candid caveat that it is federal, non-binding, and arose in a criminal-trial insanity-defense context, not a civil SORA classification.

**Issue 3:** Does the client's "general low-recidivism research supports the necessity of an individualized expert evaluation" theory actually work?

**Conclusion:** It can work, and there is real doctrinal support for it — but only if it is framed as establishing *why an individualized showing is required* (because *Gillotti* itself and, more pointedly, *People v. Curry*, 2017 NY Slip Op 09184 (2d Dep't) (tier (b)), have already told SORA respondents that generalized/aggregate risk data does not by itself move a court — only "specific, unique risk factors" tied to the individual do), rather than as a substitute for the expert. Framed that way, the general research does exactly what the § 722-c materiality/purpose showing requires: it establishes that the "threat to public safety" question is a genuinely contested, individualized factual question this specific record never addresses, which is precisely the kind of showing *Vale* and *Hatterson* found sufficient. Framed the other way — cited as if the aggregate statistics alone answer the individual question — it hands the People or the Board the exact "why do you need $3,000 of public money when you already have the studies in hand" objection the case law's own "bare assertions" and "second expert unnecessary" line of cases (*Rockwell*, *Brand*, *Garfield M.*) would support. Section 4.C works through this in full.

**Issue 4:** What is the concrete, practical recommendation for Bernecky's own motion?

**Conclusion:** Build the necessity showing around the one specific, provable gap in this record — Override 1 supplies no evidence at all on "threat to public safety" — use the general research only to establish that this gap is a live, contested, individualized question (not to answer it), name a specific CP-offender-risk-qualified proposed expert and a specific projected scope of services, and file now, well ahead of September 11, so the application cannot be read as dilatory. Section 6 sets this out in full, including a genuinely useful, previously unflagged finding: a Monroe County Court judge has already granted § 722-c funds on a "state of the science is uncertain" theory, and the Fourth Department — the actual controlling department for this hearing — affirmed that grant.

## 4. DISCUSSION / ANALYSIS

### Issue 1: The actual New York necessity standard

**A. The statute's bare elements, and what "necessity" adds on top of them.**

County Law § 722-c itself requires, as the prior memo already established: (1) a finding that "investigative, expert or other services are necessary," and (2) a finding of financial inability. The statute does not define "necessary." The NYSDA guide — which I read directly, in full, tier (a) as to what the guide itself states — organizes the necessity showing around three components, each with its own case law: **relevance**, **materiality**, and **purpose**. The single clearest statement of the operative test, which the guide attributes to *People v. Lowery*, 7 Misc 3d 1032(A) (White Plains City Ct. 2005) (tier (b), not independently read by me), is:

> "specific factual details which show to a reasonable probability that the forensic services would aid in the defense or produce relevant evidence."

This is the closest thing New York has to a unified test, and it is functionally the same two-part inquiry the federal courts use under § 3006A(e) (see Issue 2 below): a reasonable-probability standard, tied to specific facts, not a bare assertion of helpfulness.

**B. What fails — the "bare assertion of relevance" line of cases.**

The guide collects a consistent, repeated pattern across denials, all tier (b) (guide's characterization, not independently read by me): *People v. Rockwell*, 18 A.D.3d 969 (3d Dep't 2005) (no error denying investigator funds where "defendant only asserted that an investigator would be helpful"); *Matter of Jack McG.*, 223 A.D.2d 369 (1st Dep't 1996) (denial affirmed where the claim that a defense psychiatrist's testimony might "add insight" into an existing evaluation "was insufficient to require granting of request"); *People v. Gallow*, 171 A.D.2d 1061 (4th Dep't 1991) (relevance to an issue in the case "is not by itself sufficient; a showing must be made that expertise is necessary for resolution of the issue"); *People v. Moore*, 125 A.D.2d 501 (2d Dep't 1986) (denial of fingerprint-expert funds upheld for failure to demonstrate necessity); *People v. Pride*, 79 Misc 2d 581 (Sup. Ct., Westchester Co. 1974) ("defendant's moving papers are of little help to the court in the resolution of [the] question [of necessity]"). The common thread across all five: the applications stated a conclusion ("this would help") rather than facts connecting the specific expert to a specific, contested issue in the specific case.

There is a second, related pattern worth flagging for this case specifically: courts deny a *second* or *additional* expert where the record already contains sufficient expert or evaluative input on the same question. *People v. Brand*, 13 A.D.3d 820 (3d Dep't 2004) (no need for a second defense psychiatric expert where the first already covered the ground); *Matter of Garfield M.*, 128 A.D.2d 876 (2d Dep't 1987) (no abuse of discretion denying an independent psychological expert given "the extensive evaluation and psychological examination of the appellant by the Family Court Mental Health Services and the Probation Department"). Bernecky's case is the mirror image of *Garfield M.* — the Board's own record contains *no* evaluation of him at all on the "threat to public safety" prong, which is precisely why *Garfield M.*'s reasoning does not apply against him, and precisely why the motion should say so affirmatively rather than leave the point for the Board to raise.

**C. What succeeds — materiality, purpose, and the *Vale*/*Hatterson* line.**

The clearest granted examples, all tier (b) (guide's characterization): *People v. Hatterson*, 63 A.D.2d 736 (2d Dep't 1978) (denial of physician/psychiatrist funds was "an improvident exercise of discretion" where the prosecution offered expert psychological testimony in its case-in-chief and on rebuttal, and the defense sought to challenge those assertions); *People v. Jones*, 210 A.D.2d 904 (4th Dep't 1994), aff'd 85 N.Y.2d 998 (1995) (abuse of discretion to deny neurological testing where a defense physician recommended it based on a documented childhood head injury and it was tied to the defendant's justification defense); *People v. Tyson*, 209 A.D.2d 354 (1st Dep't 1994) (abuse of discretion to deny a voice-identification expert where it would seriously damage the complainant's credibility on a key contested issue); *People v. Salce*, 124 A.D.3d 923 (3d Dep't 2015) (defendant entitled to an assault/knife-expertise expert to rebut the prosecution's theory that the accuser's wound pattern was inconsistent with self-defense). Each shares the same structure: a specific, existing, contested factual dispute in the record, and a specific explanation of what the requested expert would do to address it — not a general subject-matter interest.

The most important case for this specific motion, however, is *People v. Vale*, 133 A.D.2d 297, 299–300 (1st Dep't 1987) (tier (b), guide's characterization — not independently read by me, and flagged for pull-and-confirm below), because it is the vehicle that already imports the *Ake* standard directly into New York's own § 722-c case law:

> "[W]hen a state undertakes to prosecute an indigent defendant, it must also take whatever measures are necessary to assure that the defendant is able to participate meaningfully in the proceeding. The proceeding will otherwise be fundamentally unfair and offensive to the due process guarantees of the Fourteenth Amendment…. [A]n indigent need not show that an insanity defense 'might succeed' to obtain access to expert psychiatric assistance, but only that the issue of the defendant's sanity will be an important factor at trial."

The *Vale* court reversed a conviction, deeming the denial of the § 722-c application "most improvident." This is doctrinally significant for the Writer: it means the argument does not need to reach for *Ake* as a freestanding, untested constitutional claim (as the first memo candidly noted it would have to for a direct due-process theory) — a New York Appellate Division has already held that *Ake*'s reasoning is *built into* the § 722-c necessity standard itself. The honest limitation: *Vale* is a criminal trial case addressing an insanity defense, not a SORA classification proceeding, and I have not independently confirmed its text — it needs to be pulled before the exact quotation goes in a filing.

**D. Standard of review, and why the written application matters more than it otherwise would.**

Per the NYSDA guide (tier (a) for what the guide states; the underlying cases are tier (b)): denials are reviewed only for abuse of discretion, *People v. Cronin*, 60 N.Y.2d 430 (1983); *People v. Mooney*, 76 N.Y.2d 827 (1990), and an Article 78 proceeding will not lie to compel a grant — direct appeal is the only avenue, *Brown v. Rohl*, 221 A.D.2d 436 (2d Dep't 1995); *De Jesus v. Armer*, 74 A.D.2d 736 (4th Dep't 1980). The guide's own practical advice is directly on point for this case: applications must be **written**, not oral (*Dove*, 287 A.D.2d 806, 807 (3d Dep't 2001)), and should be filed **before** the hearing rather than during it — courts have denied post-hoc or mid-hearing applications specifically because of their timing, *Matter of Tiarra D.*, 124 A.D.3d 973 (3d Dep't 2015); *People v. Barber*, 60 A.D.2d 747 (4th Dep't 1977). Given the September 11, 2026 hearing date, this is not a hypothetical concern — it is a scheduling deadline the Writer needs to build the motion's timeline around now.

### Issue 2: The federal § 3006A(e) analog — the actual test, read directly

**The statute and the "private attorney" test, read directly from *Alden*.** I obtained and read, in raw extracted text, *United States v. Alden*, 767 F.2d 314 (7th Cir. 1984) (tier (a) — I read this myself, not a fetch-tool summary). Section 3006A(e)(1) provides:

> "Counsel for a person who is financially unable to obtain investigative, expert, or other services necessary for an adequate defense may request them in an ex parte application. Upon finding, after appropriate inquiry in an ex parte proceeding, that the services are necessary and that the person is financially unable to obtain them, the court … shall authorize counsel to obtain the services."

*Alden* then states the operative test, quoting *United States v. Bass*, 477 F.2d 723, 725 (9th Cir. 1973):

> "A test commonly used to determine whether psychiatric assistance is 'necessary for an adequate defense' is the 'private attorney' standard, which has been stated as follows: 'The statute requires the district judge to authorize defense services when the defense attorney makes a timely request in circumstances in which a reasonable attorney would engage such services for a client having the independent financial means to pay for them.'"

*Alden*, 767 F.2d at 319. But the Seventh Circuit immediately qualified that standard on the facts before it:

> "[T]o require the government to pay for psychiatric services in this type of case just because a reasonable private attorney would do so might be to require the government to subsidize a 'fishing expedition,' which is not the purpose of section 3006A(e). … Accordingly, it is appropriate for the district court to satisfy itself that a defendant may have a plausible defense before granting the defendant's section 3006A(e) motion for psychiatric assistance to aid in that defense."

*Id.* Applying an abuse-of-discretion standard, *Alden* affirmed denial of psychiatric-assistance funding to two defendants whose own testimony at their ex parte hearings, combined with court-ordered psychiatric examinations that had already found them sane, gave the district court a reasonable basis to conclude a genuine insanity defense was not plausible. *Id.* at 319–20.

**A widely-repeated companion formulation.** A second, more frequently cited two-part test appears across multiple circuits (tier (b), reconstructed from search synthesis; the specific originating case was not independently confirmed and needs a direct pull before citation): a defendant must show a reasonable probability both (1) that the requested service would be of material assistance to the defense, and (2) that denial would result in a fundamentally unfair proceeding — with the showing itself requiring the defendant to explain why the expertise is needed, what it would accomplish, and why existing counsel could not gather or present the same evidence without it. This is, functionally, the same relevance/materiality/purpose structure New York's own § 722-c case law uses (Issue 1 above), which is precisely why the analogy is useful rather than merely decorative.

**Candid assessment of usefulness.** § 3006A(e) is not binding on a New York court and arose from a different, criminal-trial-specific statute. But three things make it more than a generic "other jurisdictions agree" citation: (1) the underlying reasonable-probability structure is functionally identical to New York's own test, so citing federal case law does not require importing an alien standard — it reinforces the same one; (2) New York's own § 722-c case law has already built the doctrinal bridge through *Vale*'s direct quotation and application of *Ake* (Issue 1.C above), so a federal citation here is following a path New York courts have already walked, not blazing a new one; and (3) the body of federal case law working out what "necessary" and "material assistance" mean in practice is considerably larger and more granular than New York's own thin § 722-c case law, which gives the Writer more raw material to draw analogies from when New York authority runs out on a specific sub-question (e.g., what showing suffices when the expert's role is evaluative/interpretive rather than trial-testimonial — exactly Bernecky's situation, since a SORA hearing has no jury and the expert's function is closer to *Vale*'s "assist in evaluation, preparation, and presentation" than to live cross-examination support).

### Issue 3: Testing the client's theory — does general low-recidivism research help establish necessity, or does it cut the other way?

This is the assignment's central question, and it deserves both readings worked through in full rather than a one-line answer.

**No case found squarely on point.** I searched this from several angles: SORA-specific necessity case law; the federal Criminal Justice Act literature; capital-sentencing future-dangerousness expert-funding case law (where base-rate/actuarial arguments are common on the merits, e.g., in the *Barefoot v. Estelle*, 463 U.S. 880 (1983), line of cases, but I found no case using general base-rate research as part of an *expert-funding necessity* showing rather than as merits evidence itself); SVP civil-commitment funding case law; and the general forensic-psychology literature on "reasoning from group data to individual cases" (sometimes called the "G2i problem" in that literature — see, e.g., the discussion in *Toward a Jurisprudence of Psychiatric Evidence: Examining the Challenges of Reasoning from Group Data in Psychiatry to Individual Decisions in the Law*, Univ. of Miami L. Rev. (tier (b), fetch-tool summary only — this source discusses the underlying epistemological problem but cites no case law directly on point and should be treated as background orientation only, not authority). **I found no case, in New York or elsewhere, that has squarely accepted or squarely rejected the specific argument the client proposes.** That is a genuine, honest gap — not a foreclosed position, but not a tested one either.

**Reading one — the theory helps, and there is real doctrinal support for why.** New York's own case law has, in a closely adjacent context, already drawn exactly the line this theory needs. *People v. Curry*, 2017 NY Slip Op 09184 (2d Dep't Dec. 27, 2017) (tier (b) — corroborated across three independent secondary sources, including a direct quotation reproduced in the New York Appellate Digest, but not independently read by me in raw form), rejected a defendant's attempt to use a low score on the Static-99R and Vermont Assessment — general, validated actuarial instruments — as a mitigating factor for downward departure, reasoning that permitting general instrument scores to substitute for the RAI would contradict the Guidelines' individualized structure. Critically, *Curry* did not close the door entirely: per the secondary-source quotation, the court left open that a defendant *could* rely on "specific, unique risk factors" identified within such an instrument, so long as they were individually identified rather than offered as a bare aggregate score. Read together with *Gillotti*'s own holding — already established at tier (a) in the sibling memo — that "there is nothing inherent in child pornography offenses that convinces us to completely ignore the plain language of the guidelines," 23 N.Y.3d at 858, the pattern across both cases is the same: **New York courts have told SORA respondents, repeatedly, that general/categorical/aggregate data about a class does not move a court on its own — individualized proof does.**

That is not an obstacle to the client's theory; it is the theory's best doctrinal support, correctly reframed. An expert-funding necessity motion does not need the general research to *win* the departure argument by itself — that would indeed invite the *Curry* objection. It needs the general research to do a narrower, different job: establish that "where does this particular respondent fall on the contact-recidivism risk spectrum" is a real, materially contested, individualized factual question in this case — one the Board's own Override 1 record never addresses at all — and that *Curry* and *Gillotti* themselves establish that only an individualized showing, not a citation to the general literature, can resolve it. Framed this way, the general research is not the evidence the motion is asking the court to accept; it is the reason an individualized expert is *necessary* rather than optional. That is squarely within the materiality/purpose showing *Vale* and *Hatterson* require: a specific, existing dispute (is Bernecky part of the low-risk or the elevated-risk subset of this heterogeneous population), and a specific explanation of what the expert will do about it (apply individualized clinical and/or actuarial assessment to answer that question for Bernecky specifically, which the aggregate literature by definition cannot do for any one person).

**Reading two — the theory backfires, and there is real risk in this reading too.** The "bare assertion of relevance" and "no need for a second expert" lines of case law (Issue 1.B above) supply the opposing court's best response, and it is a genuinely available one: if the studies are already in counsel's hands — the *Gillotti* 4% statistic, the USSC report, the Board's own Position Statement, all already gathered in this file — a court can reasonably ask why an additional $1,000–$3,000 (or more, if extraordinary circumstances are shown) of public funds is needed to say something the existing literature, argued by counsel directly in the departure memorandum, already says. This is not a hypothetical risk; it is exactly the *Rockwell*/*Jack McG.*/*Gallow* pattern — an application that identifies a general subject-matter interest but does not explain why *this* service, beyond what counsel and the existing record can already do, is necessary. If the § 722-c motion's own papers lean on the general studies as if they were the answer rather than the reason an individualized answer is needed, the motion risks being self-defeating on its own terms: "if the general research already tells us CP offenders are low-risk, why do you need an expert — just cite the studies," is a fair question a skeptical court could ask, and the case law gives that skepticism real teeth.

**Which reading is more likely to land, and why.** Reading one is the stronger reading, but only if the motion is drafted to foreclose reading two explicitly rather than leaving it for the Board to raise. The motion should state affirmatively, in terms, that the general research is *not* being offered as departure evidence and is *not* a substitute for the requested evaluation — it is offered solely to establish that the specific factual question at the core of Override 1 (where does this particular respondent, given his own individual history, fall within a population *Gillotti* itself recognizes is heterogeneous on exactly this axis) is a genuinely contested one that the current record does not and cannot answer without individualized clinical input. This tracks the doctrinal structure *Curry* and *Gillotti* already impose on the merits question, converts what could look like a self-defeating citation into the precise materiality showing *Vale*/*Hatterson* require, and preempts the Board's most obvious counterargument before it is made. A motion that instead leads with the statistics as if they carry independent weight, without that explicit framing, is vulnerable to exactly the reading the assignment asked me to test for — and should not be filed that way.

### Issue 4: Concrete recommendation

Everything above points toward one specific drafting structure. Set out here in the order the motion papers should actually make these points:

1. **Lead with the specific, provable evidentiary gap, not the general research.** The motion's first and strongest sentence should be the fact already flagged in the first memo's Recommendation 1: Override 1's entire supporting record addresses "risk of repeat offense" through the bare fact of the prior conviction and says literally nothing about "threat to public safety," the independent element § 168-l(6) requires. That is a specific, factual, record-grounded gap — exactly what *Lowery*'s "specific factual details" standard and the *Alden*/§ 3006A(e) "plausible defense" inquiry both demand, and exactly what the "bare assertion of relevance" cases (*Rockwell*, *Jack McG.*) punish the absence of.

2. **Use the general research (*Gillotti*'s 4% statistic, the USSC figures, the Board's own Position Statement) to establish that this gap is a genuine, contested, individualized question — explicitly, not implicitly.** State directly that these sources are offered only to show that CP offenders are not a monolithic risk population on the precise axis the statute requires the Board to assess, and that the Board's own record contains zero individualized assessment of where Bernecky specifically falls — not as departure evidence in their own right (that argument belongs in the departure memorandum, where it already lives) and not as a substitute for the requested expert. This framing is what separates Reading One from Reading Two in Issue 3 above, and it should not be left implicit.

3. **Name the mechanism the general research cannot supply.** Explain specifically that an individualized forensic risk evaluation — ideally using a validated CP-offender-specific instrument (CPORT) in combination with a full clinical interview addressing Bernecky's own treatment history, his 2017 polygraph outcome, and the disclosed 2005/2007 incidents directly — is the only available means of generating individualized evidence on "threat to public safety," as opposed to legal argument from aggregate data. This directly answers the *Curry*/*Gillotti* demand for individualized, not aggregate, proof, and gives the court a concrete "purpose" finding under the NYSDA guide's own framework.

4. **Address the double-edged risk candidly with the client and attorney before filing — this is a strategic call, not a research conclusion.** The 2005/2007 disclosed incidents are, per the sibling memo's own adverse-authority section, exactly the kind of history the Board's Position Statement and the USSC report both flag as an aggravating/risk-elevating factor. An individualized evaluation could come back unfavorable. That risk does not change the legal necessity analysis, but it should be flagged to the client and supervising attorney as a real possibility before the motion is filed, consistent with the first memo's Recommendation 8.

5. **Invoke *Vale* as the vehicle for the *Ake*/§ 3006A(e) analogy, not *Ake* standing alone.** *Vale* is New York's own § 722-c case law already applying *Ake*'s "important factor" standard; it is a stronger, more direct citation than reaching for *Ake* as an untested freestanding due-process theory, though it should be pulled and confirmed before the quotation is filed (see Section 7).

6. **Flag the Fourth Department, home-turf precedent affirmatively.** *People v. Bailey*, 47 Misc 3d 355 (County Ct, Monroe Co. 2014), aff'd 2016 NY Slip Op 07490 (4th Dep't) — a Monroe County Court judge granted § 722-c funds specifically because the state of the forensic science on a contested medical/scientific question had shifted and was uncertain, and the Fourth Department affirmed. This is not a SORA case, but it establishes, in the actual county and actual controlling department, that "the general science is unsettled/contested, and only individualized expert analysis of this specific case's facts can resolve it" is a theory this exact court system has already accepted. This is a genuinely persuasive, previously unflagged data point the Writer should use.

7. **File now, in writing, ex parte, well before September 11.** Per *Dove* and *Matter of Tiarra D.* (Issue 1.D above), timing itself is part of the necessity showing — an application made late or during the hearing is vulnerable on that basis alone, independent of its substantive merit.

8. **Confirm the current statutory compensation cap** ($1,000 per the NYSDA guide's quoted statutory text, versus $3,000 referenced elsewhere in this file's prior research) against current McKinney's before the motion states a specific dollar figure — this discrepancy remains unresolved across two memos now and should be closed before filing.

## 5. CONCLUSION

New York's actual § 722-c necessity standard is not a mystery, even without a citator: it is a reasonable-probability, specific-facts standard, built around relevance, materiality, and purpose, with a real and fairly consistent body of granted and denied examples once the NYSDA guide's synthesis is used as an orientation point. The federal § 3006A(e) analog is genuinely useful here — not decorative — because New York's own case law (*Vale*) has already imported *Ake*'s reasoning directly into the § 722-c inquiry, meaning the federal body of law is filling in a standard New York courts have already agreed to use, not urging them to adopt a foreign one.

On the client's theory specifically: my honest assessment is that it is a sound, potentially persuasive argument, but only in the "reason an individualized expert is necessary" framing, never in the "the statistics answer the question" framing. New York's own recent SORA case law (*Curry*) and its own Court of Appeals authority already in this file (*Gillotti*) both independently establish that generalized/aggregate data does not carry the day on the merits — which, correctly used, is exactly why an individualized expert is necessary rather than optional. Used the other way, the same case law becomes the Board's best weapon against the motion. This is a real, live choice the drafting has to make deliberately, not a settled question either way.

I found no authority — favorable or adverse — squarely deciding this precise question in an expert-funding-necessity posture, in New York or elsewhere. That should be stated to the Court as what it is: an argument of first impression built on sound-by-analogy doctrine, not a tested and accepted theory.
