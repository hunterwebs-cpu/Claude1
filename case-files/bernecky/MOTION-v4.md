# DEFENDANT'S MOTION TO MODIFY CONDITIONS OF SUPERVISED RELEASE
## PURSUANT TO 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2), FED. R. CRIM. P. 32.1(c), AND U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4

Defendant Jeffrey Bernecky, proceeding *pro se*, moves under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2) and Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32.1(c) to modify several conditions of supervised release imposed by the Judgment entered June 12, 2018. Dkt. No. 28.

---

## PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

A judgment's silence is not a blank space for someone else to fill in.

That is the whole of this motion. The conditions Mr. Bernecky is serving were written in 2018 for a man nobody would meet for another eight years. They were drafted broadly, at a hearing where the question in the room was how long he would spend in prison. Nobody at that hearing was asked what a computer-monitoring clause would mean to a manufacturing company's HR department, or what a treatment provider's intake packet would demand, or whether a man living in an apartment complex may say hello to the neighbor two doors down. The words went onto the page with those questions unasked, and eight years later the words are being read by whoever happens to be holding them.

Mr. Bernecky's supervision began on or about July 30, 2026, and the pattern showed itself immediately. An employer that had offered him permanent work read Special Condition (a) and asked whether hiring him would open the company's own computer systems to federal monitoring. Nothing in the condition answers that question. His probation officer gave two different informal answers over roughly two weeks, and the company told him not to report to work until the matter was settled. He lost about two weeks of pay at the exact moment he was leaving Bureau of Prisons custody and taking on a one-year lease. He was fortunate that the employer looked past the confusion and hired him anyway. The next employer may not.

The Judgment also required him to sign a twenty-one-item Computer Monitoring Program Participant Agreement that no judge has ever reviewed, which forbids him to run the antivirus software his operating system installed at the factory, forbids the private-browsing mode that ships as a default on every major browser, and lets a probation officer decide, by no stated standard, whether he may own a device at all. His personal telephone, on his own carrier plan, now tells him on its lock screen that it belongs to an organization. And Special Condition (g) commands him to answer questions under polygraph, offering in exchange a promise that he may argue about the answer later — which is not what the Fifth Amendment provides.

Mr. Bernecky does not ask this Court to revisit his sentence. He asks it to say what its own order means, now, so that he serves the next ten years knowing what each condition requires rather than learning it one violation report at a time. Congress made that authority available "at any time," 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2), and Rule 32.1's drafters explained why: "the probationer should be able to obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it." Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's note to subdivision (b) (1979).

## JURISDICTION AND AUTHORITY

This Court may "modify, reduce, or enlarge the conditions of supervised release, at any time prior to the expiration or termination of the term of supervised release." 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2). That authority exists "to account for new or unforeseen circumstances." *United States v. Lussier*, 104 F.3d 32, 36 (2d Cir. 1997).

The Second Circuit has already told a district court in this District to use this precise mechanism at this precise moment. *United States v. Balon* involved a W.D.N.Y. defendant and computer-monitoring conditions materially identical to Special Condition (a). The panel held the defendant's challenges unripe because "it is currently impossible to predict the state of computer technology at the commencement of Balon's supervised release period," dismissed that portion of the appeal, and instructed the district court "to reconsider, at the instance of the government or Balon and in light of *Lifshitz*, the special conditions regarding monitoring of Balon's computer at a time closer to Balon's term of supervised release." 384 F.3d 38, 40, 47 (2d Cir. 2004). It then held expressly "that changing computer technology is an appropriate factor to authorize a modification of supervised release conditions under Section 3583(e)." *Id.* at 47. *Balon* is the case that put Mr. Bernecky here, now.

*Balon* also marks the boundary of the rule that a condition's illegality is not itself a ground for modification. That rule, the panel explained, "held only that a condition of supervised release that was illegal *ab initio* could not be challenged in a Section 3583(e) proceeding and has no applicability to a case where fluid circumstances alter either the need for, or the efficacy of, certain conditions." *Id.* at 47 n.4; *accord United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748, 774 n.14 (2d Cir. 2023) (quoting *United States v. Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d 147, 152 & n.2 (2d Cir. 2020)). Every request below rests on circumstances that did not exist in 2018: a Participant Agreement Probation drafted and required eight years after sentencing, a category of consumer devices that did not exist in its current form, an employer dispute, a polygraph examination that is now imminent rather than hypothetical, and employment with a retirement benefit.

The Sentencing Commission has since made the same point as policy. Effective November 1, 2025, U.S.S.G. § 5D1.4(a) invites courts to conduct "an individualized assessment of the appropriateness of existing conditions ... in consultation with the probation officer after the defendant's release from imprisonment," on the Commission's stated rationale that conditions "are imposed at original sentencing, often years before the defendant begins supervision," and that circumstances "may change in and after prison, such that the original term and conditions may no longer be appropriate."

Where Probation has read a condition to reach further than this Court's own text authorizes, this Court supplies the remedy. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68 (citing *Villafane-Lozada*, 973 F.3d at 153, for the rule that where Probation "does overstep [its] authority," the sentencing court may provide relief).

## ARGUMENT

### I. Special Condition (a) — The Computer and Internet Monitoring Program

Special Condition (a) conditions Mr. Bernecky's use of any "internet capable device" on participation in CIMP, authorizes Probation to install monitoring applications on devices he "own[s] or operate[s]," authorizes examination of equipment he "own[s] or use[s]" when triggered by "impermissible/suspicious activity," and directs that any monitoring "be designed to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information." Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (a). This is the same text the Second Circuit construed on appeal from this District in *Kunz*. 68 F.4th at 754-55.

#### A. The Court's silence on employment means there is no condition on Mr. Bernecky's employment, and neither Probation nor an employer can be left to decide otherwise.

Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to state in writing that Special Condition (a) does not reach his employment and imposes no obligation on any employer.

Start with what an employment-facing condition requires. Congress authorized a court to order a defendant to "refrain, in the case of an individual, from engaging in a specified occupation, business, or profession bearing a reasonably direct relationship to the conduct constituting the offense, or engage in such a specified occupation, business, or profession only to a stated degree or under stated circumstances." 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(5). That provision is available for supervised release through § 3583(d), which permits a court to order "any condition set forth as a discretionary condition of probation in section 3563(b)." The Guideline that implements it tracks the statute and adds the findings the statute presupposes: a court may impose a condition "prohibiting the defendant from engaging in a specified occupation, business, or profession, or limiting the terms on which the defendant may do so," only on finding both "a reasonably direct relationship" between the occupation and "the conduct relevant to the offense of conviction," and that the restriction "is reasonably necessary to protect the public because there is reason to believe that, absent such restriction, the defendant will continue to engage in unlawful conduct similar to that for which the defendant was convicted." U.S.S.G. § 5F1.5(a). This is not a Guidelines formality layered on top of a statute. It is the statutory standard, written out.

Neither the statute nor the Guideline was invoked here. The government never asked for an occupational restriction. Probation never asked for one. No finding of either kind appears in the Judgment, in any Statement of Reasons, or in any filing by either party. And no such finding was available to be made: Mr. Bernecky's offense had no occupational element at all — the images were on devices he bought in his own name and kept in his own home, and no employer, workplace, or work account appears anywhere in the conduct of conviction. Certification ¶ __. Had anyone proposed an employment condition on that record, Mr. Bernecky would have objected, and neither element could have been satisfied. The silence in this Judgment is not an oversight. It is the absence of a restriction that had no basis to exist.

That silence has a practical consequence that runs in both directions. Mr. Bernecky must satisfy § 3583(e)(2)'s changed-circumstances standard to obtain relief. If the government should now assert that an employment restriction is necessary, it will be asking this Court to impose one for the first time, subject to the same statutory findings and the same standard.

Silence, once it exists, belongs to the Court to fill or leave empty — not to Probation. "A district court may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *United States v. Matta*, 777 F.3d 116, 122 (2d Cir. 2015), quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 762-63. Where Probation's own program terms reach past what a sentencing court's condition says, they "exceed[] the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court, and therefore may not be enforced as written"; a restriction that "would pose a significant burden on [a supervisee's] liberty ... would need to be imposed by the court and justified by particularized on-the-record findings" and "an express, case-specific rationale." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 767-68. A Probation-imposed term that "would contradict, rather than execute" the sentence pronounced is one "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose." *United States v. Browder*, 866 F.3d 504, 514 (2d Cir. 2017). And on the specific question of what an employer may be told, the sentencing court "must determine" the answer and "may not simply leave" it to a probation officer's "unfettered discretion." *United States v. Peterson*, 248 F.3d 79, 85-86 (2d Cir. 2001) (per curiam).

Mr. Bernecky does not fault his probation officer for being asked a hard question by a worried employer. But she was not without an answer. The law supplied one, and it does not depend on what the employer requested or on what she judged sufficient: a probation officer has no authority to add a restriction the condition does not contain.

The officer's answer went further still. She told the employer that it had some duty to monitor Mr. Bernecky beyond the company's own internal procedures. Certification ¶ __. This Court's power over the employer is zero. The company is not a party, was never heard, and holds its own Fourth Amendment rights in its own systems — rights that do not shrink because it hired someone on supervision. A probation officer cannot require the company to show her the data its systems generate, and cannot direct it to do anything differently. That is almost certainly why Special Condition (a) says nothing about employers in either direction: a sentencing court cannot strip an American company of its own constitutional protections by choosing words in a criminal judgment against someone else. The same limit applies to the other private institution in Mr. Bernecky's supervision — a court "lacks authority to tell a private treatment provider how to enforce, or not enforce, their rules and regulations." *United States v. McDonald*, No. 1:11-CR-01075-MV-1, slip op. at 12 n.2 (D.N.M. Jan. 6, 2026). What this Court does have power to do is limit Mr. Bernecky himself in the workplace. That was never contemplated here and is not necessary now.

*Brown* does not compel a different result. There, the Second Circuit was confronted with an express restriction on the use of employment computers — a condition that by its terms subjected "any electronic devices that Brown could use 'in the course of employment'" to "monitoring restrictions as permitted by the employer" — and held that the condition was not an occupational restriction under § 5F1.5(a) because it did not "target 'a specified occupation, business, or profession.'" 173 F.4th 45, 47, 50-51 (2d Cir. 2026). That is not this case. Here there is no such clause. Here there is utter silence — not from inadvertence, but because neither prerequisite to imposing such a restriction could have been established in the first place. *Brown* decided what to make of a condition that speaks to employment. Nothing in it holds that a condition silent on employment reaches employment anyway.

The Tenth Circuit faced the same ambiguity in a monitoring condition, and the sequence that followed is the sequence Mr. Bernecky asks this Court to complete here. In *United States v. Mike*, the government argued that the conditions "cannot be read fairly to encompass public computers or computers owned by employers." The panel disagreed. Pointing to a clause requiring the defendant to "warn others of the existence of the monitoring software placed on his/her computer *or any such computer the defendant may have access*," it held that "the probation officer could reasonably believe that the computer monitoring conditions apply not only to computers that Mike owns, but also to those owned by others, including Mike's employer," and remanded "to clarify their scope," instructing that if the district court intended that reach, "it must make the necessary findings" — the § 5F1.5(a) findings the same opinion set out. 632 F.3d 686, 694-95, 699 (10th Cir. 2011).

The district court then did what Mr. Bernecky asks this Court to do. It entered an amended judgment stating its purpose on the face of the document: to "clarify [the] computer monitoring condition of supervision pursuant to remand by the Court of Appeals for the 10th Circuit." Amended Judgment, *United States v. Mike*, No. 1:07-cr-01334-WJ (D.N.M. Mar. 18, 2011), ECF No. 99, at 1 (Johnson, J.). The clarified conditions tie every monitoring authorization to the defendant's own equipment — consent to examinations "of his/her computer(s)," consent to software installed "on his/her computer(s)" to monitor "his/her computer use" — and the clause the Tenth Circuit had singled out now ends at "his/her computer," the words "or any such computer the defendant may have access" having been deleted. *Id.* at 6-7. The court made no finding extending the conditions to an employer's computers, and none extending them to employment at all. The employment question was answered by narrowing the text and declining to make the findings that would have opened it.

That is the relief requested here, from the court that entered this Judgment, before the question costs another job.

Mr. Bernecky asks for a general ruling, not a case-specific patch. His injury is real and already paid: two weeks of lost wages and a job that turned for a time on an unwritten understanding. But the value of a ruling now is that it answers every version of this question rather than one. Where the Judgment is silent, the silence means there is no restriction — not that Probation may supply one and not that a third party must guess.

**Relief requested (Part I.A):** an order confirming that (1) Special Condition (a) does not apply to Mr. Bernecky's employment in any respect and imposes no obligation on any employer; (2) directing the Probation Office to advise his employer in writing that the employer owes the Probation Office nothing under this Judgment; and (3) confirming as a general matter that the Judgment's silence on a subject does not create a restriction on that subject, and that Probation may not treat such silence as authorization.

#### B. The CIMP Participant Agreement is a blanket form, and several of its terms restrict Mr. Bernecky beyond anything Special Condition (a) authorizes.

Exhibit A was never placed before Judge Larimer. It did not exist in 2018. It is a form Probation drafted for its entire CIMP caseload and required Mr. Bernecky to sign at intake eight years later, and the objection here is to how its uniform terms operate against him in particular. Mr. Bernecky does not suggest anything was concealed from the sentencing court; he did not ask for it to be produced either. The point is narrower and entirely doctrinal: an agreement built for blanket use cannot enlarge conditions that were supposed to be individualized.

*Kunz* supplies the framework, on this identical form. Where a CIMP provision is ambiguous, or would demand something absurd read with total literalism, the court construes it narrowly rather than striking it. 68 F.4th at 762-66. Where a provision cannot be reconciled with the special condition's own text, it "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court" and "may not be enforced as written." *Id.* at 767-68; *accord Browder*, 866 F.3d at 514. Five terms need attention.

**Item 3 — device authorization.** Item 2 requires advance notice of any device Mr. Bernecky owns or can access. Ex. A ¶ 2. Special Condition (a) requires exactly that, and it gives Probation complete visibility into what he has. Item 3 goes further: it forbids him to "use, own, or access any device which allows internet access other than what I have been authorized to use by the Probation Office," listing "internet appliances, mobile telephones, tablets, electronic games, gaming consoles, blu ray players, and SMART TV's." Ex. A ¶ 3. It states no standard for what earns authorization, no limit on what may be withheld, and it sorts devices by product label rather than by anything they do. A tablet ships with a general-purpose browser. A Blu-ray player reads an optical disc. Item 2 already achieves everything Special Condition (a) authorizes. Item 3 converts notice into permission.

*Kunz* struck down the same defect in this same program. It confronted a CIMP clause under which Probation could have limited the defendant to one internet-capable device and held that the clause exceeded the authority the district court delegated, because such a restriction "would pose a significant burden on his liberty, and therefore would need to be imposed by the court and justified by particularized on-the-record findings." 68 F.4th at 767-68. Item 3 is the broader form of that vice: rather than capping Mr. Bernecky at one device, it lets Probation decide, device by device and by no stated standard, whether he may have any.

Two facts show what that discretion produces. Mr. Bernecky's Roku television is locked against installing applications, for a risk a browser-less streaming device does not present, by a lock that does not reach the casting function through which outside content actually arrives. Certification ¶ __. And Special Condition (a) authorizes technology that can "surveil ... activity" — technology that watches and reports. When the message "This device belongs to your organization" appears on an Android device, it means whoever configured it has placed on it the highest level of remote control Android permits any application to have: the capacity to erase the device remotely, to block its own removal, and to dictate what other software may be installed. Google reserves that tier for equipment a company owns and issues to its employees. Google, *Full Device Management*, Android Enterprise Help, https://support.google.com/work/android/answer/9562029. Mr. Bernecky bought the phone and pays for the service.

He does not ask the Court to leave Probation without a tool for identifying devices that present a genuine monitoring risk. He asks that pre-authorization attach only to a device that has general-purpose web-browsing capability, or an application-installation mechanism through which an ordinary user could add web-browsing, anonymization, or peer-to-peer file-sharing capability, and that is compatible with the monitoring software Probation uses — and that whatever device-management privilege is used to enroll an authorized device be no broader than surveilling that device's activity requires. In exchange he offers Probation something the Judgment does not currently give it. Special Condition (a) conditions physical examination on a trigger: "[a]s triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity." Mr. Bernecky consents to physical inspection of any of his personal devices, on request, at any time, with no prior suspicion required. That consent reaches only devices within his own authority to consent to. *See United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164, 171 (1974).

**Item 21 — seizure authority.** Item 21 requires Mr. Bernecky to "permit the seizure and removal of hardware and data storage media for further analysis by law enforcement or the probation officer based upon reasonable suspicion that a violation of a condition of supervision or unlawful conduct by any person has or is about to occur." Ex. A ¶ 21. Special Condition (a) is narrower in three respects. It authorizes only "the U.S. Probation Office" to install applications and examine devices; Item 21 adds "law enforcement," empowered to seize hardware independently. Congress made a broader model available and the sentencing court did not select it. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(23) (search "by any law enforcement or probation officer"). Special Condition (a) ties examination to activity on equipment "owned or used by the defendant"; Item 21 substitutes "unlawful conduct by any person," so that a visitor's unrelated suspected crime becomes grounds to seize Mr. Bernecky's hardware. Every model Congress or the Commission has written ties the trigger to the defendant. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3563(b)(23); U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(7) ("unlawful conduct by the defendant"), quoted in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 758-59. And Special Condition (a) speaks of activity "occurring," while Item 21 adds seizure where conduct "is about to occur," converting an after-the-fact tool into a predictive one.

**Item 16 — security software.** Item 16 forbids Mr. Bernecky to "purchase, download, possess and/or install any anti-virus, anti-spyware, firewall, or internet security application without advanced authorization." Ex. A ¶ 16. Every modern operating system ships with antivirus and firewall software already installed and running, and a user does not "purchase, download, possess and/or install" what the manufacturer installed before the device reached him. Read literally, Item 16 requires Mr. Bernecky to seek authorization to leave his own operating system's factory defaults running, or else disable them and leave the very device Probation monitors open to compromise. Nothing in Special Condition (a) mentions security software. Another arm of the same federal government instructs every American to do what Item 16 gates: the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency "recommends that all computers and mobile devices on your home network run antivirus software," alongside its instruction to "[i]nstall a network firewall." CISA, *Home Network Security*, https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/home-network-security. Probation's interest is in knowing what security software runs on a monitored device. Notice serves that interest completely.

**Item 5 — private browsing.** Item 5 provides that "[w]hile using my monitored device, I shall not use any form of 'in-private' browsing at any time." Ex. A ¶ 5. Private browsing is designed to defeat third-party tracking and to clear local cache and cookies at the end of a session. It is not built to evade a monitoring agent with device-level access, and it does not: Google's own documentation states that "organizations that manage your network, like your school, employer, or internet service provider, may be able to observe your activity in Incognito." Google, *Browse in Incognito Mode*, Chrome Help, https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/95464. The feature ships as a default on effectively every browser; Mr. Bernecky uses DuckDuckGo, which offers tracker-blocking not as a mode a user switches into but as its only mode. DuckDuckGo, *Web Tracking Protections*, https://duckduckgo.com/duckduckgo-help-pages/privacy/web-tracking-protections. Item 5 therefore reaches an ordinary privacy feature used by ordinary people, on a rationale Special Condition (a) never adopted. If Probation's monitoring captures activity regardless of browser mode — which is what Special Condition (a)'s authorization to "surveil all activity" presumes — Item 5 adds nothing. If it does not, that is a fact about the method Probation chose, addressed in Part I.C, not a basis for a categorical ban the Judgment never mentions.

**Items 9 and 17 — encryption.** Item 9 provides that Mr. Bernecky "will not use any encrypted email accounts." Item 17 provides that he "shall not use any type of encryption unless pre-approved by my probation officer." Ex. A ¶¶ 9, 17. *Kunz* construed CIMP paragraph 18 — word for word the same text as Item 17 — and held that it "would be absurd to interpret Probation as purporting to require Kunz to ask permission every time he attempts to access a website whose URL happens to begin with the characters 'https'" or uses a service that encrypts messages in transit. 68 F.4th at 764. It construed the restriction narrowly rather than striking it: "so long as no encryption is initiated by or at the behest of Kunz, and so long as any encryption initiated by external forces does not meaningfully impair Probation's ability to monitor Kunz's behavior ... we construe the encryption restrictions to allow the reasonable use of services and resources that happen to employ encryption in some way." *Id.*

Item 9 needs a narrowing that goes beyond what *Kunz* had occasion to give, and the facts of this case supply the reason. Probation's monitoring captures Mr. Bernecky's screen. Whatever encryption protects a message between his device and someone else's is irrelevant to what Probation can see, because for Mr. Bernecky to read a message at all he must have it in front of him in plaintext — that is true of a routine Gmail message and equally true of a PGP-encrypted one. Encryption in transit and at rest hides nothing from a monitoring agent sitting on the device. What it does is protect him from everyone else. Transport-layer security is now baseline internet infrastructure rather than a choice a user makes; search engines effectively require it for indexing. Email encryption is a defense against the ordinary landscape of credential theft and data interception this Court is well aware of. Federal law assumes it in the most sensitive category there is: an entity transmitting a patient's own medical records to that patient must "[i]mplement technical security measures to guard against unauthorized access to electronic protected health information that is being transmitted over an electronic communications network," and the regulation names encryption as the mechanism for doing so. 45 C.F.R. § 164.312(e). Item 9, read literally, tells Mr. Bernecky he must be the one American who receives his own medical records over an unprotected channel. It also has a gap Item 17 does not: Item 17 contains an escape valve — "unless pre-approved by my probation officer" — and Item 9 has none, so there is no route to approval even for an employer's IT department requiring signed correspondence or for an encrypted portal used by this Court.

**Relief requested (Part I.B):** an order (1) replacing Item 3's device-authorization gate with the capability-based standard described above, coupled with Mr. Bernecky's consent to suspicion-free physical inspection of any device within his authority to consent to, and confining any device-management privilege used to enroll a device to no more than surveilling that device's activity requires; (2) striking from Item 21 the words "by law enforcement," "or unlawful conduct by any person," and "or is about to occur"; (3) narrowing Item 16 to require notice to Probation, rather than prior authorization, before installing or running any antivirus, anti-spyware, firewall, or other security software, whether factory-installed or added; (4) striking Item 5, or in the alternative construing it to reach only conduct undertaken to defeat monitoring; and (5) construing Items 9 and 17 to reach only encryption Mr. Bernecky himself initiates for the purpose of concealing illegal activity or defeating monitoring, and adding to Item 9 the pre-approval mechanism Item 17 already contains.

#### C. Special Condition (a) never says what monitoring consists of, and the method should be limited to the file types Mr. Bernecky's offenses actually involve.

Special Condition (a) promises that monitoring "shall be designed to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information or any private material that is not illegal or reasonably likely to lead to illegal material." Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (a). The Second Circuit has held that this exact language is a real constraint on Probation rather than hortatory text: it "constrains the supervisors in their surveillance ... by requiring them not to abuse their power and access." *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 766-67. A method that captures everything has nothing left to avoid, and the reading has already happened by the time any design would operate.

That promise is currently unadministrable, because nobody has told Mr. Bernecky what it means in practice. He has received no instruction on whether his probation officer has unfettered access to captured data or sees it only on a trigger; no instruction on what constitutes a report to Probation; and no instruction of any kind on privilege. Certification ¶ __. A privilege that the law protects even against a prison's walls cannot survive on an unwritten promise of good faith. At minimum, the process must answer three questions it has never answered: whether Mr. Bernecky may designate privileged material by folder or label so that it is excluded at capture; whether privileged material must be submitted for *in camera* review before anyone at Probation or the vendor reads it; and whether privileged material captured inadvertently must be suppressed, destroyed, and never disclosed.

The stakes are not abstract. Indiscriminate capture cannot distinguish an ordinary message from a communication with an attorney, "the oldest of the privileges for confidential communications known to the common law," *Upjohn Co. v. United States*, 449 U.S. 383, 389 (1981); a session with a psychotherapist, *Jaffee v. Redmond*, 518 U.S. 1, 10-15 (1996); a confession to clergy, *In re Grand Jury Investigation*, 918 F.2d 374, 384 (3d Cir. 1990); or a private communication between spouses, *Trammel v. United States*, 445 U.S. 40, 51 (1980). Nor does it stop at formal privilege. A modern device holds "the sum of an individual's private life," a volume of material that before such devices existed "most people cannot lug around" at all. *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373, 393-97 (2014). *Kunz* has recognized the same in this exact context: internet-connected devices are of "nearly essential" importance to modern life, so a monitoring choice made once at the outset governs a very large share of a decade. 68 F.4th at 773 (quoting *United States v. Eaglin*, 913 F.3d 88, 96 (2d Cir. 2019)).

The Second Circuit has already mapped the spectrum of methods, and the ends are not close. "[C]onstant inspection of the documents that Lifshitz creates on his computer might be more like searching his diary or inspecting his closets than it is like the highly targeted diagnosis accomplished by drug testing," while "software that alerted a probation officer only when Lifshitz was engaging in impermissible communications over e-mail or the Internet would bear much greater resemblance to screening a probationer's urine for particular drugs — as opposed to investigating a sample to ascertain all medical conditions from which the individual suffered." *United States v. Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d 173, 192 (2d Cir. 2004). Section 3583(d)(2) permits only the deprivation of liberty that is "reasonably necessary."

Applying that comparison to this case produces a specific and narrow line, and it is not the testing method — it is the file type. Mr. Bernecky's conduct in both convictions was possession of images. Certification ¶ __. He has never been alleged to have communicated with anyone about committing an offense. A drug test screens for particular drugs; the equivalent here screens for particular file types. Mr. Bernecky does not object to monitoring by the Probation Office's vendor, and he does not ask to go unmonitored. He asks that the monitoring be limited to inspecting photograph and video files on his devices, and that the vendor be prohibited from disclosing to Probation or to any other source anything other than image and video file content. That is the narrow, targeted method *Lifshitz* described, applied to the actual conduct of conviction. The Second Circuit has already identified a comparably focused tool as adequate for this offense category: "a more focused restriction, limited to pornography sites and images, can be enforced by unannounced inspections of [a probationer's] premises and examination of material stored on his hard drive or removable disks." *United States v. Sofsky*, 287 F.3d 122, 127 (2d Cir. 2002) (addressing a total access ban), quoted in *Lifshitz*, 369 F.3d at 189.

*Balon* anticipated the objection and invited the answer. Considering a defendant's proposal to limit monitoring to particular categories of data, the panel observed that a user could disguise a contraband file "as a 'word' or 'excel' document" by changing "the letters at the end of the file name," found no device then capable of doing what the defendant proposed, and said expressly: "Perhaps our analysis has missed something that Balon can clarify, but we offer this note so that Balon will address the issue when the time comes." 384 F.3d at 48. The time has come, and twenty-two years of technology have answered the note. Content-based file-type identification does not depend on a filename or an extension. Mr. Bernecky does not ask this Court to select a product or to resolve an engineering question on the papers. He asks the Court to set the legal boundary — image and video files, nothing else disclosed — and to direct the Probation Office to implement it. If Probation contends that no available technology can hold that line, that is a factual dispute, and Mr. Bernecky requests an evidentiary hearing on it. The direction of the technology cuts in Probation's favor on feasibility: automated classification has made content-targeted monitoring more capable, not less.

**Relief requested (Part I.C):** an order (1) limiting monitoring under Special Condition (a) to the inspection of image and video files, and prohibiting the monitoring provider from disclosing to the Probation Office or to any other person any captured material other than image and video file content; (2) directing the Probation Office to implement that limitation and to report to the Court on the method selected; (3) directing that Mr. Bernecky be given written notice of what data is captured, who may access it, and what triggers a report; and (4) establishing a privilege protocol providing for designation, *in camera* review before any review by Probation or the vendor, and suppression and destruction of any privileged material captured inadvertently.

#### D. "Internet capable device," "operated," and "used" no longer describe a workable class of devices.

Special Condition (a)'s reach turns on three words that have not aged well. It conditions Mr. Bernecky's use of any "internet capable device" on CIMP participation, authorizes monitoring of devices "owned or operated" by him, and authorizes examination of equipment "owned or used" by him. Dkt. No. 28, Special Condition (a).

Federal law defines a "computer" as "an electronic, magnetic, optical, electrochemical, or other high speed data processing device performing logical, arithmetic, or storage functions." 18 U.S.C. § 1030(e)(1). A Texas Instruments graphing calculator satisfies that definition, and so, at the other extreme, do a car, an aircraft, and the touchscreen fare and information systems on modern mass transit — each of which is, in ordinary English, a thing a person "operates." Read literally, the condition tells Mr. Bernecky he may not board a bus without enrolling it in CIMP. The line that does real work is not connectivity and not the verb; it is capability — whether a device can access, search for, or display child pornography.

*Kunz* confirms that this Circuit reads this program's text sensibly rather than literally when a literal reading would sweep too far. 68 F.4th at 764-65. But *Kunz*'s facts were milder than these in one decisive respect: no one there had acted on the expansive reading, so the panel needed only say that nobody would. Here someone did — twice. An employer held Mr. Bernecky's job for two weeks over what "operated" and "used" might require of its own systems. Certification ¶ __. And Probation locked down a browser-less streaming television, telling Mr. Bernecky that YouTube presented a problem — a service that carries network news, congressional proceedings, instructional material, and nearly the full range of ordinary media. *Id.* ¶ __. An interpretive canon premised on everyone recognizing the same obvious meaning cannot resolve a dispute that has already demonstrated the meaning is not obvious.

*United States v. Leone* turned aside a vagueness challenge to a monitoring condition premised on "[m]icrowaves and modern refrigerators." No. 19-1670, 2020 WL 2610536 (2d Cir. May 20, 2020) (summary order). That challenge was facial and hypothetical, brought on a record containing no officer who had actually read the condition expansively — the problem this record does contain. Mr. Bernecky is not making Leone's argument. He does not ask the Court to hold Special Condition (a) unconstitutionally vague, and no relief he seeks depends on that label. He asks this Court to exercise its own § 3583(e)(2) authority at the moment *Balon* identified — and *Balon* is the case *Leone* relies on.

**Relief requested (Part I.D):** an order striking "or operated" and "or used," leaving "owned by the defendant" as the operative standard, and defining "internet capable device" to mean a device that has general-purpose web-browsing capability or an application-installation mechanism through which an ordinary user could add web-browsing, anonymization, or peer-to-peer file-sharing capability — together with a provision that, with the exception of employer devices necessary for work performance, Mr. Bernecky shall not personally and privately possess any unreported device meeting that definition.

### II. Special Conditions (b) and (h) — Treatment Provider Rules

Special Condition (b) requires Mr. Bernecky to "follow the rules and regulations" of a sex-offense-specific treatment program; Special Condition (h) places him in a mental health program supervised the same way. He objects to any provider rule, present or future, that conditions his liberty beyond what this Court's Judgment authorizes. What he asks for now is not a ruling on rules that have not yet been applied to him — it is a route to raise one when it happens.

*Browder* establishes that a provider rule cannot exceed the sentence. Browder's provider required him to sign an agreement barring contact with any minor, including his own children, though his sentence excluded his own children. He objected in writing and did not sign. His probation officer sought a violation anyway, and Browder was detained and found to have violated his conditions. 866 F.3d at 507-08, 513-14. It took a full appeal to establish that the rule "would contradict, rather than execute," his sentence, that "the Probation Office was plainly without power to impose it," and that Browder "was therefore well within his rights to object to that term of the agreement." *Id.* at 513-14. The court framed the question exactly as it arises here: "the key question is not whether permission, if sought, would be granted; it is whether Browder can be required, by the Probation Office or its designee, to ask in the first place." *Id.* at 514.

Browder did precisely what Rule 32.1's drafters said a supervisee should be able to do, and it still cost him an appeal rather than a request to his own sentencing court. Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's note to subdivision (b) (1979). The gap is procedural, and the fix is procedural. Mr. Bernecky asks first that the Probation Office be directed to state its own position, in writing, on whether a given provider rule is within the Judgment — a question Probation is closer to than this Court and should answer in the first instance — and second that, if it declines or overrules him, he may seek this Court's clarification before any violation report is filed on that basis. Nothing in this asks the Court to compel a provider to keep treating him; a provider remains free to terminate. It asks only that a termination following a timely written objection not itself be treated as a violation, with Probation making other treatment arrangements instead.

**Relief requested (Part II):** an order providing that where Mr. Bernecky believes a rule imposed by a provider under Special Condition (b) or (h), or a provider's application of such a rule, conditions his liberty beyond what this Judgment authorizes, he may object in writing to his probation officer, who shall respond in writing; that he may seek this Court's clarification if the objection is overruled; that no violation report may be filed and no violation found based solely on a provider's termination following a timely written objection made under this procedure; and that in that circumstance the Probation Office must make other arrangements for treatment.

### III. Special Conditions (d) and (i) — Financial Monitoring and Disclosure

Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to limit Special Condition (d) to the records capable of showing what it was written to detect, and to vacate Special Condition (i) once restitution is confirmed paid.

The changed circumstance is the point. Before his incarceration Mr. Bernecky had nothing, and an objection to a financial-disclosure condition would have been unripe at best — there were no finances to disclose. He is now in his thirties, employed full time, and his employer offers a 401(k). Certification ¶ __. Retail access to the securities markets has become vastly easier and cheaper in the years he was in custody. He wants to save for retirement and to invest, and he should not have to seek advance approval to do it or produce brokerage and retirement records to a probation officer.

Special Condition (d) states its own purpose: to monitor compliance "with not buying or subscribing to online services that provide child pornography." No one has ever alleged that Mr. Bernecky purchased child pornography; his convictions are for possession, and nothing in the record suggests his access involved payment. Even taking the stated purpose at face value, the records capable of showing such a payment are a specific and identifiable set — credit and debit card statements, PayPal or comparable electronic transaction records, and cryptocurrency wallet or exchange records. A 401(k) statement cannot show a payment to a child-pornography vendor. Neither can a brokerage confirmation. And detecting such a payment requires nothing beyond the payee's name: the purpose is served in full once Mr. Bernecky discloses to whom a payment was made, without the amount, the account number, or anything else.

*United States v. Jenkins* struck a condition forbidding a child-pornography defendant from incurring any credit card charge or opening any line of credit without prior approval, holding that "[n]othing in the record suggests these restrictions ... are 'reasonably necessary' ... to protect the public or to deter [him] from continuing to engage in the conduct for which he was convicted — possession of child pornography," and asking why the defendant "should be prohibited from buying a drink on an airplane or taking an Uber ride or making a purchase on Amazon unless the transaction is pre-approved by a probation officer." 854 F.3d 181, 194 (2d Cir. 2017). Special Condition (d) requires disclosure rather than permission, and the two are not the same. But *Jenkins*'s point survives the distinction: a condition responding to a possession offense with no financial element cannot reach further than that offense gives Probation reason to look.

Special Condition (i) presents a different problem. It ties Probation's access to Mr. Bernecky's finances, and its control over his assets, to "the collection of any unpaid fine or restitution." Mr. Bernecky believes the $500 restitution has been paid in full. If so, the condition's entire stated purpose is spent — and the guideline it implements authorizes a condition of this kind only "if the court imposes an order of restitution, forfeiture, or notice to victims, or orders the defendant to pay a fine," U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(3), with the companion debt-obligation condition applying only where the defendant is not "in compliance with the payment schedule," *id.* § 5D1.3(d)(2).

**Relief requested (Part III):** an order (1) clarifying that Special Condition (d) requires disclosure only of the payee's name for transactions made by credit card, debit card, PayPal or comparable electronic payment service, or cryptocurrency, and does not reach the amount of any transaction, any account number, or any brokerage, investment, or retirement account record; (2) confirming that neither Special Condition (d) nor Special Condition (i) requires advance approval for Mr. Bernecky to participate in an employer-sponsored retirement plan, to open or fund a brokerage account, or to select investments; and (3) vacating Special Condition (i) upon confirmation that the $500 restitution obligation has been satisfied.

### IV. Special Condition (g) — Polygraph and Computerized Voice Stress Analyzer Testing

The Fifth Amendment protects the right to refuse to answer an incriminating question at the moment the question is asked. Special Condition (g) takes that right away and hands back a receipt. It commands that Mr. Bernecky "shall answer the questions posed during the examination," and offers in exchange a preserved right to argue, later and somewhere unspecified, about what may be done with the answer. The Court should strike the command.

Three sentences should go: "The defendant shall answer the questions posed during the examination, subject to the defendant's right to challenge in a court of law the use of such statements as violations of the defendant's Fifth Amendment rights"; "In this regard, the defendant shall be deemed not to have waived the defendant's Fifth Amendment rights by making any such statements"; and "The results of any polygraph pre-tests and polygraph examinations may be disclosed to the US Probation office and the Court, but shall not be further disclosed without a court order." The third is surplusage once the first two are gone — it exists only to govern what becomes of compelled statements. Everything else stands, including the opening command that Mr. Bernecky *submit to* testing on the schedule the condition sets.

**The governing rule.** Compulsion exists where the government, "either expressly or by implication, asserts that invocation of the privilege would lead to revocation" or another substantial penalty. *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 435 (1984). Murphy lost, and the two reasons he lost are the two reasons this condition is different. Murphy's condition said nothing about his freedom to decline to answer — it required truthfulness if he spoke, not an answer to every question put to him — and nothing in his case, express or implied, threatened revocation for invoking. Special Condition (g) is the mirror image on both. It speaks directly to Mr. Bernecky's freedom to decline, and commands that he "shall answer." And it is a condition of supervised release, so failing to comply with it is by definition a violation. The threatened penalty is not something an officer would have to add in the room. It is in the condition's own grammar.

That matters because of what invoking has always required. A witness invokes by declining to answer, with nothing further required of him at that moment; the validity of the claim is for a court to assess afterward if it is challenged. *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951). Special Condition (g) tells Mr. Bernecky that the single act the Constitution has always said suffices to protect him is itself a violation of his supervision. That is *Murphy*'s classic penalty situation reached by no analogy at all.

**The savings clause is not immunity.** *United States v. Von Behren* held that "a threat to revoke one's probation for properly invoking his Fifth Amendment privilege is the type of compulsion the state may not constitutionally impose," and identified in footnote 9 the circumstances that would change the outcome: "The outcome would be different, and thus the government's argument would be valid, if Mr. Von Behren had refused to answer a question relevant only to his supervised release status ..., or if he had refused to answer a question that did not involve the risk of self-incrimination, or if he were given immunity and still refused to answer any questions." 822 F.3d 1139, 1149 n.9, 1150 (10th Cir. 2016).

Special Condition (g) supplies none of the three, and the third is where the difference lives. Immunity is a promise that an answer and its fruits may not be used against the speaker — the Fifth Amendment's own coextensive substitute for the privilege. That is what *Murphy* itself contemplated: the government may "insist on answers to even incriminating questions and hence sensibly administer its probation system, as long as it recognizes that the required answers may not be used in a criminal proceeding." 465 U.S. at 435 n.7. What Special Condition (g) supplies is a preserved right to litigate. A defendant given immunity has lost nothing by answering. A defendant given a right to litigate has lost the disclosure itself and keeps only an argument about what may be done with it.

The threshold that triggers the privilege is low: a statement is incriminating if it would furnish "a lead or a link in the chain of evidence that could lead to prosecution." *Von Behren*, 822 F.3d at 1144 (quoting *Maness v. Meyers*, 419 U.S. 449, 461 (1975)). No active investigation is required; answers that could supply a lead or a link in a future investigation are incriminating too. *United States v. McDonald*, No. 1:11-CR-01075-MV-1, slip op. at 6 (D.N.M. Jan. 6, 2026). Once a person has a valid basis to invoke, the government may not compel the answer at all. It does not get to compel the answer now on the promise that a court will decide later whether it should have. A later ruling that a statement is inadmissible cannot make an investigator un-know what the answer told him or stop him from following where it leads. And the savings clause is indefinite by its own terms about where that later litigation would occur: it preserves a right to challenge use "in a court of law," without saying which one.

**The two decisions closest to this question turn on a verb this condition does not use.** In *United States v. Richards*, the Tenth Circuit upheld a condition requiring the defendant "to submit to periodic polygraph testing," on a record where nothing showed the government had threatened revocation for a valid invocation, and where the condition itself barred use of polygraph results "for purposes of instituting or instigating criminal charges." 958 F.3d 961, 964, 966-68 (10th Cir. 2020). Judge Vázquez reached the same result on the same reasoning as to a condition requiring the defendant to "submit to clinical polygraph examinations," and said why in terms that decide this case: a defendant "may be required to 'submit to clinical polygraph examinations,' but that does not necessarily translate to 'truthfully answer even incriminating questions during mandatory clinical polygraph examinations.'" *McDonald*, slip op. at 11. Special Condition (g) closes that gap by its own terms. It does not tell Mr. Bernecky to submit to an examination; it tells him to answer the questions posed during one. Constitutional avoidance permits a court to choose among readings a text can bear. There is no reading of "shall answer" that leaves open the room to decline that "submit to" leaves open. Both courts also left the door open to exactly this motion: *Richards* invited a renewed challenge if the government's position changed, 958 F.3d at 968, and *McDonald* said the court "would consider adding language akin to that approved in *Richards*" on a motion to modify, slip op. at 13.

The Second Circuit has not decided this question. Its one polygraph decision, *United States v. Johnson*, upheld language close enough to Special Condition (g) that the resemblance is unlikely to be coincidental — an answer requirement paired with a preserved right to challenge later use. 446 F.3d 272 (2d Cir. 2006). The theory presented there was narrower than the one raised here: Johnson argued that, absent formal use and derivative-use immunity, he faced an unconstitutional choice between confessing to past conduct and standing in violation for silence. Nobody in *Johnson* argued that compelling the answer itself, at the moment it is demanded, is the constitutional violation. That is Mr. Bernecky's theory, and *Johnson* never reached it.

**Why now.** This is not a challenge to the legality of a condition as imposed in 2018. *See Balon*, 384 F.3d at 47 n.4. In 2018 there was no examination scheduled, no question posed, and nothing for anyone to compel; the dispute could not have been presented. It can be presented now, and it will be presented one way or another. Mr. Bernecky states plainly what he intends to do: he will not follow an answer-now-challenge-later model. He will invoke the Fifth Amendment question by question, at the moment each question is asked, on any question whose truthful answer could supply a lead or a link. That is the posture Judge Vázquez expressly recommended to defendants navigating these conditions without counsel, writing to give them "a practical road map": "defendants should err on the side of asserting the privilege even where the question does not directly ask about the commission of a crime." *McDonald*, slip op. at 12. The Judiciary's own policy directs the officer administering this condition to respond the same way: "[i]f the person on supervision refuses to answer a specific question on the grounds that it is self-incriminating, the officer should not compel (e.g., through threat of revocation) the person to answer the question," and where "there is uncertainty about whether the invocation of the privilege against self-incrimination is valid ... the probation officer should refer the matter to the court to make this determination." Guide to Judiciary Policy, Vol. 8E, § 250(b). Special Condition (g)'s "shall answer" does not merely omit that safeguard. It instructs the officer to do what the policy tells him not to do.

If the government or the Probation Office responds to Mr. Bernecky's refusal with a violation report, an arrest, or a revocation hearing, he will raise this identical argument at that point, and this Court will decide this identical question — after a warrant, after a detention, and after the case has been converted into a revocation proceeding. Rule 32.1's drafters wrote the answer to that sequence: "the probationer should be able to obtain resolution of a dispute over an ambiguous term or the meaning of a condition without first having to violate it." Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1 advisory committee's note to subdivision (b) (1979).

Two further points bear on cost rather than entitlement. The record contains no instance of dishonesty by Mr. Bernecky toward a probation officer, a treatment provider, or this Court. Certification ¶ __. Whatever verification interest polygraph testing serves, it is not answering a demonstrated problem in this case. And Mr. Bernecky's record raises the stakes rather than lowering them: a compelled answer from a defendant with a prior conviction of this kind carries materially greater consequences than the same answer from a first offender, which is precisely why the protection has to operate at the moment of the question rather than after it.

The Fifth Amendment's text is not an interpretive puzzle, and its application to compelled answers is not a recent development. A court that resolves this question now, rather than waiting for a revocation proceeding to force it, is doing the work that makes judicial review a real check on the government's power to compel testimony instead of a remedy available only after the compulsion has already happened.

**Relief requested (Part IV):** an order striking, in full and without replacement, Special Condition (g)'s compelled-answer sentence, its accompanying non-waiver sentence, and the now-surplus disclosure sentence, leaving Mr. Bernecky's obligation to *submit to* polygraph and CVSA testing otherwise intact; and, in the alternative, an order providing that Mr. Bernecky may invoke the privilege question by question without penalty, that no violation report may be filed based on a refusal to answer, and that any dispute about the validity of a particular invocation be referred to this Court.

### V. Special Condition (h) — Alcohol Abstinence

Special Condition (h) requires abstinence from alcohol "[w]hile in treatment or taking psychotropic medication." The abstinence requirement should attach when a physician says it should, and not otherwise.

Nothing in this record ties alcohol to Mr. Bernecky's offense, to his history, or to any violation. His offense involved images on a device. The Second Circuit vacated a comparable blanket restriction for exactly that gap. *United States v. Betts*, 886 F.3d 198, 202-03 (2d Cir. 2018). It has since applied the same rule to a nearly identical "during treatment" abstinence clause. *United States v. Rodriguez*, No. 22-1820-cr (2d Cir. Dec. 7, 2023) (summary order).

The clause's own wording compounds the problem. Its trigger is disjunctive — "[w]hile in treatment *or* taking psychotropic medication" — so talk therapy alone requires abstinence even though no interaction risk can exist without a medication. And treating "psychotropic medication" as one uniform category ignores that alcohol-interaction risk varies sharply by drug class. This clause is this District's standard template, not a finding made about Mr. Bernecky.

**Relief requested (Part V):** an order amending Special Condition (h) so that the alcohol-abstinence requirement applies only upon, and only for as long as, a treating physician determines that alcohol is contraindicated for a specific medication or treatment Mr. Bernecky is actually receiving.

### VI. Standard Conditions 5 and 8 — Residence Approval and Contact with Persons Convicted of Felonies

Standard Condition 5 requires Mr. Bernecky to live at a place "approved by the probation officer." Standard Condition 8 forbids him to knowingly communicate or interact with anyone he knows has a felony conviction without the probation officer's advance permission. Each tells him permission is required. Neither says what earns it.

**The residence condition needs a stated standard.** Because nothing defines what satisfies "approved," an officer may condition approval on anything at all, and Mr. Bernecky has no way to show he has met a requirement nobody has stated. "A condition is too vague if it requires a reasonable person to speculate as to its meaning." *United States v. Reeves*, 591 F.3d 77, 80-81 (2d Cir. 2010). It is also a delegation problem: a court "may not delegate to the Probation Department decisionmaking authority which would make a defendant's liberty itself contingent on a probation officer's exercise of discretion." *Matta*, 777 F.3d at 122. The only articulated basis Probation has offered is that "the probation officer assesses whether the change is appropriate to meet the defendant's risk and needs and whether it poses any risk to the defendant or to others" — broad enough to justify any outcome and specific enough to guide none. U.S. Courts, *Overview of Probation and Supervised Release Conditions*, ch. 2, § V (July 2024). Probation knows how to write a real standard when it wants one: its own guidelines require a written plan, supervisory approval, and a defined reasonable-suspicion showing before a search of Mr. Bernecky's home. Guide to Judiciary Policy, Vol. 8E, Appx. 3A. Nothing comparable governs a decision that can put him out of that home.

**The felon-contact condition should require disclosure, not approval.** Mr. Bernecky is not aware of a dispute with his probation officer on this point, and he does not raise one. He raises the condition because he has no idea what it requires of him and because the answer should come from the Court rather than from an accumulation of guesses.

The conditions of his life since release make that concrete. He lives in an apartment complex with numerous neighbors who have felony convictions. He is required to attend a counseling and support group composed of people convicted of felonies. He works, shops, and rides public transit in a society in which roughly eight percent of the adult population has a felony conviction. Sarah K.S. Shannon et al., *The Growth, Scope, and Spatial Distribution of People With Felony Records in the United States, 1948-2010*, 54 Demography 1795 (2017). Read as written, Standard Condition 8 requires him to obtain advance permission before saying good morning in a hallway.

The condition's premise also does not describe this offense category or this defendant. Advance-approval gating of associations fits crimes committed by groups, in concert, or in conspiracy, where the association is itself the mechanism of the offense. Child-pornography possession is not that crime. It is committed alone, and it is strongly associated with social isolation — which describes Mr. Bernecky's own history and is a large part of how he came to be convicted twice. Certification ¶ __. The Court sent him to a Bureau of Prisons facility for roughly eight years, where every person around him had a felony conviction. He formed friendships there with people who encourage him toward a law-abiding life. Cutting a man with his history off from the people who help him stay out of trouble does not serve any purpose supervised release exists to serve; it recreates the condition that produced the offense.

Probation's legitimate interest is in knowing who Mr. Bernecky spends time with and being able to ask about it. Disclosure serves that interest completely. Advance approval adds nothing except a permission gate applied to a category of person he cannot avoid and should not have to.

**Relief requested (Part VI):** an order (1) directing that Mr. Bernecky be provided a written standard governing what will satisfy "approved" under Standard Condition 5, with leave to challenge the substance of any standard Probation proposes; and (2) amending Standard Condition 8 so that Mr. Bernecky must disclose to the probation officer any ongoing association with a person he knows to have a felony conviction, rather than obtain advance permission for it, with the probation officer retaining the ability to inquire about any such association.

### VII. Standard Condition 12 — Risk Notification

Standard Condition 12, as it appears in Mr. Bernecky's Judgment, provides that "[i]f the probation officer determines that you pose a risk to another person (including an organization), the probation officer may require you to notify the person about the risk and you must comply with that instruction." That is the exact condition the Second Circuit held invalid, agreeing that it "is vague and affords too much discretion to the probation officer" and vacating it as indistinguishable from an employer-notification condition already struck for leaving the obligation to "the probation officer's unfettered discretion." *United States v. Boles*, 914 F.3d 95, 111-12 (2d Cir. 2019) (quoting *Peterson*, 248 F.3d at 86).

Within two months of *Boles*, this District entered a standing order directing that this language be removed from every judgment imposing supervision and replaced with a version requiring the court itself to determine, based on "criminal record, personal history and characteristics, and the nature and circumstances of [the] offense," that a defendant poses a risk before any notification requirement attaches. *Amended Standing Order, In re United States v. Boles* (W.D.N.Y. Mar. 22, 2019) (Geraci, C.J.). That order directs amendment of "the Judgment and Commitment order in all criminal cases in which a term of probation or supervised release is imposed." Mr. Bernecky's Judgment was entered nine months earlier and was never conformed to it. At a pre-supervision meeting his probation officer presented him with this exact, superseded language and required him to sign an acknowledgment of it. Certification ¶ __. He asks the Court to apply to him the correction this District has already ordered for everyone else.

He objects separately to the structure the corrected condition creates. Under the standing order, a risk determination is made between the probation officer and the Court. Mr. Bernecky would have no notice of it, no opportunity to be heard on it, no ability to present evidence, and no ability to contest any assertion made about him — while the consequence is that he must go tell a third party that he is dangerous. As far as he knows, no such determination has been made as to him, and he does not ask the Court to resolve the question today. He raises it now so that it is not later treated as waived, and states his position plainly: where Probation believes a risk notification is warranted, it should proceed by petition and summons, on notice, rather than by internal determination — and because the proceeding would determine whether Mr. Bernecky must disclose himself as a danger to a named person, counsel should be appointed for any hearing on it.

**Relief requested (Part VII):** an order (1) amending Standard Condition 12 to match the language adopted by this District's March 22, 2019 standing order following *Boles*; and (2) providing that any risk determination under the amended condition be initiated by petition and summons, with notice to Mr. Bernecky, an opportunity to be heard, and appointment of counsel for any hearing.

## CONCLUSION

Every condition addressed above shares the same defect. Each was written broadly enough to cover a worst case, at a moment when nobody had to test its limits, and each is now being read as broadly as its words can be stretched by whoever happens to be applying it. Three of them have already been stretched that far — the employer dispute, the browser-less television locked down over YouTube, and the risk-notification language this District ordered removed seven years ago and a probation officer asked Mr. Bernecky to sign anyway. The rest have not yet, only because they have not yet had the occasion.

None of this requires the 2018 Judgment to have been wrong. It requires only that someone say what it means. Doing that now costs the government nothing it is entitled to. Not doing it means the answers get supplied one at a time, by people without the authority to supply them, and reviewed by this Court only after Mr. Bernecky has paid for the guess.

For the foregoing reasons, Mr. Bernecky respectfully requests that the Court:

1. **Special Condition (a) — employment.** Confirm that the condition does not apply to Mr. Bernecky's employment and imposes no obligation on any employer; direct the Probation Office to advise his employer in writing that it owes the Probation Office nothing under this Judgment; and confirm that the Judgment's silence on a subject creates no restriction on that subject and does not authorize Probation to supply one.

2. **Special Condition (a) — CIMP Participant Agreement.** Replace Item 3's device-authorization gate with a capability-based standard, coupled with Mr. Bernecky's consent to suspicion-free physical inspection of his own devices and a limit on device-management privileges; strike from Item 21 "by law enforcement," "or unlawful conduct by any person," and "or is about to occur"; narrow Item 16 to notice rather than prior authorization; strike Item 5 or construe it to reach only conduct undertaken to defeat monitoring; and construe Items 9 and 17 to reach only encryption Mr. Bernecky himself initiates to conceal illegal activity or defeat monitoring, adding Item 17's pre-approval mechanism to Item 9.

3. **Special Condition (a) — monitoring method.** Limit monitoring to the inspection of image and video files, prohibit the provider from disclosing anything else, direct the Probation Office to implement that limitation and report on the method selected, direct written notice to Mr. Bernecky of what is captured and who may see it, and establish a privilege protocol providing for designation, *in camera* review, and destruction of inadvertently captured privileged material. Mr. Bernecky requests an evidentiary hearing on technical feasibility if Probation contests it.

4. **Special Condition (a) — device scope.** Strike "or operated" and "or used," leaving "owned by the defendant," and define "internet capable device" by capability rather than product category, together with the possession provision described in Part I.D.

5. **Special Conditions (b) and (h) — treatment.** Establish the written-objection and clarification procedure set out in Part II.

6. **Special Conditions (d) and (i) — finances.** Limit Special Condition (d) to payee identity for payment-capable transactions; confirm that neither condition requires advance approval for retirement or investment activity; and vacate Special Condition (i) upon confirmation that restitution is paid in full.

9. **Standard Conditions 5 and 8.** Provide a written standard for residence approval, and amend Standard Condition 8 to require disclosure of associations with persons convicted of felonies rather than advance permission.

10. **Standard Condition 12.** Conform the condition to this District's March 22, 2019 standing order, and require any risk determination to proceed by petition and summons with notice, an opportunity to be heard, and appointed counsel.
