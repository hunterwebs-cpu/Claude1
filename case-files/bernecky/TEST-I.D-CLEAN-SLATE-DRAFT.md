**D. The Computer Monitoring Program Participant Agreement that Probation required Mr. Bernecky to sign at intake imposes obligations that no court has ever ordered, and several of its terms exceed what the Judgment's own special conditions actually authorize.**

Special Condition (a) of the Judgment tells Mr. Bernecky what he must do to use a computer or an internet-capable device: participate in the Computer and Internet Monitoring Program ("CIMP"), or obtain authorization from the Court or Probation. Judgment at 3B (Dkt. 28). It does not say that Probation may withhold or condition his ownership of a device on its own case-by-case say-so, and it does not authorize seizure of his property based on suspicion of somebody else's conduct. Yet at intake — on or about July 30, 2026, eight years after sentencing — Probation required Mr. Bernecky to sign a twenty-one item "Computer Monitoring Program Participant Agreement" that it drafted unilaterally, that no judge has ever reviewed, and that in several respects does exactly what Condition (a) does not authorize. Mr. Bernecky is complying with the Agreement in full. He nonetheless asks the Court to hold that its terms mean no more than what Condition (a) and Condition (f) actually delegate to Probation, and, where they mean more, that they may not be enforced against him as written.

The Agreement itself is transcribed in full from Mr. Bernecky's own uploaded photographs of the signed document. Its operative terms include the following, numbered as in the original:

> 2. I shall notify the U.S. Probation Office, in advance, of any computer(s), connected device(s), internet capable devices and/or gaming devices I own or have access to. . . .
>
> 3. I shall not use, own, or access any device which allows internet access other than what I have been authorized to use by the Probation Office. This includes, but is not limited to, internet appliances, mobile telephones, tablets, electronic games, gaming consoles, blu ray players, and SMART TV's.
>
> 5. While using my monitored device, I shall not use any form of "in-private" browsing at any time.
>
> 9. I understand that I will not use any encrypted email accounts.
>
> 16. I shall not purchase, download, possess and/or install any anti-virus, anti-spyware, firewall, or internet security application without advanced authorization by the U.S. Probation Office and my probation officer.
>
> 17. I shall not use any type of encryption unless pre-approved by my probation officer.
>
> 21. I shall permit the seizure and removal of hardware and data storage media for further analysis by law enforcement or the probation officer based upon reasonable suspicion that a violation of a condition of supervision or unlawful conduct by any person has or is about to occur.

Mr. Bernecky does not object to Item 2; he agrees it tracks what Condition (a) itself requires — advance notice of devices he uses. He objects to Items 3, 5, 9, 16, 17, and 21.

He owns two devices that qualify as "internet capable" under the Agreement's own terms: a Roku television and an Android cell phone on the US Mobile network (make and model to be confirmed). He purchased the Roku before his supervision began and reported it to Probation immediately, in the belief that a browser-less smart television fell outside the device-authorization requirement (the specific basis for that belief to be confirmed). He told his probation officer that the Roku does not support general web browsing. Probation's response was to place a parental lock on the device, which — on Mr. Bernecky's account — blocks him from installing any program or app on the television without separate clearance, while not preventing another device on the same home network from casting its own content to that same screen regardless of the lock. As to the phone, Mr. Bernecky did what Item 2 (which he does not dispute) and Condition (a) require: he notified his probation officer of the device and contacted the CIMP vendor, ICCP, to arrange installation of the required monitoring software (installation details to be confirmed). Since that installation, his phone's lock screen automatically displays the message "This device belongs to your organization." That message is generic Android system text, not something ICCP or Mr. Bernecky typed in; Google's own Android Enterprise developer documentation explains that a device-management application can run in one of two modes — "profile owner mode on personal devices or in device owner mode on fully managed devices" — and that device owner mode, the mode associated with that lock-screen designation, gives the controlling application "full control over the device." Android Developers, *Device control | Android Enterprise* (developer.android.com/work/dpc/device-management); Android Management API, *ManagementMode* reference (developers.google.com) ("DEVICE_OWNER: . . . Android Device Policy has full control over the device."). Independent industry reporting corroborates that the specific "belongs to your organization" phrasing is a default, non-configurable artifact of Android Enterprise's fully-managed-device (device owner) enrollment, rather than custom text entered by ICCP — though the undersigned has not located an official Google source confirming that particular wording is unconfigurable, and that narrower point should be treated as unverified pending further inquiry, distinct from the verified point that "device owner" status is Android's own designation for organization-owned hardware, not personal devices.

Both the special conditions the Judgment actually imposes and the CIMP Agreement Probation actually administers here trace to the same source. [[Judge Vilardo's 1998 — actually 2018 — decision on remand]] in *United States v. Browder* restated a W.D.N.Y. defendant's computer-monitoring condition "with greater specificity" after the Second Circuit directed him to do so, and the operative language he wrote is, word for word, Special Condition (a) of Mr. Bernecky's own Judgment: "The defendant shall not use or possess any computer, data storage device, or any internet capable device unless the defendant participates in the Computer and Internet Monitoring Program (CIMP), or unless authorized by the Court or the U.S. Probation Office. . . . As triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity, . . . the defendant shall consent to and cooperate with unannounced examinations of any computer equipment owned or used by the defendant. . . . Any such monitoring or examinations shall be designed to avoid, as much as possible, reading any privileged information or any private material that is not illegal, reasonably likely to lead to illegal material, or evidence related to illegal activity." *United States v. Browder*, No. 10-CR-263 (W.D.N.Y. Mar. 14, 2018) (Vilardo, J.), aff'g in relevant part 866 F.3d 504 (2d Cir. 2017). Judge Vilardo wrote that condition specifically to correct overreach in Probation's own agreement paperwork. He held that "Browder cannot be forced to sign or abide by agreements that reach beyond the Court's ordered conditions," id., and on that basis struck a clause in the CIMP-predecessor agreement that had purported to secure Browder's advance consent to "the unannounced search and seizure of [his] computer" even without "reasonable suspicion to believe that there has been a violation of either law or a condition of supervised release," id. (quoting Docket Item 175 at 4). He confined the reimposed condition's search-and-seizure authority to what is "triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity" and "based on reasonable suspicion" — precisely the language Condition (a) uses today.

The Second Circuit revisited the same program directly in *United States v. Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748 (2d Cir. 2023), construing this identical CIMP form — several of its numbered items match Mr. Bernecky's verbatim, including the antivirus, encryption, encrypted-email, IP-spoofing, and evidence-cleaning provisions. *Kunz* supplies three governing principles. The Second Circuit revisited the same program directly in *Kunz*, 68 F.4th 748 (2d Cir. 2023), construing this identical CIMP form. *Kunz* supplies three governing principles. Second, a CIMP provision that "exceeds the authority actually delegated to Probation by the district court . . . may not be enforced as written." Id. at 767–68. The court applied that holding to strike a paragraph in the very same form providing "I understand that I may be limited to the possession and use of one Internet-capable device," because nothing in the special condition's own text — which spoke only of notifying Probation of devices "that will be used" and permitting installation of monitoring software — delegated to Probation any authority to cap or otherwise control how many devices the defendant could own. Id. at 765–68. Third, the Sentencing Guidelines' own policy statement governing this class of condition, which *Kunz* quotes, limits reasonable-suspicion search authority to "unlawful conduct by the defendant" — not by any other person. Id. at 758–59 (quoting U.S.S.G. § 5D1.3(d)(7)).

Measured against that framework, Item 3 fails for the same reason the "one device" clause failed in *Kunz*, only more starkly. Where the version of the form construed in *Kunz* capped Probation's discretion at a fixed number of devices, the version now presented to Mr. Bernecky abandons any cap and instead conditions his ownership, use, or access to *any* internet-capable device on Probation's open-ended, standardless prior "authoriz[ation]." Nothing in Condition (a) delegates that authority. Condition (a)'s structure is participatory, not permissive: it tells Mr. Bernecky that CIMP participation (which he has undertaken) is what authorizes his devices — it does not say that each individual device further requires Probation's own advance sign-off before he may own or use it at all. Item 3 inverts that structure, and the Roku episode shows what that inversion means in practice: in response to a device Mr. Bernecky disclosed in advance and that — on his unrebutted account — does not support general web browsing, Probation did not simply monitor it; it functionally disabled it, blocking app installation altogether, while (again on his account) leaving the underlying casting vulnerability the lock was ostensibly meant to prevent unaddressed. That is a restriction on the device, not monitoring of it, and under *Kunz* a restriction of that character on a "nearly essential" category of technology is not a "minor detail" Probation may impose on its own authority; it is the kind of "significant . . . impos[ition]" on liberty that must be ordered by the Court itself, with particularized findings, if it is to be imposed at all. Id. at 765–67 (citing *United States v. Matta*, 777 F.3d 116, 122–23 (2d Cir. 2015)).

Item 21 exceeds both Condition (a) and Condition (f) in three independent respects. Condition (a) authorizes examination and seizure only "as triggered by impermissible/suspicious activity" detected through monitoring; Condition (f) separately authorizes search "based upon reasonable suspicion," but — consistent with the Guidelines policy statement *Kunz* quotes — of the defendant's own violation or unlawful conduct, not a third party's. *Kunz*, 68 F.4th at 758–59. Item 21 authorizes seizure on reasonable suspicion of a violation or "unlawful conduct by *any person*" that "has or is about to occur" — reaching prospective conduct by strangers, untethered to anything Mr. Bernecky's own monitored activity has shown — and it extends that authority to "law enforcement," an actor neither Condition (a) nor Condition (f) mentions. This is the same species of overreach Judge Vilardo confronted and rejected when Probation's earlier agreement tried to secure consent to seizure without tying it to suspicion of an actual violation; the remedy there was the same remedy available here — a holding that Mr. Bernecky "cannot be forced to sign or abide by agreements that reach beyond the Court's ordered conditions."

Items 9 and 17 should be construed the way *Kunz* construed the identical encryption provision in that form: to reach only encryption Mr. Bernecky himself affirmatively invokes to defeat monitoring, not the invisible, provider-side transport encryption built into ordinary email services. A literal reading of "I understand that I will not use any encrypted email accounts" or "I shall not use any type of encryption unless pre-approved" would place Mr. Bernecky in violation for using Gmail, Outlook, or any website whose address begins "https" — the exact reading *Kunz* rejected as neither compelled by the CIMP's own language nor sensible in light of its evident purpose. 68 F.4th at 763–64. To the extent Probation's Agreement is administered to reach that conduct, it exceeds what *Kunz* held the identical language permits.

Item 16's blanket bar on antivirus, anti-spyware, and firewall software without advance authorization stands on different footing: it is not a passive/active ambiguity of the kind *Kunz* resolved, but an affirmative prohibition that neither Condition (a) nor Condition (f) mentions at all. Whatever the outer bounds of "reasonably related" and "no greater deprivation of liberty than is reasonably necessary" under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(d)(2), a categorical prior-authorization requirement for ordinary defensive software cannot be reasonably related to a monitoring condition whose entire premise is a securely monitored device, since a device stripped of basic protection is more vulnerable to compromise by others, not less. See *Browder*, 866 F.3d at 510–12 (special conditions must be reasonably related to, and no more restrictive than reasonably necessary for, the purposes of monitoring).

Item 5's bar on "in-private" browsing "at any time" is, on its face, unclear in scope. Mr. Bernecky uses DuckDuckGo for ordinary browsing, which blocks third-party trackers but does nothing to conceal activity from device-level monitoring software. Consistent with *Kunz*'s method of construing ambiguous CIMP language according to its evident purpose rather than its broadest literal sweep, Item 5 should be read to reach browser features designed to defeat local monitoring — true private/incognito modes — and not privacy-respecting search tools that have no bearing on what the monitoring software installed on his device can see.

Mr. Bernecky asks the Court to hold that Items 3, 9, 16, 17, and 21 of the Agreement may not be enforced against him beyond what Special Conditions (a) and (f) actually authorize, and that Item 5 be construed, or clarified, to reach only browsing conduct that defeats device-level monitoring. To the extent Probation believes any of these restrictions should bind Mr. Bernecky more broadly than the Judgment currently provides, *Kunz* and *Browder* both make clear that the request belongs before this Court under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(2), supported by the particularized findings the statute requires — not before Probation alone, at intake, by signature.
