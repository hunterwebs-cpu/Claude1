## 1. Introduction

This memo tests a single client-originated theory, captured in full in `client-directives.md` at the entry titled *"SORA MEMORANDUM — client disputes the 'sexual contact' label for the 2005/2007 incidents and raises a new treatment-privilege theory to strike the statements entirely"* (2026-08-15): that Mr. Bernecky's 2017 disclosure of two 2005/2007 incidents, made during a polygraph examination administered as part of his sex-offender treatment program, is privileged under New York law; that any waiver he executed ran only to his federal supervising probation officer, not to a New York SORA proceeding; and that the resulting statements should be excluded from the 9/11/2026 hearing on privilege grounds, independent of whether they are also factually accurate.

**Scope and limitation stated up front:** this is, in the language of the client-directive entry itself, "a genuinely new legal theory for this matter" with no prior research track in this file. This memo is the first research pass on it. It does not revisit the separate, already-argued point that the "sexual contact" label is a factually inaccurate characterization of the underlying conduct (climbing on a lap/back during ordinary bus/after-school activity) — that is a factual-accuracy argument, addressed elsewhere in the main memorandum draft. This memo addresses only the privilege theory: whether the statements can be kept out regardless of accuracy.

**Bottom line, stated now and developed below:** the theory has real statutory footing — CPLR 4508 is a genuine, applicable New York privilege that plausibly covers Mr. Bernecky's treatment communications, and a structural statutory-construction argument (comparing SORA's Correction Law Article 6-C to the sibling Mental Hygiene Law Article 10 civil-management statute) supports the client's position better than anything else found in this research pass. But **no New York case has ever applied CPLR 4507, 4508, or any other statutory privilege to bar evidence at a SORA hearing, and no case addresses a treatment-program polygraph specifically.** This is close to unbriefed territory. The honest assessment is that the client has a serious, non-frivolous argument worth raising — not a settled winner. Several sub-issues (who exactly received the 2017 disclosure, whether general NY waiver doctrine would treat a disclosure to one recipient as a waiver as to all) cut against the client and are flagged as such below, not minimized.

## 2. Issues and Summary Conclusions

**Issue 1 — Which privilege, if any, fits Mr. Bernecky's actual treatment setting?**
*Summary conclusion:* CPLR 4508 (social worker privilege) is the correct fit, not CPLR 4507 (psychologist) or Mental Hygiene Law Article 33. Mr. Bernecky's confirmed treatment provider, per this matter's own primary-source fact-verification, is Emmanuel Petracca, LCSWR of Linden Oaks Sexual Abuse Treatment Services (LOSATS) — a licensed clinical social worker, not a psychologist. CPLR 4508 attaches to the clinician's individual license and does not depend on the treatment program's institutional licensure status, which resolves what would otherwise be an open MHL Article 33 question.

**Issue 2 — Does a treatment-program polygraph fall within that privilege's scope?**
*Summary conclusion:* Plausibly, but with a real structural gap: no New York statute or case addresses polygraph examinations in the treatment-privilege context at all. The privilege attaches to communications with the licensed social worker, not to a polygraph examination as such — meaning the strength of the argument may turn on facts not yet confirmed (who actually administered the exam, and to whom Mr. Bernecky's statements were made or reported).

**Issue 3 — Does a limited-purpose confidentiality waiver (treatment provider → federal probation officer) extend to a New York SORA proceeding?**
*Summary conclusion:* No New York authority squarely answers this. General New York privilege-waiver doctrine tends toward treating disclosure to any third party as a broad waiver, not a scope-limited one — this cuts against the client's position more than the client-directive entry's framing suggests. The strongest textual hook for the client's narrower reading is CPLR 4508(a)'s own language authorizing the social worker to disclose "as the client may authorize" (framed as an authorization to a recipient, not framed as a general forfeiture) — but no case interprets that clause the way the client's theory needs it interpreted.

**Issue 4 — Does SORA's reliable-hearsay standard (Correction Law § 168-n(3); *People v. Mingo*) override a properly invoked privilege, or does privilege operate independently?**
*Summary conclusion:* *Mingo* does not address privilege at all — it is a pure hearsay-reliability case. Reliable-hearsay and privilege are analytically independent doctrines that no court has yet been asked to reconcile in a SORA proceeding. The best available argument is a statutory-construction one: the Legislature knew how to expressly override privilege for a sex-offender proceeding (it did so, explicitly, in Mental Hygiene Law § 10.08(c) for Article 10 civil-management proceedings) and did not do so anywhere in Correction Law Article 6-C (SORA). That silence is a real, arguable point — not a decided one.

**Issue 5 — Has any SORA case addressed a Board Case Summary or PSR containing statements originally made in sex-offender treatment, on privilege or confidentiality grounds?**
*Summary conclusion:* None was located in this research pass. This appears to be a genuine gap in the reported SORA case law, not a resolved-against-the-client question.

**Issue 6 — If no on-point holding exists, what is the strongest analogous authority, and how far does it actually reach?**
*Summary conclusion:* The statutory-construction argument from MHL § 10.08(c)'s express "notwithstanding any other provision of law" override, contrasted with Article 6-C's silence, is the strongest available building block. It reaches only as far as showing the Legislature did not affirmatively abrogate privilege in SORA proceedings — it does not affirmatively establish that a court presented with this argument would honor the privilege, since no court has decided the question either way.

## 4. Governing Law

### A. CPLR 4508 — Social Worker Privilege

Confirmed directly against the official New York Senate legislative text portal (cross-checked against Justia and a NYS Unified Court System "Guide to New York Evidence" chapter summary, both independently converging on the same substance) *(Tier a, tool-mediated retrieval of primary-source text — see verification note below)*:

- CPLR 4508(a) provides that a licensed master social worker or licensed clinical social worker "shall not be required to disclose a communication made by a client, or his or her advice given thereon, in the course of his or her professional employment."
- Exceptions: the social worker "may disclose such information as the client may authorize"; the social worker need not keep confidential a communication revealing a contemplated crime or harmful act; where the client is a child under 16 and the information indicates the client was the victim of a crime, the social worker may be compelled to testify; and a client waives the privilege by suing the social worker over confidential communications.
- CPLR 4508(b): a client who authorizes disclosure *for the purpose of obtaining insurance benefits* is **not** deemed to have waived the privilege generally — a narrow, express, purpose-limited non-waiver carve-out.

### B. CPLR 4507 — Psychologist Privilege

Same sourcing/tiering as above. CPLR 4507 places confidential psychologist-client communications "on the same basis as those provided by law between attorney and client" — i.e., it is, if anything, a *broader* privilege than 4508's social-worker privilege (it borrows the attorney-client framework rather than listing its own narrower exception set). It carries the identical insurance-purpose non-waiver carve-out. **This is not the applicable statute here**, because Mr. Bernecky's confirmed treatment provider is a licensed clinical social worker, not a psychologist — see Section 5(A) below. It is addressed here only because the client-directive entry and the task both correctly flagged it as a candidate to be ruled in or out.

### C. Mental Hygiene Law § 33.13 — Clinical Records Confidentiality

Per a tool-mediated review of the current statutory text, MHL 33.13 protects the confidentiality of clinical records at a "facility" as defined in MHL § 1.03 — a definition tied to operation by, contract with, or funding/approval from the Office of Mental Health, OPWDD, or a local governmental unit's director of community services. *(Tier b-single — this was not independently cross-checked against a second source the way 4507/4508 were, and should be treated as provisional.)* **Whether LOSATS qualifies as a "facility" under this definition is an open factual question this memo cannot resolve** — private outpatient sex-offender treatment programs are not uniformly OMH/OASAS-licensed "facilities," and nothing in this matter's files establishes LOSATS's institutional status one way or the other. This is precisely why CPLR 4508 is the better-grounded theory: it attaches to the *individual clinician's license*, not to the treatment program's institutional status, and therefore does not require resolving this open question.

### D. *People v. Mingo*, 12 N.Y.3d 563 (2009)

Read directly from the Court of Appeals' own opinion text via Cornell's official New York Court of Appeals repository, cross-checked against independent secondary summaries that converged on identical language *(Tier a)*. *Mingo* holds that hearsay is admissible at a SORA hearing "if, based on the circumstances surrounding the development of the proof, a reasonable person would deem it trustworthy," and identifies case summaries, presentence reports, grand jury testimony, and sworn documents as presumptively reliable categories. **Confirmed: *Mingo* does not discuss psychologist-client privilege, social-worker privilege, or any statutory evidentiary privilege at all.** It is a pure hearsay-reliability decision.

### E. Correction Law § 168-n(3)

Tool-mediated retrieval of the current statutory text from the official Senate legislative portal, quoted fragments cross-checked across two independent fetches for consistency *(Tier a, same verification caveat as Section A above)*. The operative clauses: the People bear the burden of proving SORA determinations "by clear and convincing evidence"; the court "may consider reliable hearsay evidence submitted by either party, provided that it is relevant to the determinations"; materials "may be obtained by subpoena if not voluntarily provided"; and — notably — the court "shall seal any portion of the court file or record which contains material that is confidential under any state or federal statute." **This sealing clause is a double-edged finding and should be presented to the Writer as such, not overclaimed in the client's favor:** it shows the Legislature anticipated that material coming before a SORA court might be "confidential under any state or federal statute," which could be read either way — (i) favorably, as proof the Legislature was thinking about confidentiality/privilege interactions in this exact statute and chose a narrow remedy (record-sealing) rather than a broad one (admissibility override), leaving the antecedent admissibility question untouched; or (ii) unfavorably, as an implicit assumption that confidential material *will* be received and used at SORA hearings, with sealing as the sole and sufficient safeguard. Section 6(D) below develops why reading (i) is the better one, but candor requires flagging that reading (ii) is available to the People.

### F. Mental Hygiene Law § 10.08(c) — the comparator statute

Tool-mediated retrieval, cross-checked across two independent search passes that converged on identical quoted language *(Tier b-corr. — this was reconstructed from search-engine-summarized secondary sources describing the statute, not fetched directly from the full primary text the way 4507/4508/168-n were; the core quoted clause below should be independently re-verified against the codified text before it is relied on in a filing)*. MHL § 10.08(c), governing the separate Article 10 sex-offender civil-management proceeding (a different, later-stage proceeding from SORA, reserved for the highest-risk offenders subject to possible civil confinement), provides that "notwithstanding any other provision of law," the Commissioner, the case review panel, and the Attorney General are entitled to request from any state agency records "relating to the respondent's commission or alleged commission of a sex offense," including treatment records and "any medical, clinical or other information" relevant to the civil-management determination. This is an **explicit, textual privilege-override clause** for a sex-offender proceeding closely related to, but statutorily distinct from, SORA.

## 5. Discussion / Analysis

### A. Issue 1 — Which privilege fits Jeff's actual treatment setting?

**Rule:** CPLR 4508 covers communications to a licensed master social worker or licensed clinical social worker in the course of professional employment; CPLR 4507 covers communications to a psychologist; MHL 33.13 covers clinical records at an MHL-defined "facility."

**Application:** Mr. Bernecky's confirmed treatment provider — the only one this matter's files independently verify, Emmanuel Petracca, LCSWR — is a licensed clinical social worker. CPLR 4508 is therefore the correct statute to lead with, not CPLR 4507. This is a meaningful drafting point: CPLR 4507 borrows the full attorney-client framework and is, on its text, the stronger of the two privileges, but citing it here would be citing the wrong statute for this provider's actual license type — a credibility risk if the government or the court checks it, exactly the kind of overclaiming this matter's own research discipline exists to prevent. CPLR 4508 is narrower on its face (it lists its own specific exceptions rather than borrowing attorney-client law wholesale) but it is still a real, substantial privilege, and it is the one that actually fits the confirmed facts.

MHL 33.13 is not needed to make this argument, and reaching for it risks an unresolved institutional-licensure fight (is LOSATS an MHL "facility"?) that CPLR 4508 avoids entirely, since 4508 attaches to Petracca's personal license regardless of LOSATS's institutional status. **Recommendation: lead with CPLR 4508; do not rely on MHL 33.13 unless LOSATS's facility status is independently confirmed.**

**Conclusion:** CPLR 4508 is the best-fit privilege, assuming (subject to the open fact flagged in Section 3) that the 2017 disclosure in fact went to a licensed social worker in the treatment relationship.

### B. Issue 2 — Does a treatment-program polygraph fall within CPLR 4508's scope?

**Rule:** CPLR 4508 protects "a communication made by a client... in the course of his or her professional employment" — i.e., it protects the *communication*, tied to the professional relationship, not a particular *procedure* (like a polygraph) as such.

**Application:** No New York statute or case was located that addresses a polygraph examination specifically in the CPLR 4507/4508 privilege context — this is a genuine gap, not a resolved point either way. The client's underlying theory does not actually need "polygraph" itself to be a privileged category; it needs the *disclosure Mr. Bernecky made* — describing the 2005/2007 incidents — to have been made "in the course of" his professional relationship with his treatment provider. On the facts as currently understood (a disclosure made as part of an ongoing sex-offender treatment program, in a "sexual-history polygraph" format that this matter's own separate Fifth Amendment research track already confirms is the standard PCSOT model used in treatment programs of this kind), that framing is plausible.

The real vulnerability is the unconfirmed fact flagged in Section 3: in the standard post-conviction sex-offender treatment (PCSOT) polygraph model, the exam is often physically administered by a separately credentialed polygraph examiner, distinct from the treating clinician, who then reports results back to the treatment team. No New York statute creates an independent evidentiary privilege for polygraph examiners as such. If the 2017 exam was administered that way, the strength of a CPLR 4508 argument may depend on treating the examiner as an agent or necessary intermediary of the treatment relationship — an extension that has support by analogy in New York physician-patient privilege case law (technicians and support staff assisting a physician are generally treated as within the privilege), but this memo did not locate any case extending that agent theory specifically to a social-worker privilege plus polygraph-examiner fact pattern. This should be argued, if at all, candidly as an extension by analogy, not as an established rule.

**Conclusion:** Plausible, not certain. The strength of this specific sub-argument turns on facts (who administered the exam, and the reporting relationship between the examiner and the treating LCSW) that are not yet confirmed in this matter's files.

### C. Issue 3 — Waiver scope

**Rule:** CPLR 4508(b) creates one, narrow, express non-waiver carve-out: disclosure for the purpose of obtaining insurance benefits does not waive the privilege generally. Outside that carve-out, general New York privilege-waiver doctrine (developed mainly in the attorney-client and physician-patient contexts, and applied by analogy to the statutory therapist privileges) treats voluntary disclosure to a third party outside the privileged relationship as a waiver — New York has historically not recognized a general-purpose "limited waiver" doctrine of the kind Federal Rule of Evidence 502(c) supplies for federal privilege law. A 2023–2025 legislative effort to add a New York limited-waiver provision (proposed CPLR 4550, addressing *inadvertent* disclosure specifically) remains, per the sourcing available in this pass, in the bill/report stage rather than confirmed as enacted law, and in any event is drafted around *inadvertent* disclosure, not the deliberate, purpose-specific disclosure at issue here.

**Application:** This is the weakest link in the client's theory as currently framed, and should be presented to the client candidly, not softened. The client-directive entry's framing — that a waiver "for the purpose of federal probation-officer access... does not extend beyond that specific purpose" — describes how the client believes New York privilege law *should* work, and is consistent with general legal intuitions about purpose-limited consents, but this research pass did not find New York statutory or case authority holding that a CPLR 4507/4508 waiver executed for one recipient (a supervising officer) stays confined to that recipient and does not open the communication to use in a later, different proceeding (a state SORA hearing). CPLR 4508(b)'s own structure cuts a specific way here: the Legislature, when it wanted to create a purpose-limited non-waiver rule, wrote one — narrowly, for insurance disclosures only. Under ordinary expressio unius reasoning, the presence of that one express carve-out is at least some evidence the Legislature did not intend other purpose-specific disclosures (like a supervision-compliance disclosure) to enjoy the same protection by implication.

The client's best textual foothold is CPLR 4508(a)'s own wording that the social worker "may disclose such information as the client may authorize" — phrased as authorizing a disclosure to someone, not as a blanket relinquishment of confidentiality generally. A court could plausibly read an "authorization" to disclose to a federal supervising officer as exactly that: authorization running to that officer, not a general public forfeiture. But no case was located adopting that reading, and it should be presented as an argument from the statute's own text and structure, not as settled law.

**Conclusion:** A genuinely open, arguable point, not a favorable one. The strongest honest framing: New York has no established doctrine either confirming or rejecting a purpose-limited reading of a CPLR 4508 waiver, and the statute's own text (the "authorize" language, read against the narrow express insurance carve-out) supports an argument for a limited reading — but this is advocacy built on statutory structure and the absence of contrary authority, not on a case that has already decided the point.

### D. Issue 4 — Does the *Mingo* reliable-hearsay standard override privilege, or does privilege operate independently?

**Rule:** *Mingo* sets the hearsay-admissibility standard for SORA proceedings. Privilege is a distinct evidentiary doctrine that operates as a bar to the *use* of otherwise-relevant, even otherwise-reliable, evidence — reliability and privilege answer different questions (reliable-hearsay asks "can we trust this information," privilege asks "should this information be kept out regardless of how trustworthy it is").

**Application:** Because *Mingo* never mentions privilege, it cannot fairly be read as having decided that reliable hearsay defeats privilege — the two issues were never presented together in that case. The stronger analytical move, and the one this memo recommends, is the comparative-statute argument built from Section 4(E)–(F): the Legislature, drafting a companion sex-offender statute (MHL Article 10, the civil-management track) in the same general legislative family as SORA, included an explicit privilege-overriding clause — "notwithstanding any other provision of law" — when it wanted confidential/privileged treatment material to be usable in that proceeding. Correction Law Article 6-C contains no comparable clause anywhere in §§ 168-a through 168-o. Under ordinary statutory-construction principles (the same expressio unius/surplusage-canon reasoning this matter's own SORA memorandum draft already deploys elsewhere, e.g. on the recidivism/public-safety distinction), that contrast supports reading SORA's silence as a decision *not* to override privilege — meaning a court applying *Mingo*'s reliable-hearsay standard to material that happens to also be privileged would still need to separately clear the privilege hurdle; reliable-hearsay status alone would not do that work.

This argument should be presented candidly for what it is: an original synthesis built from statutory comparison, not a holding any court has issued. The § 168-n(3) sealing clause (Section 4(E)) is the main complication and should be disclosed to the Writer, not buried — a court could instead read that clause as evidence the Legislature *did* contemplate confidential material coming in at SORA hearings (with sealing as the only safeguard it thought necessary), which would cut against this argument. The better response to that reading is that "confidential" and "privileged" are not the same thing under New York law — sealing protects material from public disclosure; it says nothing about whether a party can be compelled, over a valid privilege objection, to have the material admitted as evidence in the first place. That distinction is doctrinally sound but, again, no SORA case has drawn it.

**Conclusion:** *Mingo* and reliable-hearsay do not, on their own terms, resolve the privilege question either way. The comparative-statute argument is the strongest tool available, but it is a first-impression argument, not existing law.

### E. Issue 5 — Has any NY SORA case addressed treatment-originated statements on privilege grounds?

**Application:** No case was located, in this research pass, applying CPLR 4507, CPLR 4508, or any comparable statutory privilege to Board Case Summary or PSR content originating in sex-offender treatment — either to admit such content over a privilege objection or to exclude/limit it on privilege grounds. This should be reported to the client and the Writer exactly as found: as an honest gap, not as silent support for either side. It is also worth noting, candidly, that the *volume* of SORA litigation in New York is large and this was not an exhaustive search of every reported and unreported SORA decision — the recommendation in Section 7 below is to run a targeted Westlaw/Lexis search (see Section 8) before representing to the court that no such case exists, since a negative claimed on a WebSearch-based pass alone is inherently less reliable than one built on a full database search.

### F. Issue 6 — Strongest analogous authority, and its actual reach

The MHL § 10.08(c)/Article 6-C silence comparison (Section 5(D)) is the strongest analogous authority this pass located. Its actual reach: it supports an argument that the Legislature did not intend to override privilege in SORA proceedings the way it did for Article 10. It does **not** reach, and should not be represented as reaching, any of the following: (1) a holding that CPLR 4508 privilege in fact covers a treatment-program polygraph disclosure specifically (Issue 2, above, remains open); (2) a holding on the waiver-scope question (Issue 3, above, remains open and is the weakest link); or (3) any actual court's application of privilege doctrine to exclude evidence at a SORA hearing (no such case exists in this pass's findings). The gap between what this authority shows and what the client's theory ultimately needs — an actual order striking the 2017 disclosure from the record on privilege grounds — is real and should not be minimized in how this is presented to the client or drafted into the memorandum.

## 6. Conclusion

On the current record, the client has a genuine, arguable, first-impression privilege theory — not a frivolous one, and not a settled winner either. CPLR 4508 is the right statute to invoke, given the confirmed LCSWR treatment provider. The comparative-statute argument (MHL § 10.08(c)'s express override vs. Correction Law Article 6-C's silence) is a real, independently-constructed argument that has not been tested in any reported decision. The weakest link is the waiver-scope question (Issue 3): New York's general disposition toward third-party disclosure as a broad waiver, combined with the absence of any case recognizing a purpose-limited reading of a CPLR 4508 waiver outside the statute's own narrow insurance carve-out, means the People have a live, non-frivolous response available even if the court accepts that CPLR 4508 privilege attached in the first place. My honest read: this argument is worth raising, and worth developing further on the facts (Section 7), but should be presented to the court and to the client as a novel argument built from statutory structure, not as an argument any prior SORA decision compels.
