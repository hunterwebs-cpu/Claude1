STATEMENT OF THE ISSUES

I. Whether Kumar's Fifth Amendment claims — vindictive prosecution
and preindictment delay — survive his unconditional guilty plea under
Class v. United States, 583 U.S. 174 (2018), or alternatively whether
counsel's constitutionally deficient performance provides cause and
prejudice to excuse procedural default under Murray v. Carrier, 477

USS. 478 (1986).

II. Whether the government violated Kumar's Fifth Amendment due
process rights by deliberately sequencing his prosecution — holding
Massachusetts charges in reserve while Kumar served his Rhode Island
sentence, with complete investigative knowledge and a made charging
decision — for the specific purpose of manufacturing a criminal history
enhancement, foreclosing concurrent sentencing, and intercepting

Kumar immediately upon his completion of that sentence.

----- PDF PAGE 11 -----
Case: 26-1250 Document: 00118496192 Page:11 Date Filed: 08/24/2026 —_ Entry ID: 6836756

III. Whether counsel rendered constitutionally ineffective assistance by: (a)
failing to secure Massachusetts proffer protection before Kumar
participated in sessions with Massachusetts federal agents in Boston, in
the presence of the lead Massachusetts prosecutor; (b) advising Kumar
that his Guidelines exposure was 15 to 20 months when his actual range
was 87 to 108 months, inducing withdrawal of a pending motion to
dismiss; and (c) failing to make an explicit § 5K2.23 departure motion

at sentencing.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE

A. The Rhode Island Prosecution and the Proffer Sessions

On August 25, 2019, Manish Kumar was arrested at JFK Airport on a
criminal complaint filed in the District of Rhode Island alleging conspiracy
to commit wire fraud, wire fraud, and aggravated identity theft arising
from fraudulent call center operations and credit card fraud. United States
v. Kumar, No. 20-cr-00089-JJM-PAS (D.R.1.), RI-ECF 3, 4. No drug
charges were filed. The Rhode Island investigation was conducted by
federal agents based in Massachusetts. Dkt. No. 122-2 (Basil Aff.) § 2.

Following his arrest, and on advice of counsel, Kumar agreed to
cooperate with a separate Massachusetts-based investigation into money
laundering and drug trafficking. Dkt. No. 122 at 2. Over the course of
eleven proffer sessions conducted in the District of Massachusetts, from
the day of his arrest through February 27, 2020, Kumar provided
information to federal investigators and authorized access to his personal
electronic devices and accounts. Dkt. No. 130 at 3 (citing PSR {J 10, 40).
Counsel was present at every session. Jd.

Kumar had a proffer agreement with the District of Rhode Island.
Dkt. No. 122 at 2-3 (citing PSR { 42). He had no proffer agreement with
the District of Massachusetts. Ja; Rule 11 Hr'g Tr., Dkt. No. 91, at 6-7.

The Rhode Island proffer agreement permitted derivative use of Kumar's

C. The Rhode Island Conviction and Its Sentencing Consequences

On October 21, 2020, the Rhode Island U.S. Attorney filed an Information
charging Kumar with seven counts of wire fraud, conspiracy, and
aggravated identity theft. RI-ECF 14. Kumar waived indictment and
pleaded guilty unconditionally on November 5, 2020. RI-ECF 16, 17. On
February 12, 2021, the Rhode Island district court sentenced Kumar to 24
months of imprisonment. RI-ECF 27. Kumar was a non-citizen subject to
deportation upon completion of his sentence — a fact known to everyone
in the courtroom on February 12, 2021.

The Rhode Island conviction carried three criminal history points
under U.S.S.G. § 4A1.1(a), placing Kumar in Criminal History Category II
for purposes of any future federal sentencing. Dkt. No. 130 at 6 (citing
PSR {| 67-69). Had the Massachusetts conduct been charged
simultaneously with or prior to the Rhode Island conviction, Kumar would
have had no criminal history and would have been sentenced as a CHC I
defendant. Dkt. No. 96 (Def.'s Sent. Mem.) at 7. The difference between
CHC I and CHC II at offense level 27 is a Guidelines floor of 70 months
versus 87 months — a 17-month differential built mechanically into the

Guidelines table.

On May 12, 2021 — the same day AUSA Basil learned Kumar had
been released from BOP custody — the government filed a criminal

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

Manish Kumar is serving 87 months in federal prison for drug trafficking
offenses prosecuted in Massachusetts. The government had complete
knowledge of the Massachusetts conduct from the day it began — August
2019 — when Kumar participated in proffer sessions with Massachusetts
FBI agents in Boston. It did not charge him. It waited while Kumar was
convicted and sentenced in Rhode Island. It waited while Kumar served
every day of that sentence. It waited while Kumar was released to ICE
custody on May 7, 2021 — his birthday — and began deportation
proceedings. Then, on May 12, 2021, the same day AUSA Basil learned
Kumar had been released from federal prison, the government filed a
complaint in the District of Massachusetts. Kumar was arrested out of ICE
custody on May 13 — six days after completing his Rhode Island
sentence.

The government's own prosecutor explained the timing under oath.
AUSA Basil stated that he had already decided to charge Kumar and ‘had
intended to charge him with a co-conspirator later in the year,' but moved
when he did to arrest Kumar ‘before he was removed from the United
States by ICE.’ Dkt. No. 122-2 § 8. The government had known since
February 12, 2021 — the date of Kumar's Rhode Island sentencing — that
Kumar would be deported upon completing that sentence. Nothing about

the ICE consequence was new on May 12. Nothing about the investigation

13

----- PDF PAGE 21 -----

changed between February and May. The case was ready to charge. The
charging decision had been made. The only thing that changed on May 12
was Kumar's custodial status.

What the government's timing strategy accomplished is documented
in the record with mathematical precision. Kumar's Rhode Island
conviction produced three criminal history points, placing him in Criminal
History Category I. Had he been charged in Massachusetts before the
Rhode Island conviction was final — as he could have been — he would
have been sentenced as a CHC I defendant with no prior criminal history.
The difference is a Guidelines floor of 70 months versus 87 months. The
sentencing court itself confirmed the consequence: it stated that until
Kumar's allocution, it had ‘considered imposing a sentence of 72 months.’
Sent. Hr'g Tr., Dkt. No. 111, at 50. Under Glover v. United States, 531
U.S. 198, 203 (2001), any non-trivial increment of additional incarceration
is constitutionally cognizable prejudice as a matter of law.

This appeal presents three grounds for reversal, each independently
sufficient.

First — the threshold question. The district court held Kumar's
Fifth Amendment claims waived. That ruling was error on three
independent grounds. Class v. United States, 583 U.S. 174 (2018), directly
preserves Kumar's vindictive prosecution claim — the Court identified

Blackledge v. Perry, 417 U.S. 21 (1974), as the paradigm case surviving an

14

----- PDF PAGE 22 -----
Case: 26-1250 Document: 00118496192 Page:22 Date Filed: 08/24/2026 _—_ Entry ID: 6836756

unconditional plea, and vindictive prosecution is that paradigm. Class also
provides the principled framework for preserving Kumar's preindictment
delay claim, which — unlike conventional investigative-delay claims —
challenges the government's authority to exercise its prosecutorial power
in a manner deliberately designed to manufacture punishment.
Independently, counsel's constitutionally deficient advice — a Guidelines
estimate of 15 to 20 months against an actual range of 87 to 108 months —
provides cause and prejudice under Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478
(1986), to excuse any procedural default. And the withdrawal of the
motion to dismiss, induced by that five-fold underestimate, cannot
constitute the knowing and intelligent relinquishment Johnson v. Zerbst,
304 USS. 458 (1938), requires.

Second — the constitutional merits. The government's deliberate
prosecution sequencing violated Kumar's Fifth Amendment right to due
process through two doctrinal expressions of the same constitutional
violation. As preindictment delay: the government had complete
investigative knowledge, had made the charging decision, and waited —
not to investigate, but to ensure the Rhode Island conviction would accrue
as criminal history and the window for concurrent sentencing would close.
Basil's own affidavit eliminates the Lovasco safe harbor. As vindictive
prosecution: the complaint was filed the day Basil learned Kumar had been

released, six days after Kumar completed his sentence and was transferred

to ICE. The government's sequencing strategy structurally disabled the
sentencing court's ability to impose a sentence sufficient but not greater
than necessary, by splitting what should have been a single consolidated
prosecution into two proceedings no single court could evaluate as a
whole. Kumar was arrested on August 27, 2019. His projected release date
is August 26, 2027 — exactly eight years of continuous federal custody for
a defendant with no prior criminal history, stemming from a single arrest.
The 24 months served in Rhode Island and the 87 months being served in
Massachusetts total 102 months of de facto consecutive federal custody.
According to the United States Sentencing Commission's Judiciary
Sentencing Information for fiscal years 2021-2025, defendants sentenced
under § 2B1.1 at offense level 27 in Criminal History Category I — where
Kumar would have been in a consolidated prosecution — received a
median sentence of 48 months. Kumar is serving 102 months: more than
double the median. Even in his artificially inflated CHC II cell, the median
is 66 months. Kumar's Massachusetts sentence alone exceeds that by 21
months — and he received no departure or variance. Compare United
States v. Ihenacho, 716 F.3d 266, 269 (1st Cir. 2013) (comparable conduct
prosecuted in single indictment; defendant sentenced to 63 months).

Third — ineffective assistance of counsel. Counsel failed Kumar
at every stage at which he could have been protected. He allowed Kumar

to participate in eleven proffer sessions — conducted in Boston, with

16

Massachusetts FBI agents, culminating in a session personally attended by
the lead Massachusetts prosecutor — without securing Massachusetts
proffer protection. Those sessions provided the evidentiary foundation for
the prosecution. He estimated a Guidelines range five times below the
actual range, inducing Kumar to abandon a motion to dismiss he had
fought for through two attorneys and a Faretta hearing. And at sentencing,

_ he argued for a below-Guidelines sentence to account for the sequential
prosecution consequences without providing the Guidelines vehicle that
would have allowed the court to get there: a § 5K2.23 departure motion

A. Class v. United States Directly Preserves the Vindictive Prosecution
Claim and Provides the Principled Framework for Extending

Preservation to the Preindictment Delay Claim.

1. What Class Actually Held — and What It Did Not.

Class v. United States, 583 U.S. 174 (2018), held that an unconditional
guilty plea does not bar a defendant from raising claims that challenge ‘the
Government's power to criminalize [the defendant's] admitted conduct' and
thereby 'call into question the Government's power to "constitutionally
prosecute him." Jd. at 185 (quoting United States v. Broce, 488 U.S. 563,
575 (1989)). The Court distinguished such claims from ‘antecedent
constitutional violations’ waived under Tollett v. Henderson, 411 U.S. 258,
266-67 (1973) — trial-process errors that do not go to the government's
authority to prosecute. Class, 583 U.S. at 183.

Kumar acknowledges what Class involved: a facial challenge to the
constitutionality of the statute of conviction. The statutes under which
Kumar was convicted are unquestionably valid. He does not challenge
them. But Class's holding was not limited to facial statutory challenges.
The Court rooted its analysis in the Menna-Blackledge line and
specifically identified Blackledge v. Perry, 417 U.S. 21 (1974), as the
paradigm of a surviving claim. Class, 583 U.S. at 181-82. Blackledge did
not involve a facial statutory challenge. It involved a claim that the

government's conduct in bringing charges — escalating from misdemeanor

19

----- PDF PAGE 27 -----
Case: 26-1250 Document: 00118496192 Page:27 Date Filed: 08/24/2026 —_ Entry ID: 6836756

to felony after Perry exercised. his right to appeal — constitutionally
forfeited its authority to prosecute him at all. Blackledge, 417 U.S. at
28-30. The operative test from Class is whether the claim goes to 'the very
power of the State to prosecute.' 583 U.S. at 181 (quoting Blackledge, 417
USS. at 30).

2. Vindictive Prosecution: Direct Class Application, Not

Extension.

Kumar's vindictive prosecution claim is not an extension of Class. It is its
direct application. Class identified Blackledge — a vindictive prosecution
case — as the paradigm surviving claim precisely because vindictive
prosecution goes to the government's authority to prosecute, not to the
faimess of the trial process. A defendant who establishes vindictive
prosecution establishes that the government brought charges as a penalty
for his exercise of a constitutional right, thereby forfeiting any legitimate
authority to prosecute him at all. Blackledge, 417 U.S. at 28-29.

The Supreme Court confirmed this in Wolfe v. Virginia, 139 S. Ct.
790 (2019), vacating a state court's refusal to reach a Blackledge vindictive
prosecution claim notwithstanding the defendant's guilty plea and
remanding for reconsideration in light of Class. Even before Class, courts
recognized vindictive prosecution as categorically different from ordinary
pre-plea constitutional violations. See United States v. De Vaughn, 694
F.3d 1141, 1145-46 (10th Cir. 2012) (pre-Class, recognizing that a ‘narrow

exception exists’ for 'due process claims for vindictive prosecution’ that
survive a guilty plea under Blackledge). The Fifth Circuit in United States
v. Riojas, No. 24-40378 (Sth Cir. 2025), listed vindictive prosecution
among recognized Class survivors.

This Court has not directly addressed the question, but its MDLEA
decisions supply the answer. In United States v. Davila-Reyes, 84 F Ath
400, 415-16 (1st Cir. 2023) (en banc), the court presumed that
constitutional: challenges to the government's prosecutorial authority
survived unconditional pleas under Class. The principle stated there —
that claims calling into question the government's ‘power to
"constitutionally prosecute" the defendant’ reach the merits — compels the
same result here. Vindictive prosecution is the Class paradigm case. This

Court should so hold.

3. Preindictment Delay: A Principled Extension This Court
Should Adopt.

Kumar's preindictment delay claim presents a harder question and he
acknowledges it candidly. Most preindictment delay claims turn on
evidentiary prejudice: lost witnesses, faded memories, destroyed evidence.
Such claims are analytically closer to ‘antecedent constitutional violations'
under Tollett, because they go to the fairness of the trial process rather than
to the government's authority to prosecute. Even De Vaughn described the

Blackledge/Menna exception as ‘narrow.' 694 F.3d at 1145-46.

Kumar nonetheless submits that this Court should extend Class to

Framed that way, the claim is structurally indistinguishable from
Blackledge vindictiveness. In Blackledge, the government's misconduct

government's misconduct was deliberately timing charges to engineer a

The district court held that Kumar waived his constitutional claims by

Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), requires that waiver of
a constitutional right be 'an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a

presumption against waiver of fundamental constitutional rights.’ Jd.

facts and circumstances surrounding that case.' Jd.

Concepcion-Guliam does not resolve this question. That case held

C. Preindictment Delay.

1. The Governing Standard.

The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause protects against oppressive
preindictment delay. United States v. Marion, 404 U.S. 307, 324 (1971);
United States v. Lovasco, 431 U.S. 783, 789 (1977). In the First Circuit, a
defendant must establish substantial actual prejudice and intentional delay
to gain a tactical advantage. United States v. Irizarry-Col6én, 848 F.3d 61,
70-71 (1st Cir. 2017). Lovasco established that delay for legitimate —
investigative purposes does not violate due process even if prejudice
results. 431 U.S. at 795-96. But Lovasco expressly reserved the case of
delay 'solely to gain tactical advantage over the accused’ —— the precise
scenario Marion identified as the constitutional violation. Lovasco, 431
US. at 795 (quoting Marion, 404 U.S. at 324). This case presents that

reserved scenario in its purest form.

2. The Government's Delay Was Not Investigative — The Basil
Affidavit Proves It.

The government's standard defense to a preindictment delay claim is the

Lovasco safe harbor: it was still investigating. That defense is unavailable

here because the government's own prosecutor eliminated it under oath.
AUSA Basil stated in his affidavit: ‘Although I had intended to

that single sentence defeat the Lovasco safe harbor entirely.

First: Basil had already decided to charge Kumar. ‘Intended to
charge’ is not the language of an ongoing investigation — it is the language
of a made decision awaiting execution. Lovasco protects delay to complete
investigation. It does not protect delay after the charging decision is made.

Second: The timing was entirely within Basil's control. He planned

investigative necessity. Lovasco explicitly distinguishes between these.
431 USS. at 795 0.17.

investigation did not suddenly conclude on May 12. Nothing changed

4. The Lovasco Safe Harbor Is Unavailable.

Lovasco established that delay for legitimate investigative purposes does
not violate due process even if prejudice results. 431 U.S. at 795-96. The
government will invoke this safe harbor. It is unavailable on this record for
three reasons, all established by the government's own submissions.

The charging decision was already made. Basil's affidavit states he
‘had intended to charge him with a co-conspirator later in the year.’ Dkt.
No. 122-2 9 8. A prosecutor who has decided to charge and is planning the
timing is not investigating. Lovasco protects delay to complete
investigation of the defendant's conduct. It does not protect delay after the
charging decision is made.

The affidavit internally contradicts itself. Paragraph 5 asserts
ongoing COVID-impaired investigation through spring 2021. Paragraph 8
reveals Basil had decided to charge Kumar and was planning timing
around co-conspirator coordination. An investigation genuinely still
underway does not produce a filing schedule built around co-conspirator
timing. The two paragraphs cannot both be true.

The government had complete prosecutorial knowledge from the
proffer sessions. Dkt. No. 130 at 3 (citing PSR ff 10, 40). Lovasco
expressly reserved the case of a government that ‘had complete knowledge’
and delayed 'solely to gain tactical advantage.’ 431 U.S. at 795 (quoting
Marion, 404 U.S. at 324). That is this case.

D. Vindictive Prosecution. :

i. The Governing Standard.

The Due Process Clause prohibits the government from bringing charges
against a defendant in retaliation. for the exercise of a legal right.
Blackledge v. Perry, 417 U.S. 21, 28-29 (1974). Where the circumstances
give rise to a ‘realistic likelihood of vindictiveness,’ a presumption arises
that the government must rebut with legitimate, non-vindictive
justification. United States v. Goodwin, 457 U.S. 368, 373 (1982). The
presumption is strongest in post-conviction settings where the defendant
has exercised a right that threatens to undo the government's prior

litigation position. Blackledge, 417 U.S. at 27-28.

2. The Sequence Establishes a Realistic Likelihood of

Vindictiveness.

Kumar served his full Rhode Island sentence. His release was the
mandatory consequence of completing the sentence he was lawfully
imposed. Upon completing that sentence he was transferred to ICE
custody for deportation. The government had known since February 12,
2021 — the date of the Rhode Island sentencing — that this is exactly
what would happen. The same day Basil learned Kumar had been released
from BOP custody, he filed the Massachusetts complaint. Dkt. No. 122-2
8.

42

----- PDF PAGE 50 -----
Case: 26-1250 Document: 00118496192 Page:50 Date Filed: 08/24/2026 _—_ Entry ID: 6836756

The government will argue this reflects administrative necessity —
preventing deportation. That argument fails on its own terms. The
government knew since February 12, 2021 that Kumar would be subject to
deportation upon release. If preventing deportation was the legitimate
reason for filing, it had three and a half months to file before Kumar
completed his sentence. It chose not to. The ‘prevent deportation’
justification explains why the government filed at all — not why it filed at
that precise moment.

The Blackledge presumption does not require proof of subjective
animus. It requires circumstances that give rise to a realistic likelihood that
the prosecution was retaliatory. Goodwin, 457 U.S. at 373. A prosecution
filed the same day a defendant completes his sentence and is transferred to
immigration custody — by a prosecutor who had already decided to charge
him, knew the deportation consequence since sentencing, and chose to

wait until that precise moment — presents exactly that realistic likelihood.

3. The Barrios Overlap Confirms the Vindictive Pattern.

The Massachusetts prosecution did not arise in a vacuum. It arose from the
same investigative matrix — the same Massachusetts-based FBI agents,
the same Boston proffer sessions —- that produced the Rhode Island case.
Dkt. No. 122-2 {§ 2-3; Dkt. No. 123 (Kumar Reply). The government's
decision to bring Massachusetts charges only after Kumar completed his
Rhode Island sentence was not the product of independent prosecutorial
judgment by a separate office. It was a continuation of a’single federal
investigation by a single team that had been monitoring Kumar's status

‘no one from the U.S. Attorney's Office in Rhode Island asked me to take

is not Kumar's argument. Kumar's argument is about Basil's own
knowledge and his own timing decisions, all established by paragraphs 6

and 8 of his own affidavit without any need to prove inter-office direction.

4. The District Court's Analysis Was Legally Deficient.

The district court rejected the vindictive prosecution claim on two
grounds: waiver and the absence of evidence that the government harbored
animus toward Kumar. Dkt. No. 130 at 16-17. The waiver holding. is
addressed in Argument I. On the merits, the district court's animus analysis
misapplied the governing standard.

Blackledge does not require proof of subjective animus. It requires a
realistic likelihood of vindictiveness arising from the circumstances of the
prosecution. Goodwin, 457 U.S. at 373. The district court demanded that
Kumar prove 'the government prosecutor harbored any animus toward
him.' Dkt. No. 130 at 17. That is the wrong standard. The Blackledge
presumption exists precisely because subjective animus is rarely provable
— the doctrine operates on objective circumstances, not psychological
states. By demanding proof of subjective animus the district court
effectively nullified the Blackledge presumption entirely. That legal error

is reviewed de novo and requires reversal.

Ii. COUNSEL RENDERED CONSTITUTIONALLY
INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE AT MULTIPLE CRITICAL

A. The Governing Standard.

To establish ineffective assistance of counsel, Kumar must show that
counsel's performance fell below an objective standard of reasonableness
under prevailing professional norms, and that but for the deficient
performance there is a reasonable probability the result would have been
different. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687-88 (1984). In the
guilty plea context, prejudice requires showing a reasonable probability
that but for counsel's errors Kumar would not have pleaded guilty and
would have insisted on going to trial. Hill v. Lockhart, 474 U.S. 52, 59
(1985). At sentencing, Glover v. United States, 531 U.S. 198, 203 (2001),
establishes that any increase in actual jail time constitutes cognizable
Strickland prejudice — ‘any amount of actual jail time has Sixth
Amendment significance.’

This Court reviews the district court's legal conclusions on IAC de
novo and its factual findings for clear error. Damon, 732 F.3d at 3. Each of
the three grounds below independently satisfies both Strickland prongs and
independently satisfies the Carrier cause-and-prejudice standard for

excusing the procedural default of the Fifth Amendment claims in

Argument II. Under Lynch v. Ficco, 438 F.3d 35, 49-50 (1st Cir. 2006),
the Strickland prejudice showing simultaneously satisfies Frady actual

B. Ground One: Counsel's Failure to Secure Massachusetts Proffer
Protection Was Constitutionally Deficient and Caused the

Prosecution.

1. Deficient Performance.

Kumar participated in eleven proffer sessions with federal investigators
between August 2019 and February 27, 2020. Dkt. No. 130 at 3 (citing
PSR {fj 10, 40). Counsel was present at every session. Kumar had a proffer
agreement with the District of Rhode Island. Dkt. No. 122 at 2-3 (citing
PSR { 42). He had no proffer agreement with the District of
Massachusetts. Rule 11 Hr'g Tr., Dkt. No. 91, at 6-7.

This was not a strategic choice — and it was not a subtle
professional judgment call. The Massachusetts jurisdictional exposure was
unmistakable from the first session and became impossible to ignore by the
last. The sessions occurred physically in Boston, in the District of
Massachusetts, with Massachusetts-based FBI agents. Dkt. No. 130 at 3-4;
Dkt. No. 122-2 43. AUSA Basil — the lead Massachusetts prosecutor who
would ultimately sign the complaint against Kumar — personally attended

the February 2020 session in Boston. Dkt. No. 122-2 § 3. A defense

attorney present in that room, watching his client provide information
about Massachusetts conduct to a Massachusetts AUSA and
Massachusetts agents, faced an unambiguous professional obligation
before his client said a single word: he was required to secure
Massachusetts proffer protection as a precondition to further cooperation,
or at minimum to raise the absence of such protection with his client as a
threshold concern.

There is no conceivable strategic rationale for allowing a client to
proffer information to the lead prosecutor of a separate jurisdiction — in
that jurisdiction's courthouse city, to that jurisdiction's agents — without
first securing that jurisdiction's protection. The failure to do so fell well

all with respect to the Massachusetts prosecution. Without a Massachusetts
proffer agreement, Kumar had none. Basil's affidavit identifies the
February 27 session as the event that converted Kumar from cooperating

witness to prosecution target. Dkt. No. 122-2 3. If any session required

C. Ground Two: Counsel's Materially Inaccurate Guidelines Advice
Induced the Withdrawal of the Motion to Dismiss and the

Unconditional Plea.

1. Deficient Performance.

On September 30, 2022, Kumar sent a letter to the district court explaining
that he wanted to withdraw his motion to dismiss and plead guilty because
he believed his Guidelines sentencing range was only 15 to 20 months.
Dkt. No. 122 at 6 (citing Dkt. No. 83). His actual Guidelines range was 87
to 108 months — more than five times the range counsel communicated to
him. Dkt. No. 130 at 6-8.

This was not a close call or a matter of reasonable professional
disagreement. Kumar was charged with drug trafficking offenses carrying
a fraud cross-reference under U.S.S.G. § 2N2.1 tied to a loss amount
exceeding $3.5 million. The loss table under § 2B1.1(b)(1) applies
mechanically to a documented loss figure. The computation that generates
a 15—20 month estimate for a $3.5 million loss cross-reference case is not a

reasonable professional prediction. It reflects a fundamental failure to

apply the Guidelines to the charged conduct. McMann v. Richardson, 397

competence demanded of attorneys in criminal cases' is constitutionally
infirm). An estimate five times below the actual Guidelines floor falls

outside that range by any measure.

2. Prejudice — The Withdrawal of the Motion to Dismiss.

The causal chain from counsel's deficient advice to Kumar's prejudice is
documented in Kumar's own contemporaneous letter to the district court.
He did not withdraw the motion because he concluded it lacked merit. He
withdrew it because he was told the sentencing consequences of

The record leaves no serious question about what Kumar would

Dkt. No. 93, at 20-21. He directed Attorney Fasoldt to file a supplemental
brief in support on May 12, 2022. Dkt. No. 75. He withdrew it only after
receiving a Guidelines estimate so dramatically wrong that proceeding

appeared irrational. Dkt. No. 83.

A defendant who fired two lawyers over this motion, drafted it

3. Prejudice — The Resulting Sentence and the Glover Standard.

Independently of the motion to dismiss, the Guidelines miscalculation
produced direct sentencing prejudice measurable in months of actual
incarceration. Had counsel accurately estimated the Guidelines exposure
before the plea, Kumar would have had the opportunity to negotiate a plea
agreement that addressed the sequential prosecution consequences —
including a § 5G1.3(b) credit argument, a § 5K2.23 departure request, and
a potential CHC I stipulation. The unconditional plea with no agreement
foreclosed all of these avenues.

Under Glover, any increase in actual jail time flowing from
counsel's deficient performance constitutes cognizable Strickland

prejudice. 531 U.S. at 203-04. The 17-month differential between the

CHC I Guidelines floor of 70 months and the CHC II floor of 87 months
— confirmed by the sentencing court's own pre-allocution statement that it
had considered imposing 72 months — is not a theoretical calculation. It is
a documented, record-proven increment of additional incarceration
flowing directly from counsel's failure to protect Kumar's position before
the plea. Under Glover that is prejudice as a matter of law.
Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129 (2018), and
Cheveres-Morales, 83 F.4th at 43, reinforce this conclusion. A
miscalculated Guidelines range ordinarily warrants relief because the
range serves as the initial benchmark from which all sentencing decisions
proceed. The anchoring effect of the inflated benchmark is itself the

D. Ground Three: Counsel's Failure to Make the § 5K2.23 Departure

Motion at Sentencing.

1. Deficient Performance.

Counsel understood that the sequential prosecution had distorted Kumar's
criminal history. He argued as much in the sentencing memorandum. Dkt.

But counsel gestured at the argument without anchoring it in the
Guidelines provision that directly codifies it. U.S.S.G, § 5K2.23 provides
that a downward departure is warranted where the defendant has
completed a sentence for related conduct that, had it been undischarged at
sentencing, would have qualified for a § 5G1.3(b) adjustment. The
Sentencing Commission's own commentary to Amendment 660 states the
provision's purpose in terms that map directly onto Kumar's facts: to
mitigate ‘the possibility that the fortuity and timing of separate
prosecutions will grossly increase the defendant's sentence.’

A competent attorney arguing against the sentencing consequences
of sequential federal prosecution does not appeal to fairness and equity as

that codifies that principle uncited. The § 5K2.23 departure motion should
have been the centerpiece of the sentencing argument. The failure to make
it explicitly and in Guidelines terms fell below the objective standard of
reasonableness.

The government's response below — that § 5G1.3(b) applies only to
undischarged terms — proves the point rather than defeating it. Dkt. No.
122 at 2. § 5K2.23 exists precisely for the situation where the prior term
has been discharged. Counsel's failure to distinguish between the two
provisions and to invoke the applicable one reflects the same fundamental

unfamiliarity with the Guidelines framework that produced the five-fold

satisfies Strickland's prejudice standard. Under Glover, the additional
months of incarceration resulting from counsel's failure to make this

argument constitute cognizable Sixth Amendment injury.

E. The Three IAC Grounds Are Mutually Reinforcing and

Collectively Establish the Carrier Gateway.

Each of the three IAC grounds independently satisfies Strickland. Taken
together they describe systemic failure across every stage at which counsel
could have protected Kumar from the consequences of the government's
sequential prosecution strategy. Counsel failed at the cooperation stage —
allowing Kumar to proffer to Massachusetts investigators without
Massachusetts protection, in Massachusetts, to a Massachusetts AUSA.
Counsel failed at the litigation stage — providing a Guidelines estimate so
dramatically wrong that it induced Kumar to abandon the only legal
challenge that could have stopped the prosecution. And counsel failed at
the sentencing stage — arguing for a result the court was predisposed to
reach without providing the Guidelines vehicle that would have allowed
the court to get there.

The Carrier gateway follows directly. Under Lynch v. Ficco, 438
F.3d at 49-50, the Strickland prejudice showing on any one of these three
grounds simultaneously satisfies the Frady actual prejudice requirement
for excusing the procedural default of the Fifth Amendment claims in
Argument II. The IAC is not merely an independent ground for relief — it
is the explanation for why the constitutional claims were never properly

litigated below, and the reason this Court must reach them on the merits.
