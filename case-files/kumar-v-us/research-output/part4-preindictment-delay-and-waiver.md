## 1. The correct First Circuit test -- the Government is substantially right that it is conjunctive and demands improper purpose

**Marion/Lovasco floor (BINDING):**
- United States v. Marion, 404 U.S. 307, 324-25 (1971) [CONFIRMED text]: due process would require dismissal if the delay "caused substantial prejudice to appellees' rights to a fair trial and that the delay was an intentional device to gain tactical advantage over the accused"; the Court found "No actual prejudice to the conduct of the defense is alleged or proved, and there is no showing that the Government intentionally delayed to gain some tactical advantage over appellees or to harass them."
- United States v. Lovasco, 431 U.S. 783, 795-96 (1977) [CONFIRMED text]: "investigative delay is fundamentally unlike delay undertaken by the Government solely 'to gain tactical advantage over the accused' ... precisely because investigative delay is not so one-sided." Lovasco left open "prosecutorial delay incurred in reckless disregard of circumstances, known to the prosecution, suggesting that there existed an appreciable risk that delay would impair the ability to mount an effective defense" (the Government's concession, quoted at 795 n.17 [pinpoint VERIFY]); Lovasco found "no evidence of recklessness."

**First Circuit test (BINDING) -- confirmed from the opinions themselves:**
- Irizarry-Colon, 848 F.3d 61, 70 (1st Cir. 2017) [CONFIRMED]: "excessive pre-indictment delay can sometimes, albeit rarely, violate the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause if the defendant shows both that the 'delay caused substantial prejudice to his right to a fair trial' and that 'the [government intentionally delayed indictment ... to gain a tactical advantage.'" (quoting Bater, 594 F.3d at 54, quoting United States v. Picciandra, 788 F.2d 39, 42 (1st Cir. 1986)). "With respect to intentional delay for tactical reasons, this court has indicated that such delay violates the Due Process Clause when it is the product of ''bad faith' reasons.' United States v. Crooks, 766 F.2d 7, 11 (1st Cir. 1985)." Permissible: delay "for the purpose of investigation," "for the purpose of discovering all the participants in a conspiracy so they could be tried together," "for the purpose of letting state authorities pursue their own prosecution." Possibly impermissible: delay "in order to deprive the defendant of witnesses" (Bater) or "impose the burden of defending oneself in back-to-back trials" (Ramos-Gonzalez, 775 F.3d 483, 491 (1st Cir. 2015)).
- Crooks, 766 F.2d at 11 [CONFIRMED]: delay is late "only if the delay significantly prejudices the defendant and the government 'intentionally delayed' the indictment 'to gain an unfair tactical advantage or for other bad faith motives.'" (quoting Marler, 756 F.2d 206, 215 (1st Cir. 1985)). Government's "efforts to discover all those who participated in the conspiracy and to try them together" = legitimate.
- Bater, 594 F.3d 51, 54 (1st Cir. 2010) [CONFIRMED]: the claim "would still fail because there is no evidence that the government delayed the indictment to deprive Bater of Phillips' testimony and this alone defeats the due process claim."
- Munoz-Franco, 487 F.3d 25, 58 (1st Cir. 2007) [CONFIRMED]: "Even if appellants had demonstrated some degree of prejudice from the delay, they have made no concomitant showing that the government intentionally delayed indictment to gain tactical advantage." (Gov't cites 58; pinpoint plausible.)
- Royle, 86 F.4th 462 (1st Cir. 2023) [CONFIRMED via CourtListener opinion 9898696]: quotes Irizarry-Colon; "The second prong requires showing 'deliberate misconduct by the prosecutor (or at least something very close to that).' Bater, 594 F.3d at 54." Review "for abuse of discretion. Irizarry-Colon, 848 F.3d at 70." **But Royle is not a pre-indictment-delay case**: it concerned delayed notice of a warrantless search; the court applied pre-indictment-delay "principles" only by assumption ("Even if we assume that the 'principles' from the pre-indictment delay context govern Royle's claim ... this is not one of the rare cases"). The Government cites it at 471-73 for the proposition that the district court's determinations "are plainly fatal." It is a fair "see also," not authority on the delay issue.
- McCoy, 977 F.2d 706, 711 (1st Cir. 1992) [CONFIRMED]: "the further argument that the delay diminished the opportunity to serve concurrent time on the state and federal offenses is based on the speculation that either sentence would be made to run concurrently." Stokes, 124 F.3d 39, 44 (1st Cir. 1997) [CONFIRMED]: delay prejudice claims "related to sentencing possibilities" are "too speculative"; denial of "the benefit of running his state and federal sentences concurrently ... is pure speculation." **Adverse and directly on point to Kumar's sentencing-prejudice theory.**

**So does the First Circuit "require improper motive" as the Government says, or a balancing test?**
The First Circuit uses the conjunctive two-prong test (substantial prejudice to the right to a fair trial, AND intentional delay for tactical advantage/bad faith), not the balancing approach other circuits use. [I did not verify the other circuits' formulations in this pass; do not cite a "circuit split" until a separate survey is done -- the April 2026 internal memo's circuit-split passages came from a cert. petition and are unverified.] The district court's phrase "improper motive" (as quoted by the Government) is a fair paraphrase of "intentional ... to gain a tactical advantage" / "bad faith." **We lose nothing by accepting the test; our fight is over what counts as "prejudice" and what the Basil affidavit proves about purpose.**

**Standard of review the Government invokes:** "abuse of discretion" (United States v. Handa, 892 F.3d 95, 101 (1st Cir. 2018)). **Handa is a Sixth Amendment speedy-trial (post-indictment) case**, not a pre-indictment-delay case; at 101 it says the First Circuit has "generally reviewed district court rulings on speedy trial motions for abuse of discretion" but notes that practice "is in tension with the rules of other circuits" and does not revisit it "because the parties do not dispute that abuse of discretion review applies." [CONFIRMED text; pinpoint [VERIFY].] Moreover, the abuse-of-discretion standard in these cases governs a direct appeal from a ruling on a *motion to dismiss the indictment*. Here the district court was deciding a Section 2255 claim; there was no ruling on the motion to dismiss. In a 2255 appeal the framework is de novo for legal conclusions and clear error for fact-findings (see Part 3). Royle and Irizarry-Colon do state abuse-of-discretion for pre-indictment delay, so the Government has some support; but it cited the wrong case (Handa) for the proposition. [Minor but useful audit point.]

## 2. Government waiver ground (i): withdrawal of the motion to dismiss

**Gov't:** "he expressly withdrew his motion to dismiss the indictment on that precise ground [ECF 58, 75, 80, 82], which is classic waiver." United States v. Concepcion-Guliam, 62 F.4th 26, 31 (1st Cir. 2023); United States v. Rodiguez [sic: Rodriguez], 311 F.3d 435, 437 (1st Cir. 2002).

**What the cases hold [CONFIRMED]:**
- Rodriguez, 311 F.3d at 437: "A party waives a right when he intentionally relinquishes or abandons it. Johnson v. Zerbst ... This is to be distinguished from ... 'forfeiture.'" "A party who identifies an issue, and then explicitly withdraws it, has waived the issue." The court stressed the withdrawal was repeated "both personally and through counsel" and "[i]ndeed, it is difficult to conceive of a more conspicuous example of a knowing and voluntary abandonment of a legal right." The case was decided on the government's motion for summary affirmance, and presented "a pure question of law."
- Concepcion-Guliam, 62 F.4th 26 (1st Cir. 2023) [CONFIRMED text via CourtListener opinion 9378585; the pinpoint (31 vs. 32) is uncertain -- Gov't says 31; Kumar's brief says 31; the April memo says 32]: "When a defendant raises a Fourth Amendment claim before the district court and subsequently withdraws that claim, he has waived the claim and is precluded from resurfacing it on appeal." Facts: counseled defendant withdrew a suppression motion "days before the scheduled hearing." Direct appeal. No claim that the withdrawal was induced by constitutionally deficient advice.

**Points of distinction (use all):**
1. Both are **direct appeals**; this is **collateral review** where the question is whether the default can be excused by cause and prejudice. Waiver on direct appeal ends the inquiry; on collateral review, IAC supplies "cause." Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986) [CONFIRMED]: "Ineffective assistance of counsel, then, is cause for a procedural default." The Government's motion never mentions cause and prejudice.
2. Waiver requires a **knowing and intelligent** relinquishment: Rodriguez itself relies on Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) ("an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege") [CONFIRMED exists]. Kumar's contemporaneous letter (ECF 83) says he withdrew because he thought his range was 15-20 months; actual 87-108.
3. Waiver by an "erroneous prediction" is usually not enough (Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742, 757 (1970); McMann) -- **be candid**: ordinary miscalculation does not make a plea involuntary. Our claim must be that the advice fell outside "the range of competence demanded of attorneys in criminal cases" (McMann, 397 U.S. at 771) [CONFIRMED exists; pinpoint VERIFY]. A fivefold error on a Guidelines range is on its face outside that range, but see the pro se/standby-counsel problem below.
4. The Government's own sources do not claim that Kumar understood the sentencing consequence of withdrawing. They cite ECF 58, 75, 80, 82 and say nothing about ECF 83.

**The pro se / standby-counsel question -- partly resolved by the docket, still needs the September 2022 entries.** Kumar's brief says he proceeded "pro se with standby counsel" when he filed the motion (Br. 16, 51-52) -- true for 2/28/2022. But the CourtListener RECAP docket (see record-index-district-docket-excerpt.md) shows that after the 3/15/2022 Faretta colloquy "Mr. Kumar states that he wants to be represented by counsel, and Attorney Fasoldt is appointed" (ECF 65); Judge Wolf then ordered Kumar to "consult with Mr. Fasoldt" about whether to maintain the motion (ECF 72); Fasoldt filed the supplement (ECF 75). **So the withdrawal in Sept. 2022 very probably occurred while Fasoldt was counsel**, which removes the main legal obstacle to an IAC-as-cause theory (a defendant who elects self-representation "cannot thereafter complain that the quality of his own defense amounted to a denial of 'effective assistance of counsel.'" Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806, 834 n.46 (1975) [CONFIRMED verbatim]; McKaskle v. Wiggins, 465 U.S. 168 (1984) [exists]). **Cautions:** (a) the RECAP docket stops at ECF 77 (7/14/2022); the withdrawal papers (ECF 78-85) are not available; Kumar may have discharged counsel again and gone pro se before September 2022 (his brief and the Government's "ECF 80, 82" suggest further procedural events); (b) Kumar's brief omits that he was re-represented from 3/15/2022; (c) the Sept. 30, 2022 letter (ECF 83) is Kumar's own; we do not know whether the 15-20 estimate came from Fasoldt, from the Government, from Probation, or from Kumar's own reading of the Guidelines. **Resolve with ECF 78-93 and Kumar's/Fasoldt's account before asserting that counsel gave the advice.**

**Waiver vs. forfeiture; counsel's authority.** Withdrawing a motion is a tactical decision for which the Government will argue counsel (or a pro se defendant) has authority to waive (cf. New York v. Hill, 528 U.S. 110 (2000) [not checked; VERIFY]). Our response must be that when the withdrawal is the *product* of the ineffectiveness, it is not a valid waiver of the underlying claim for collateral-review purposes.

**Rating:** The Government's withdrawal-waiver ground is **strong on its face**; our counter is **moderate** (cause via IAC, and a Government omission) and **improved by the docket (counsel appointed 3/15/2022)** but still **depends on facts we do not have** about who gave the Guidelines advice and what the court said when it accepted the withdrawal.

## 3. Government waiver ground (ii): Rule 12(b)(3)(A)(ii)

**Gov't:** "he never pursued the motion to dismiss, as required by Fed. R. Crim. P. 12(b)(3)(A)(ii)." Cites Robertson, 162 F.4th 209, 224-25 (1st Cir. 2025); Turner, 124 F.4th 69, 77-78 (1st Cir. 2024); Brown, 498 F.3d 523, 528 (6th Cir. 2007); Oslund, 453 F.3d 1048, 1057-58 (8th Cir. 2006).

**Rule text** [LII; CONFIRMED but secondary]: Rule 12(b)(3) requires that "The following defenses, objections, and requests must be raised by pretrial motion if the basis for the motion is then reasonably available and the motion can be determined without a trial on the merits" including (A)(ii) "preindictment delay." Rule 12(c)(3): "If a party does not meet the deadline for making a Rule 12(b)(3) motion, the motion is untimely. But a court may consider the defense, objection, or request if the party shows good cause." [Pull the official rule text from uscourts.gov or govinfo before quoting.]

**What the cited cases hold:**
- **Turner, 124 F.4th 69** [CONFIRMED text via CourtListener opinion 10771661]: defendant "did not move in the district court for dismissal of the felon-in-possession count" on Second Amendment grounds, first mentioning it in a sentencing memorandum, 18 months after the pretrial-motions deadline; "A late-filed Rule 12(b)(3) motion may not be considered unless 'the party shows good cause'"; "When a party misses a Rule 12 deadline and does not demonstrate good cause ... the consequence is that the matter is waived on appeal." Turner also states "Class ... [does not] address Rule 12." **Distinction: Turner never filed a motion; Kumar filed one (ECF 58) and a supplement (ECF 75).** Rule 12(b)(3)'s requirement ("raised by pretrial motion") was *met*.
- **Brown, 498 F.3d 523 (6th Cir. 2007)** [CONFIRMED text via CAP]: "Brown never moved to dismiss the indictment based on delay. His argument on appeal is therefore waived." (citing former Rule 12(e)). **Distinction: no motion at all; out-of-circuit; decided under the pre-2014 Rule 12(e) "waiver" language, since replaced by 12(c)(3) "good cause"** (2014 amendments; [VERIFY committee-note language: LII summary says the Committee "deliberately avoided the term 'waiver'"]).
- **Oslund, 453 F.3d 1048 (8th Cir. 2006)** [CONFIRMED text via CAP]: "Oslund raises this issue for the first time on appeal." **Distinction: same; pre-2014; out-of-circuit.**
- **Robertson, 162 F.4th 209 (1st Cir. 2025)** [UNCONFIRMED: CourtListener citation lookup returned "NOT FOUND"; search of ca1 opinions since 1/1/2025 for case name "Robertson" returned nothing; a web search surfaced no 1st Cir. Robertson at that cite (only an unrelated 6th Cir. 7/17/2026 Robertson, No. 25-3644). **Do not assume it exists or says what the Government says; pull the volume.** Possibly mis-cited or not yet indexed.]

**Key point:** the Government quotes Rule 12(b)(3)(A)(ii) to say he "never pursued the motion." The rule imposes a duty to **raise** a delay claim *by pretrial motion*; it says nothing about "pursuing" a filed motion to a ruling. Kumar **raised** it. The consequence of a withdrawal is governed by ordinary waiver doctrine (ground (i)), not Rule 12. The Government's second ground is thus **derivative of the first and adds nothing**, and each of its authorities concerns a defendant who never filed a motion. If the Court treats withdrawal as a "failure to meet the deadline," Rule 12(c)(3) provides "good cause" relief -- and the same IAC facts supply good cause.

**Rating:** Government's ground (ii) is **weak to moderate** on the law as applied (cases distinguishable), **but** the rule's "good cause" requirement hands the Government a framework that, if the court rejects IAC, resolves the issue against Kumar.

## 4. Government waiver ground (iii): the unconditional plea (Tollett)

**Gov't:** Tollett v. Henderson, 411 U.S. 258, 267 (1973); United States v. Maldonado, 177 F.4th 99, 105 (1st Cir. 2026); United States v. Gaffney, 469 F.3d 211, 214-15 & n.2 (1st Cir. 2006).

**Tollett (BINDING) [CONFIRMED verbatim]:** "a guilty plea represents a break in the chain of events which has preceded it in the criminal process. When a criminal defendant has solemnly admitted in open court that he is in fact guilty of the offense with which he is charged, he may not thereafter raise independent claims relating to the deprivation of constitutional rights that occurred prior to the entry of the guilty plea. **He may only attack the voluntary and intelligent character of the guilty plea by showing that the advice he received from counsel was not within the standards set forth in McMann.**" (411 U.S. at 267.) Hill, 474 U.S. at 58, ties Strickland's first prong to "the standard of attorney competence already set forth in Tollett v. Henderson ... and McMann v. Richardson." **Therefore Tollett expressly permits (even in 2255) an IAC attack on the plea's voluntariness; the Government's Tollett ground does not answer a claim that the plea (and the withdrawal that preceded it) were products of ineffective assistance.** The Government's motion never says this.

**Maldonado (1st Cir. 2026)** [CONFIRMED via CourtListener opinion 11335992: United States v. Maldonado, No. 24-2088 (1st Cir. June 2, 2026) (published); CourtListener shows no reporter cite -- **"177 F.4th 99" UNCONFIRMED; verify**]: "Maldonado's unconditional guilty plea has waived any challenge to the grand jury proceedings as having been infected by prosecutorial overreach." It quotes Tollett (including the "attack the voluntary and intelligent character" carve-out through deficient counsel advice), says Class "reaffirmed Tollett's holding," and says the First Circuit "assiduously followed the letter and spirit of Tollett, holding with monotonous regularity that an unconditional guilty plea effectuates a waiver of any and all independent non-jurisdictional lapses that may have marred the case's progress up to that point." (quoting United States v. Cordero, 42 F.3d 697, 699 (1st Cir. 1994)). **Direct appeal; grand-jury claims.** It also notes that the plea defeats claims that are not "jurisdictional." **Honest read: it is adverse to Kumar's Class argument for the delay claim and supports the Government on Tollett; it is distinguishable for vindictive prosecution (Blackledge) and for IAC-based voluntariness attacks.**

**Gaffney, 469 F.3d 211 (1st Cir. 2006)** [CONFIRMED text via CAP]: the court **declined** to rest on Tollett. After stating the Tollett rule, it wrote: "The government contends that Tollett is applicable here and urges us to deny Gaffney's appeal on that basis. We decline to do so." It observed that the plea there was "intertwined with the alleged constitutional violation" and found "it unnecessary to decide whether Gaffney's complaint is indeed an 'independent claim' waived by Tollett, or whether it is so intertwined with the plea itself that it evades Tollett's strictures." **So Gaffney, as cited at 214-15 & n.2, supports the *existence* of the Tollett rule but the court refused to apply it. A "see" cite for the rule is accurate; a reader may infer more than the case gives.** (Footnote 2 not separately read.)

**Class v. United States, 583 U.S. 174 (2018) [CONFIRMED via CourtListener opinion 4246856 and the summary]:** "The question is whether a guilty plea by itself bars a federal criminal defendant from challenging the constitutionality of the statute of conviction on direct appeal. We hold that it does not." Syllabus: "where the claim implicates 'the very power of the State' to prosecute the defendant, a guilty plea cannot by itself bar it. Blackledge, 417 U.S., at 30"; claims that survive "do not contradict the terms of the indictment or the written plea agreement" and can be "resolved 'on the basis of the existing record.'" **Blackledge itself was a *habeas (collateral)* vindictive-prosecution case in which the guilty plea did not bar the claim.** [CONFIRMED in Class: "Perry pleaded guilty. He then sought habeas relief on the grounds that the reindictment amounted to an unconstitutional vindictive prosecution. ... But this Court held that it did not [bar it]."] **Use:** (a) vindictive prosecution survives the plea even collaterally; (b) the preindictment-delay claim is the harder one.

**Menna v. New York, 423 U.S. 61, 62-63 & n.2 (1975)** [CONFIRMED exists in CAP (short per curiam); pinpoint VERIFY]: a plea does not waive a double-jeopardy claim that "judged on its face" bars the prosecution. Kumar's ECF 58 raised double jeopardy and "successive prosecution" (ECF 75) too; the Government's motion and the COA do not mention these.

**Does Tollett bar a Section 2255 claim that counsel's ineffectiveness caused the waiver?** No, per Tollett's own text (above); and the structure of Massaro v. United States, 538 U.S. 500, 504 (2003) [CONFIRMED]: IAC claims may be brought in 2255 whether or not raised on direct appeal. This means the IAC claims (proffer and misadvice) are *not* procedurally defaulted by the failure to raise them on direct appeal -- an important point for the "cause and prejudice" argument: the IAC *claims* themselves are before the court; the question is what relief follows if they succeed.

## 5. Other Government arguments to anticipate: procedural default (failure to raise on direct appeal) and the Government's own forfeiture

- **Procedural default.** Kumar did not raise vindictive prosecution/delay/IAC on direct appeal (Gov't mot. 2: "On direct appeal, he only challenged his sentence."). The general rule: "claims not raised on direct appeal may not be raised on collateral review unless the petitioner shows cause and prejudice." Massaro, 538 U.S. at 504 (citing United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. 152, 167-68 (1982); Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 621-22 (1998)) [CONFIRMED]. **The Government's motion does not assert procedural default** -- it argues "waiver" three ways. If the Government did not raise default in ECF 122, it may have forfeited it. Authorities: Trest v. Cain, 522 U.S. 87, 89 (1997) [CONFIRMED text]: procedural default "is not a jurisdictional matter," and a court of appeals "is not 'required' to raise the issue of procedural default sua sponte"; Wood v. Milyard, 566 U.S. 463 (2012) [CONFIRMED text]: "An affirmative defense, once forfeited, is 'exclu[ded] from the case,' ... and, as a rule, cannot be asserted on appeal," while courts of appeals have discretion (to be exercised in "exceptional cases") to raise a *forfeited* timeliness defense on their own; where the government *deliberately waived* it, the court may not resurrect it. [These are 2254/AEDPA-limitations cases; extension to 2255 procedural default is analogical -- [VERIFY First Circuit 2255 treatment].] **Action:** read ECF 122 and ECF 130 at 16-17 to see exactly what defenses the Government raised and what the district court decided ("waiver" per the Government's motion). The Gov't may defend the judgment on any ground in the record, so forfeiture arguments are not guaranteed to succeed; they are most promising for any ground that required factual development the Government did not seek.
- **Can the Government raise waiver-type grounds on summary disposition when the COA expressly covers the claim and the district court reached the merits?** Yes in principle -- an appellee may defend the judgment on any ground supported by the record, and the district court *also* reached waiver (per the Gov't and Kumar's brief: "denied ... as waived and, in the alternative, as meritless"). The COA does not decide the waiver question; but under Slack, 529 U.S. at 484, where a district court rejects a claim on procedural grounds, a COA requires a showing that jurists of reason would find *both* the underlying constitutional claim and the procedural ruling debatable. **The district court's COA on the delay claim therefore implies (but does not state) that the procedural (waiver) ruling is debatable.** [Moderate argument; [VERIFY Slack wording]. 2253(c)(3) requires the certificate to "indicate which specific issue or issues satisfy the showing" -- ECF 141's language ("claims regarding ... preindictment delay in violation of the Fifth Amendment") is general.]

## 6. The merits of the delay claim, candidly

**Kumar's theory (Br. 29-41):** the Government had "complete investigative knowledge" after 2/27/2020, had decided to charge ("intended to charge him with a co-conspirator later in the year" -- Basil aff. para 8), and waited until Kumar served his D.R.I. sentence so that the D.R.I. conviction would count as criminal history (CHC II) and foreclose concurrent sentencing, filing the complaint 5/12/2021 only when it learned of ICE release.

**Problems (candid):**
1. **Prejudice must be "to his right to a fair trial"** (Irizarry-Colon, Bater, Crooks). Kumar concedes "Kumar does not argue that the delay cost him evidence or witnesses" (Br. 22). He pleaded guilty and had no trial. First Circuit law treats sentencing-related consequences of delay as "too speculative" (Stokes, 124 F.3d at 44) or "speculation" (McCoy, 977 F.2d at 711). Kumar's attempt to bridge via Glover v. United States, 531 U.S. 198, 203 (2001) is a Sixth Amendment IAC sentencing-prejudice case (the "any amount of actual jail time" language) and not a Fifth Amendment preindictment-delay prejudice holding [CONFIRMED exists in CAP; text of 203 not re-read -- VERIFY].
2. **Likely "legitimate purpose" under the First Circuit's own list**: delay to charge together with a co-conspirator = "discovering all the participants in a conspiracy so they could be tried together" (Irizarry-Colon at 70; Crooks at 11). Basil's affidavit para 8 ("intended to charge him with a co-conspirator later in the year") on its face supports the Government. And "to arrest him before he was removed" shows an interest in preserving jurisdiction to prosecute -- not a "tactical advantage" in the Marion sense. The best counter is Kumar's own: the "co-conspirator" rationale is inconsistent with the "COVID/investigation ongoing" rationale (para 5) -- i.e., the affidavit gives two explanations. **Not strong.**
3. **The overlapping Guidelines range** (87 months lies within CHC I and CHC II ranges at the offense level the court used, ECF 130 at 21) -- but compare Kumar's counter (72-month remark; Cheveres-Morales). Need ECF 111 at 41-50 and ECF 130 at 21-22. Also Kumar's own figures seem inconsistent (Part 0 sec. 3, item 3).
4. **No 5G1.3 or 4C1.1 relief was available in any event**: by the 1/2023 sentencing the D.R.I. term (24 months, served; released 5/7/2021) was *discharged*, so 5G1.3(b)/(c) (undischarged term) did not apply; 4C1.1 ("zero-point offender") did not exist until Nov. 1, 2023 [VERIFY effective date and non-retroactivity to 2255]. 5K2.23 (completed term) existed but is a departure, discretionary.
5. **The delay claim is also not even obviously "delay"**: 14.5 months between 2/27/2020 and the 5/17/2021 indictment (Br. says 14 months), much of it during COVID, and within the 5-year limitations period (18 U.S.C. 3282(a)) [VERIFY].

**Rating of the delay merits:** **weak** under First Circuit law. Our value in preserving it is (a) the Basil affidavit's candid statement, and (b) the argument that the First Circuit has never decided whether deliberate timing to enhance a Guidelines range is "tactical advantage" or whether sentencing prejudice can be "substantial prejudice" -- a legal question the COA purports to leave open (Kumar's brief calls it "first impression").

## 7. Vindictive prosecution (not in the COA) -- candid merits note
Blackledge/Goodwin require that charges be brought *in retaliation for the exercise of a legal right*. The Government's sworn explanation (arrest before ICE removal) is non-retaliatory on its face; Kumar's brief identifies no protected right whose exercise triggered the 5/12/2021 complaint except the 3/24/2021 pro se letter correcting his BOP release date (Br. 7), which is not framed as the trigger. Goodwin, 457 U.S. 368 (1982) [CONFIRMED exists]: pretrial setting rarely supports presumption. **Rating: weak on the merits even if reachable under Class.**
