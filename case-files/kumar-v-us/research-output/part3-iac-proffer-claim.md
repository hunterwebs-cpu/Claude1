## 1. What the Government argues (Gov't Mot. 6-7)

1. Quote: "the prejudice element [of Strickland] requires a showing of a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's errors, he would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on going to trial." United States v. Caramadre, 807 F.3d 359, 371 (1st Cir. 2015).
2. The district court "supportably found" no prejudice, assuming deficiency, because (a) "[p]roffer letters typically do not protect a person from prosecution for making false statements as he did in his meeting with federal authorities in Massachusetts" and (b) "even if counsel had obtained an agreement in the District of Massachusetts, there is no evidence that the agreement would have protected against the derivative use of Kumar's statements to further investigate him for criminal wrongdoing as the government points out." (ECF 130 at 13-14.)
3. Therefore "Kumar cannot show a realistic possibility that any evidence would have been suppressible ... or that he could have successfully moved to dismiss the charges" and so cannot show "there is a reasonable probability he would not have pled guilty" (United States v. Fernandez-Santos, 856 F.3d 10, 17 (1st Cir. 2017)).
4. "overwhelming and independent evidence of Kumar's guilt [supra 2-4]" and acceptance-of-responsibility incentive (USSG 3E1.1 cmt. n.2).
5. Standard: Kumar must show "clear error." Dugas v. Coplan, 506 F.3d 1, 3 (1st Cir. 2007).

## 2. The legal standard (BINDING)

- **Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687-88, 694 (1984)** [CONFIRMED via CAP]: performance below an objective standard of reasonableness; prejudice = "a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different."
- **Hill v. Lockhart, 474 U.S. 52, 58-59 (1985)** [CONFIRMED]: "In the context of guilty pleas, the first half of the Strickland v. Washington test is nothing more than a restatement of the standard of attorney competence already set forth in Tollett v. Henderson, supra, and McMann v. Richardson, supra. The second, or 'prejudice,' requirement, on the other hand, focuses on whether counsel's constitutionally ineffective performance affected the outcome of the plea process. In other words, in order to satisfy the 'prejudice' requirement, the defendant must show that there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's errors, he would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on going to trial." (Note this ties Tollett expressly to IAC.)
- **Hill at 59** [CONFIRMED text; pinpoint 59-60 [VERIFY]] on failure-to-investigate-type errors: "where the alleged error of counsel is a failure to investigate or discover potentially exculpatory evidence, the determination whether the error 'prejudiced' the defendant by causing him to plead guilty rather than go to trial will depend on the likelihood that discovery of the evidence would have led counsel to change his recommendation as to the plea. This assessment, in turn, will depend in large part on a prediction whether the evidence likely would have changed the outcome of a trial." **Honest note:** this is the framework the Government is using. A failure to secure an evidentiary protection is analyzed by predicting whether the protection would have changed the trial outcome (i.e., suppression of material evidence).
- **Lee v. United States, 582 U.S. 357 (2017)** [CourtListener opinion 4181053 read in part; pinpoints [VERIFY]]: (i) "the inquiry we prescribed in Hill v. Lockhart focuses on a defendant's decisionmaking, which may not turn solely on the likelihood of conviction after trial." (ii) "A defendant without any viable defense will be highly likely to lose at trial. And a defendant facing such long odds will rarely be able to show prejudice from accepting a guilty plea that offers him a better resolution than would be likely after trial. But that is not because the prejudice inquiry in this context looks to the probability of a conviction for its own sake. It is instead because defendants obviously weigh their prospects at trial in deciding whether to accept a plea." (iii) "Courts should not upset a plea solely because of post hoc assertions from a defendant about how he would have pleaded but for his attorney's deficiencies. Judges should instead look to contemporaneous evidence to substantiate a defendant's expressed preferences."
- First Circuit formulation [CONFIRMED]: Caramadre, 807 F.3d at 371: "In the plea-withdrawal context, the prejudice element requires a showing of 'a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's errors, he would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on going to trial.'" (quoting Moreno-Espada v. United States, 666 F.3d 60, 64 (1st Cir. 2012), a Section 2255 case) [star page 371 confirmed by CourtListener pagination; the Government's pin is accurate].

## 3. Standard of review -- the Government's "clear error" cite is incomplete and misleading in context

Dugas v. Coplan, 506 F.3d 1 (1st Cir. 2007) is a **Section 2254 state-prisoner** case (New Hampshire arson conviction) decided after a federal evidentiary hearing; the Government's page cite (506 F.3d at 3) corresponds to the introduction: "Finding no clear error in the district court's prejudice determination, we affirm." [CONFIRMED; star page of the intro = 3 per context; the longer discussion of the standard is later in the opinion, pinpoint [VERIFY].]

What Dugas actually holds on review (CourtListener cluster 202981; CAP text; verbatim):

> "When the district court undertakes no independent factfinding in a habeas case, we are effectively in the same position as the district court vis-a-vis the state court record and have the ability to review that record from the same vantage point. Consequently, the district court's recension of that record will engender de novo review." (quoting Pike v. Guarino, 492 F.3d 61, 68 (1st Cir. 2007)).

> "The new fact-finding by the district court alters the standard of review that we must apply in this second appeal. Now that the district court has held its own evidentiary hearing, our review is comparable to the review that would apply if we were considering a direct criminal appeal or a § 2255 petition after a federal criminal conviction. ... When the district court has held an evidentiary hearing and made its own determinations as to the weight of the evidence and the credibility of witnesses, its findings are entitled to significant deference. ... Thus, we conduct a de novo review of legal issues, but disturb the district court's factual conclusions only if they are clearly erroneous."

> "the interplay between the district court's factual findings on disputed issues, all of which are subject to the clearly erroneous standard, and its ultimate determination on the prejudice issue, which is often characterized as a mixed question of law and fact. ... The standard of review we apply to mixed questions of fact and law 'depends, in the last analysis, on the extent to which a particular question is fact-dominated or law-dominated.'" (quoting Pike, 492 F.3d at 68; Scarpa v. DuBois, 38 F.3d 1, 9 (1st Cir. 1994) ("[A]n inquiry into the effectiveness of counsel is almost always a mixed question of law and fact.")).

**How to use it (strong, low-risk):**
1. Dugas applies clear-error review to fact findings made **after an evidentiary hearing**; the Government's own cite says that, absent independent fact-finding, review is de novo. Per Kumar's brief there was no hearing (Br. 49, 57; "denial without a hearing" was error). **[VERIFY from ECF 130 whether the court held any hearing, took testimony, or made credibility findings. If it decided on the papers, Dugas supports de novo review.]**
2. The two "findings" the Government quotes are not findings of historical fact at all: "[p]roffer letters typically do not protect ..." is a generalization; "there is no evidence that the agreement would have protected against derivative use" is a statement about the *absence of proof*. Neither is a credibility/weight finding entitled to clear-error deference.
3. The ultimate prejudice question is mixed; where, as here, it depends largely on construing the legal effect of a document that was never put in the record, it is "law-dominated."
4. Also note the Government cites a 2254 case where AEDPA deference did not apply to the unadjudicated prejudice prong (Dugas explains why). The case is not "wrong" law; it is a mismatch with the motion's proposition. Do NOT say "clear error is wrong"; say "clear error applies to findings after a hearing; here there were none."
5. The more apt First Circuit 2255 formulations to cite [VERIFY each before use; I did not locate a 2255-specific "de novo/clear error" sentence in Damon v. United States, 732 F.3d 1 (1st Cir. 2013), which Kumar's brief cites at 3]: United States v. DiCarlo, 575 F.2d 952, 954-55 (1st Cir. 1978) (cited in Dugas for clearly-erroneous review of findings in 2255 proceedings); Pike, 492 F.3d at 68, 75; Scarpa, 38 F.3d at 9.

## 4. Was an evidentiary hearing required under Section 2255(b)? (the "on the papers" point)

- Statute (CONFIRMED, LII): "Unless the motion and the files and records of the case conclusively show that the prisoner is entitled to no relief, the court shall cause notice thereof to be served upon the United States attorney, grant a prompt hearing thereon, determine the issues and make findings of fact and conclusions of law with respect thereto."
- First Circuit gloss -- **not favorable to automatic hearings**: United States v. McGill, 11 F.3d 223, 225-26 (1st Cir. 1993) [CONFIRMED text]: "evidentiary hearings on motions are the exception, not the rule"; the petitioner "bears the burden of establishing the need for an evidentiary hearing"; the court takes factual averments as true but "need not give weight to conclusory allegations, self-interested characterizations, discredited inventions, or opprobrious epithets"; the judge who presided "is at liberty to employ the knowledge gained during the earlier proceedings." David v. United States, 134 F.3d 470, 477 (1st Cir. 1998) [CONFIRMED text; pinpoint VERIFY]: a hearing may be forgone when "(1) the motion is inadequate on its face, or (2) the movant's allegations, even if true, do not entitle him to relief, or (3) the movant's allegations 'need not be accepted as true because they state conclusions instead of facts, contradict the record, or are "inherently incredible."'" Denial of a hearing reviewed for **abuse of discretion** (David).
- **The Government's best response:** even assuming a hearing, Kumar's allegations "even if true" don't show prejudice (Count 3 not protectable; derivative use). **Our best response:** the dispute is over *what the D.R.I. letter said, whether any D. Mass. letter existed, and what terms a competent lawyer could have obtained* -- all facts. The record admits the signed copy cannot be found and Probation never received it (Br. 49, citing ECF 130 at 4 & ECF 122 at 3 n.2). The district court could not decide what the letter "typically" provides without the letter. Machibroda v. United States, 368 U.S. 487, 495 (1962), is the cited basis for hearings when allegations are "detailed and specific" (quoted in David) -- and the 112 F.4th record shows the key interactions were documented.
- **Judge-who-presided note:** Judge Wolf presided over the plea and sentencing and may use that knowledge (McGill); that cuts against a hearing as to events Wolf observed. But the proffer sessions were *pre-indictment, out of court*, and Wolf did not observe them. Kumar's claim does not depend on anything Wolf saw.
- Honest rating on "hearing required": **moderate-to-weak** under First Circuit abuse-of-discretion review; strongest as an alternative request for a limited remand on (i) existence/terms of any proffer letter(s), (ii) what counsel advised before each session, (iii) what, if any, derivative evidence came from the sessions (device access) versus independent sources.

## 5. What a proffer ("queen for a day") agreement protects -- and why Count 3 is the Government's strongest point

**Count 3 is a false-statement prosecution (18 U.S.C. 1001(a)(2)), based on a statement made *during* the 2/27/2020 session.** Prosecution for false statements made during a protected session is not barred by use protection, even statutory immunity.

- **United States v. Apfelbaum, 445 U.S. 115 (1980)** [CONFIRMED existence and holding via CAP]: the Supreme Court held that the use-immunity statute (18 U.S.C. 6002) does not bar the use of immunized grand-jury testimony in a prosecution for false statements made in that testimony; Fifth Amendment/immunity protection does not extend to false statements. [Pinpoint 117, 122-23 [VERIFY].]
- **United States v. Mezzanatto, 513 U.S. 196 (1995)** [CONFIRMED]: a defendant may knowingly waive the Rule 410/11(e)(6) exclusionary protections for plea-discussion statements; proffer letters routinely include such waivers (impeachment/rebuttal use). This is why the Government can say proffer letters "typically" have carve-outs. [Pinpoint [VERIFY].]
- **Kastigar v. United States, 406 U.S. 441 (1972)** [CONFIRMED]: "such immunity from use and derivative use is coextensive with the scope of the privilege against self-incrimination" -- *compelled*, statutorily immunized testimony. A voluntary proffer is governed by contract (the letter), not Kastigar. A defendant who has a *letter* with only direct-use protection (most D. Mass./D.R.I.-style letters) has no Kastigar derivative-use bar. [Whether the D.R.I. letter allowed derivative use is Kumar's own statement (Br. 4-5) and a document we have not seen.]
- **United States v. Velez, 354 F.3d 190 (2d Cir. 2004)** [CONFIRMED text; 2d Cir.; PERSUASIVE only]: enforcing a proffer-agreement waiver permitting use of statements if defendant introduced inconsistent evidence. Not a First Circuit case. **I did not locate First Circuit authority on proffer-letter terms/derivative-use in the time available. [GAP -- run a targeted search on "proffer agreement" "derivative use" ca1 (e.g., United States v. Rosenthal? -- not verified; do not cite).]**

**Consequences for our position:**
1. As to Count 3, the Government is right and Kumar's brief concedes the point ("The question is not whether Kumar's false statements could have been used regardless" Br. 48). **Do not argue that a proper proffer letter would have barred Count 3.** Instead argue (if at all) that effective counsel, aware the Massachusetts prosecutors were present, would have advised him not to speak, or to speak only truthfully under a written agreement -- a causation theory that the false statement was *his* choice and that Strickland prejudice here is not about Count 3.
2. As to Counts 1-2, the argument depends on the (unseen) letter terms and on whether the Government's evidence (spreadsheets, call recordings, email accounts, devices) was **derived from** the proffer sessions or came from independent sources (e.g., border search of devices at JFK; D.R.I. warrants; seizure). **Kumar's brief says he "authorized access to his personal electronic devices and accounts" during the sessions** (Br. 4, citing ECF 130 at 3 / PSR paras 10, 40). 112 F.4th 30 says the spreadsheets and emails were "saved on Kumar's laptop" and recorded calls were "located in Kumar's email account." **If the devices/accounts were accessed only through his proffer-session consent, derivative-use protection would have mattered enormously; if obtained via the 2019 border/D.R.I. process, it would not.** This is the single most important factual question on the IAC claim, and we cannot answer it. **Needed: PSR paras 10, 25-45, 40, 42; ECF 122, 122-1, 122-2; ECF 130 at 3-4, 13-14.**
3. The Government's logic (no suppression -> no prejudice) assumes that Kumar *could not have bargained* for derivative-use protection. The claim is that competent counsel would not have allowed eleven sessions to proceed *without* a Massachusetts agreement. If the Government would have refused any protection, the counsel's alternative was to stop cooperating -- the question then is whether Kumar would have stopped, and what the Government would have done without his devices and statements. Under Hill the "prediction whether the evidence likely would have changed the outcome of a trial" is the test; we need a factual record to make that prediction.

## 6. The "typically" language is an unsupported generalization -- strongest procedural point

- The Government quotes ECF 130 at 13-14: "[p]roffer letters typically do not protect a person from prosecution for making false statements..." and "there is no evidence that the agreement would have protected against derivative use ..." The phrase "typically" reveals that the ruling rests on what proffer letters *usually* say, not on the actual terms of (a) the D.R.I. letter (ECF 122-1) or (b) any D. Mass. letter counsel could have obtained.
- The record problem is acknowledged on both sides: the Probation Office never received the letter and the Government "cannot locate a signed copy" (ECF 122 at 3 n.2, per Kumar's brief). Counsel objected to the PSR statement that there was no cooperation agreement (ECF 130 at 4).
- **Argument:** A court cannot find no prejudice from a missing agreement by assuming the agreement's contents. Under Hill (prediction of outcome) the missing terms are material, and the burden-shifting risk is that the *Government* may argue Kumar bears the burden to prove what protection was obtainable. Be careful: Strickland puts the burden of proving prejudice on the defendant. Frame this as: (a) the question is fact-bound and unresolved (requires hearing), and (b) the Government's assertion that "there is no evidence" is an assertion about a record the court did not develop. [Burden caution: McGill, 11 F.3d at 225-26.]

## 7. Latent threshold risk the Government did not raise: the Sixth Amendment right to counsel and the proffer sessions

- The IAC claim presupposes a Sixth Amendment right to effective counsel at the proffer sessions. The right "is offense specific" and "cannot be invoked once for all future prosecutions" and attaches only when adversary judicial proceedings have begun on that offense. McNeil v. Wisconsin, 501 U.S. 171, 175-76 (1991); Texas v. Cobb, 532 U.S. 162, 167-68 (2001) [both CONFIRMED to exist in CAP; Cobb text read ("McNeil's offense-specific definition"); pinpoints [VERIFY]]; Rothgery v. Gillespie County, 554 U.S. 191 (2008) [CONFIRMED exists].
- The D. Mass. offenses were uncharged until 5/2021, and the sessions (8/2019-2/2020) occurred while D.R.I. wire-fraud/ID-theft charges were pending. A court could hold that Kumar had no Sixth Amendment right to counsel at sessions concerning the uncharged Massachusetts drug conspiracy, so no IAC claim lies for counsel's pre-charge advice/failure to obtain protection. (Counter: counsel was actually retained and acting; some courts examine pre-indictment advice under Strickland when it affects plea consequences, and the sessions were part of cooperation negotiations tied to the pending D.R.I. case; I did not research the doctrine in depth. **[GAP: research pre-charge IAC in the First Circuit before we file; the panel may affirm "on any ground supported by the record" even though the Government assumed deficiency arguendo.]**)
- Practical advice: do not volunteer this point. But prepare for it; it bears on whether to expand the COA to add the "Guidelines misadvice" ground, which arises at a stage where the right to counsel indisputably attached (post-indictment, plea decision), as the cleaner IAC vehicle.

## 8. Counsel's pre-plea advice and sentencing exposure (Ground Two) -- the cleaner IAC claim

- Kumar's 9/30/2022 letter (ECF 83) says he withdrew the motion to dismiss because he believed his Guidelines range was 15-20 months; actual range 87-108 (Br. 50). This is "contemporaneous evidence" under Lee, 582 U.S. at 369 [pinpoint VERIFY], because it was written *before* the plea and before any prejudice from a bad outcome.
- Law: McMann v. Richardson, 397 U.S. 759, 770-71 (1970) [CONFIRMED to exist in CAP] ("range of competence demanded of attorneys in criminal cases"); Hill, 474 U.S. at 58-59; Tollett, 411 U.S. at 267 ("He may only attack the voluntary and intelligent character of the guilty plea by showing that the advice he received from counsel was not within the standards set forth in McMann.") [CONFIRMED verbatim]. A defendant is not entitled to a perfectly accurate Guidelines prediction; the Supreme Court has held plea voluntariness is not undermined by mere miscalculation (Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742, 756-57 (1970)) [CONFIRMED exists; pinpoint VERIFY]. So deficiency requires a gross error, not a missed estimate; a 15-20 vs. 87-108 gap is gross on its face but we do not know *who* gave the 15-20 estimate (appointed counsel Fasoldt? Kumar's own reading? the Government or Probation?). **Status question (partly resolved):** Kumar's brief says he was proceeding pro se with standby counsel when he filed the motion (true on 2/28/2022). The CourtListener RECAP docket shows that on 3/15/2022 he elected counsel and Attorney Charles Fasoldt was appointed (ECF 65), and that Judge Wolf ordered Kumar to consult Fasoldt on whether to maintain the motion to dismiss (ECF 72); counsel filed the supplement (ECF 75). A pro se defendant cannot claim IAC for his own decisions (Faretta, 422 U.S. 806, 834 n.46 [CONFIRMED]); but if Fasoldt was counsel when the motion was withdrawn, Ground Two is an ordinary post-indictment IAC claim. **[VERIFY with ECF 78-93, which RECAP does not have: who represented Kumar on 9/30/2022 and who gave the 15-20 month estimate. Also state his status accurately (the brief omits the 3/15/2022 appointment).]**
- Is Ground Two within the COA? The COA (per the Gov't motion) is for "(1) ineffective assistance of counsel due to failure to obtain proffer protection [and] (2) preindictment delay." **Literally, no.** Argument for coverage: the Government itself put the withdrawal at issue as a *waiver* of the delay claim; whether the withdrawal was knowing and induced by IAC is part of whether the certified delay claim may be reached. See Part 4 and Part 5.

## 9. The Government's reliance on USSG 3E1.1 n.2 and "overwhelming evidence"

- Text (from memory -- [VERIFY BEFORE FILING; pull the Nov. 2021 Guidelines Manual used at sentencing, 112 F.4th n.3]): Application Note 2 to 3E1.1 states that the adjustment "is not intended to apply to a defendant who puts the government to its burden of proof at trial by denying the essential factual elements of guilt, is convicted, and only then admits guilt and expresses remorse," but "[c]onviction by trial, however, does not automatically preclude a defendant from consideration for such a reduction. In rare situations a defendant may clearly demonstrate an acceptance of responsibility for his criminal conduct even though he exercises his constitutional right to a trial." The Government cites it only generally ("See USSG 3E1.1 cmt. (n.2)").
- Problems with the argument: (i) it is an *incentive-to-plead* argument that presumes Kumar's decision-making; Lee says the inquiry "focuses on a defendant's decisionmaking, which may not turn solely on the likelihood of conviction after trial." (ii) The plea here was **open** -- "without a plea agreement" -- so Kumar gave up the right to trial without any charge or sentencing concession from the Government. Lee's logic ("accepting a guilty plea that offers him a better resolution than would be likely after trial") presupposes a bargain; an open plea gets only the Guidelines acceptance credit. (iii) Whether Kumar even *received* acceptance credit is unknown: the sentencing court found his allocution "insincere"/"inconsistent with his guilty plea" (112 F.4th; Br. 10). If acceptance was denied or reduced, the Government's incentive argument is hollow in hindsight, though Lee/Hill ask about the *ex ante* decision. **[Need PSR calculation: offense level components, acceptance adjustment, and whether the 87-108 range was before or after acceptance.]**
- "Overwhelming and independent evidence" (Gov't 6-7, citing "supra 2-4"): the "evidence" cited at pp. 2-4 is a recitation of **PSR facts**, not trial evidence: PSR paras 8-45, 12-14, 15-19, 20, 21-27, 28, 31-45, 40. The PSR is the probation officer's summary. The Government asserts the evidence was "independent" of the proffer sessions but cites no basis. The same pages describe "numerous spreadsheets on his computer" (PSR paras 21-27, 31-39, 41-45) and "customers' credit card information" and the call-center operations. **Which of that came from the sessions, from his devices (accessed with his proffer-session consent?), from the D.R.I. case, or from third parties?** The motion does not say. (112 F.4th: "the government explained that it started with sales spreadsheets and emails that were saved on Kumar's laptop ... recorded sales calls located in Kumar's email account.")
- Caution: the sheer volume of documentary evidence about the scheme (call-center managers, audio recordings, spreadsheets) is, if independent of Kumar's statements, a serious obstacle to Hill prejudice. Honest assessment: **if the Government can show the core evidence was independent, the IAC-proffer claim likely fails on prejudice.** That is why the factual record (and possibly a hearing) is our only real opening.
