# PART 2 -- GOVERNING STANDARD FOR SUMMARY DISPOSITION; FIRST CIRCUIT MOTIONS-PRACTICE NOTE (Track B; replaces judge profile)
Kumar v. United States, No. 26-1250 (1st Cir.). PRIVILEGED / WORK PRODUCT. 2026-10-05.

## 1. The rule, verbatim (BINDING)

1st Cir. R. 27.0(c) (First Circuit Rulebook, amendments through 5/13/2025, p. 55-56; fetched 10/5/2026 from https://www.ca1.uscourts.gov/sites/ca1/files/rulebook.pdf):

> "(c) Summary Disposition. At any time, on such notice as the court may order, on motion of appellee or sua sponte, the court may dismiss the appeal or other request for relief or affirm and enforce the judgment or order below if the court lacks jurisdiction, or if it shall clearly appear that no substantial question is presented. In case of obvious error the court may, similarly, reverse. Motions for such relief should be promptly filed when the occasion appears."

Status: CONFIRMED verbatim. (The Court's 2012 notice of proposed amendment, https://www.ca1.uscourts.gov/sites/ca1/files/Notice_Proposed_Amendment_to_22_1%28e%29.pdf, shows the same text before striking the "four copies of a memorandum or brief" clause; the current rule has no such clause.) The Government's motion calls its vehicle "Local Rule 27(c)" and "Rule 27(c)" in places; the Gov't certificate cites "Fed. R. App. P. 27(d)(2)(A)" for the 5,200-word limit. The operative rule is 1st Cir. R. 27.0(c). FRAP 27(c) separately provides that "A circuit judge may act alone on any motion, but may not dismiss or otherwise determine an appeal or other proceeding" -- so only a panel can grant this.

Key textual points to use:
1. The standard is **"clearly appear."** The court must be able to see from the motion and the record, without full briefing, that **no** substantial question exists. It is a standard about *clarity*, not about who is more likely to win.
2. The rule is **symmetric**: "In case of obvious error the court may, similarly, reverse." Summary action is for obvious cases either way.
3. "**On such notice as the court may order**" -- the Court controls the notice and (by implication) the opportunity to respond. FRAP 27(a)(3)(A) gives 10 days; the Court may extend.
4. "**promptly filed when the occasion appears**" -- the motion came 18 days after the briefing notice; not a strong timing argument (see Part 1).
5. "**lacks jurisdiction**" -- relevant to the notice-of-appeal flag (Part 1, Flag A). If the NOA was untimely the proper disposition is dismissal, not affirmance; the Government has not argued it.

- **Santiago-Lugo v. United States, 538 F.3d 23 (1st Cir. 2008)** (per curiam) -- reasoned disposition of a Rule 41(g) limitations question, ending: "The government's motion for summary affirmance is granted. See 1st Cir. R. 27.0(c)." [CONFIRMED.]
- **United States v. Ellis, 619 F.3d 72 (1st Cir. 2010)** (per curiam) -- "We have reviewed the record and the parties' submissions. We allow the government's motion for summary disposition, and we affirm." Followed by a reasoned discussion of a narrow ACCA question. [CONFIRMED.]
- Older: Farm Construction Servs., Inc. v. Fudge, 831 F.2d 18 (1st Cir. 1987); United States v. Jones, 997 F.2d 967 (1st Cir. 1993) (both under the former Local Rule 27.1; "no substantial question"). I did not extract holdings beyond the one-line disposition language. [VERIFY BEFORE FILING if cited.]

Pattern (inference from these three, not a stated rule): First Circuit summary dispositions tend to involve (i) pure questions of law or (ii) an issue the court can resolve on a clear record. Both published examples recite that the court reviewed "the record and the parties' submissions." That supports an argument that the record must be before the panel. Do NOT present this as a holding.

**Related First Circuit text confirming the court converts summary-disposition candidates to full briefing.** IOP VII(D)(3): panel assignment may change "where a case has been assigned to a panel, but ... changes in the procedural handling of the case (e.g., a case intended for summary disposition is thereafter set for oral argument) require rescheduling." So the court itself contemplates cases "intended for summary disposition" that end up fully briefed and argued.

## 3. Applying "no substantial question" to our posture -- the best arguments, and their limits

**A. The COA is a threshold finding that a substantial constitutional question exists.**
- 28 U.S.C. 2253(c)(2): "A certificate of appealability may issue under paragraph (1) only if the applicant has made a substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right." 2253(c)(3): the certificate "shall indicate which specific issue or issues satisfy the showing required by paragraph (2)." [Cornell LII text; CONFIRMED.]
- Slack v. McDaniel, 529 U.S. 473, 484 (2000): "Where a district court has rejected the constitutional claims on the merits, the showing required to satisfy 2253(c) is straightforward: The petitioner must demonstrate that reasonable jurists would find the district court's assessment of the constitutional claims debatable or wrong." (Quoted in Miller-El; Slack text in CAP; pinpoint [VERIFY].) Where a claim is rejected on procedural grounds the petitioner must show jurists of reason would find it debatable (a) whether the petition states a valid constitutional claim and (b) whether the district court was correct in its procedural ruling. [VERIFY exact wording at 484.]
- Miller-El v. Cockrell, 537 U.S. 322, 338 (2003): "We do not require petitioner to prove, before the issuance of a COA, that some jurists would grant the petition for habeas corpus. Indeed, a claim can be debatable even though every jurist of reason might agree, after the COA has been granted and the case has received full consideration, that petitioner will not prevail." [CONFIRMED text via CAP; pinpoint 338 [VERIFY].] **Use:** a COA signals the case is to receive "full consideration." The Government's request is, in substance, that the panel find "no substantial question" on the two issues a federal judge certified as presenting a "substantial showing."
- **Limits (be candid):** (i) the COA standard ("debatable among jurists of reason") is not identical to Rule 27.0(c)'s "no substantial question"; courts do summarily affirm COA-certified issues; (ii) the district court's COA order was (per Kumar) issued "sua sponte" citing "unusual facts," without stated reasons per issue; (iii) the panel is not bound by a district judge's COA; (iv) the Government will say the COA issues have since been shown to be insubstantial by the waiver and prejudice law. So this is a persuasive, not controlling, argument.

**B. The dispositive questions are not "clear."**
1. *Standard of review for the IAC prejudice ruling.* The Government invokes "clear error" via Dugas v. Coplan. The same opinion says de novo where the district court "undertakes no independent factfinding" and clear error only after "the district court has held an evidentiary hearing and made its own determinations as to the weight of the evidence and the credibility of witnesses." (Part 3 for quotes.) Whether the district court held a hearing is unknown; Kumar says it did not (Br. 49, 57).
2. *Whether IAC-induced withdrawal can be "cause" that defeats the waiver defense* -- the Government's motion never addresses cause and prejudice (Murray v. Carrier) even though that is Kumar's central answer to waiver. A motion for *summary* disposition that ignores the opponent's principal response does not make the answer "clear."
3. *Whether Tollett bars a Section 2255 claim that counsel's ineffectiveness caused the abandonment* -- Tollett itself carves out the attack on the voluntariness/intelligence of the plea through counsel's constitutionally deficient advice, and Maldonado (2026) repeats that carve-out (Part 4).
4. *Record dependence.* The Government's prejudice and merits arguments rely on ECF 130 pages, PSR paragraphs, an unavailable proffer letter, and an affidavit (ECF 122-2) whose para 8 helps Kumar. None are attached to the motion we have (FRAP 27(a)(2)(B)(iii) requires the trial court's opinion as a separate exhibit for motions seeking substantive relief).
5. *Panel practice treats record-heavy fact disputes as requiring briefing* (inference; no authority located).

**C. Where the Government is right (do not overclaim).**
- The scope-of-COA argument (Part 5) is strong on First Circuit law and the Court's own 7/1/2026 order.
- Count 3 (18 U.S.C. 1001) is not protected by a proffer agreement as a matter of settled law (Apfelbaum, 445 U.S. 115 (1980) [CONFIRMED existence/content via CAP, pin [VERIFY]]; United States v. Mezzanatto, 513 U.S. 196 (1995) upholds waiver of exclusionary protections in plea/proffer discussions).
- Preindictment-delay merits: the First Circuit test is conjunctive and demanding (Irizarry-Colon, Bater, Crooks, Royle, McCoy, Stokes).
- Tollett/Maldonado: the First Circuit applies Tollett "with monotonous regularity."
So a realistic outcome is that the panel will treat at least Argument I (Class extension) and the vindictive-prosecution merits as outside the case or without merit. The practical fight is over (a) whether the two COA issues get full briefing (and a hearing), and (b) whether the COA is expanded for the claim that underlies the waiver issue (IAC-misadvice).

## 4. FIRST CIRCUIT PANEL / MOTIONS PRACTICE NOTE (replaces judge-profile track)

Sources: First Circuit Rulebook IOPs V, VII (fetched 10/5/2026), FRAP 27.

1. **Who decides.** IOP V(B): "The motion judge and the motion panel duties are rotated among the judges of this Court"; "single judge matters are transmitted to a single judge and the matters calling for three judge action are transmitted to a three judge panel." A motion for summary disposition is three-judge business (FRAP 27(c)). IOP V(B): "All motions are decided without oral argument."
2. **Timing.** IOP V(B): motions are "submitted to the Court after the response time provided in Fed. R. App. P. 27(a)(3)(A) has run except for (1) routine procedural motions which are usually processed forthwith, and (2) emergency motions..." and "The court will not ordinarily await the filing of a reply to a response before acting on a motion and response." **Implication:** the summary-disposition motion likely went to the panel on or about 9/25/2026 (after the 9/24 deadline), and the Court "will not ordinarily await" further papers. If we want to be considered we must file now.
3. **Stay motion = procedural motion.** Likely handled "forthwith" by the clerk or a single judge. Clerk's orders on routine procedural motions "state on their face that [they are] entered pursuant to 1st Cir. R. 27.0(d)" (IOP V(C)). A party "adversely affected ... may promptly move for reconsideration" (1st Cir. R. 27.0(d); FRAP 27(b)). "Timely opposition filed after the motion is granted ... does not constitute a request to reconsider" (FRAP 27(b)). Therefore if the stay was granted, file a motion for reconsideration to vacate it.
4. **Briefing/calendar logic.** IOP VII(F): cases with an appellee brief filed by the 15th of a month are screened for the second month thereafter. Stay of the Gov't brief delays calendar placement; the briefing notice's "December, 2026 session" projection assumed a 9/23 Gov't brief. Delay is concrete prejudice to Kumar (projected release 8/26/2027 per his brief; expedited review requested; IOP VII(B) says such requests "should be made shortly after the case is docketed").
5. **Local Rule 45.0(b)**: if the appellee's brief is in default, "the cause must be assigned to the next list and the appellee will not be heard at oral argument except by leave of the Court." (If no stay was granted and the Gov't did not file by 9/23.)
6. **Panel continuity for COA expansion.** Bui v. DiPaolo, 170 F.3d 232, 238 (1st Cir. 1999), quoted at IOP VII(D)(2): "to the extent practicable, the panel that determines whether to issue a complementary COA also will be the panel that adjudicates the appeal on the merits." If we move to expand the COA, expect the same panel to handle both.
7. **Bui deferral rule (potentially valuable):** "when the district court grants a limited COA and the petitioner seasonably seeks to expand it, his appeal on the certified issue(s) should be held in abeyance, and full briefing deferred, until this court determines the appealability of the issues that the district court deemed unworthy of appellate scrutiny." (170 F.3d 232, ~237-38 [pinpoint VERIFY]; CAP text confirmed.) The word "seasonably" cuts against us (the 7/31 deadline passed) but supports asking the Court to decide expansion BEFORE or together with summary disposition, and to deny the Government's request to dispose of the appeal on a narrowed COA while an expansion request is pending.
8. **Composition of the panel is unknown** (not assigned until screening; IOP VIII(B): panel names are disclosed only 7 days before a session). No judge-specific research is possible now. If the case is put on the December 2026 list, panel will be known about 7 days before.
9. **Pro se filings.** The First Circuit's general practice is to construe pro se filings liberally (I did not locate a controlling First Circuit cite in the time available; Kumar's brief cites Ciampi, 419 F.3d at 24, but my read of the CAP text shows Ciampi mentioning the point only as the petitioner's argument -- [VERIFY BEFORE FILING; find a better cite, e.g., Erickson v. Pardus, 551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007) (pro se filings "held to less stringent standards"), [VERIFY]]).

## 5. Takeaways for the opposition
- Quote Rule 27.0(c) exactly and make "clearly appear" the organizing phrase.
- Lead with the Court's own COA decision and Miller-El ("full consideration"), then the unaddressed cause-and-prejudice question and the standard-of-review error, then record deficiencies.
- Ask in the alternative for a ruling on expansion of the COA before any merits disposition (Bui).
- Do not argue that the Government's cases are "wrong" where they are simply on point; concede the COA-scope default and spend credit on cause/prejudice and the hearing request.
