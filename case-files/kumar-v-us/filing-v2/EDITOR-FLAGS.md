## EDITOR FLAGS: Kumar v. United States, No. 26-1250, Response in Opposition to Summary Disposition

### Big idea

A proffer letter does not protect a lie; it protects the truth, and the truth Kumar told, plus the devices he handed over, is what the drug case was built on. The Government's "no protection for false statements" answer disposes only of Count 3. Whether Kumar would have pleaded to Counts 1 and 2 with his own words out of the case-in-chief is an unheard fact question that two judges certified. On scope: the only judge who decided the motion certified all of it, and the narrowing came from a clerk's Rule 27.0(d) order that no party asked for and Kumar never received. Waiver requires a known right knowingly given up.

### Strategy and accuracy items the client should see

1. Timeliness. FRAP 27(a)(3)(A) sets a response deadline of 10 days after service. FRAP 26(c) adds 3 days for mail service. I.O.P. V(A) says the date of service fixes the due date. Service was September 11, 2026, so the rule deadline was September 24, 2026. A flat statement that the response is "timely" would be inaccurate. Part IV therefore says the response is "properly before the Court": no order set a date, the Court has not ruled, and Rule 27.0(c) refers to "such notice as the court may order." It then asks for an extension or late-filing leave under FRAP 27(a)(3)(A) and 26(b), and the title flags that request.
2. The Rule 27.0(d) reconsideration request. Both Rule 27.0(d) and FRAP 27(b) speak of clerk action "on a motion." The brief says so in one sentence and argues that an order entered without any motion is no less reviewable.
3. Clerk's authority. The argument says no rule commits COA determinations to the Clerk. That is accurate as to published rules. I.O.P. V(C) and Rule 27.0(d), however, refer to the Court's "standing instructions," which are not public. If those instructions cover requests to district courts to specify COA issues, the Court may treat the May 8 order as authorized. The brief argues the legal limits of the order, not misconduct, and keeps a respectful register.
4. Bui, 170 F.3d at 238 n.2. In 1999 the circuit had opted, "at least for the time being," to commit COA determinations to three-judge panels. The brief states this as historical fact ("When this Court adopted its procedure..."). Current practice is unverified.
5. Gonzalez is used for three points: the defect is nonjurisdictional, the remedy is triggered by a party's timely objection, and the panel addresses it. Gonzalez does not hold that a court may never act on such a defect sua sponte. The brief does not claim that it does.
6. Griggs. The divestiture principle has well-known exceptions, such as acts in aid of the appeal and remands by the appellate court. The brief ties the narrowing to a lack of this Court's authority, not to an absolute bar.
7. The "information provided" theory. Under ECF 122-1 ¶ 1, data from devices a proffering defendant hands over may count as "information provided," which cannot be used directly in the case-in-chief, rather than as a derivative lead. That reading is an argument from the text of the letter, with no case authority behind it. The brief presents it as an unaddressed question, not a settled rule. A Researcher check for First Circuit or other authority on proffer-letter treatment of consensually produced device data would strengthen it.
8. Count 3 concession. The concession is stated plainly and cabined. If the plea is vacated under Hill, Count 3 could still be tried. The brief does not say otherwise.
9. Delay claim. The certified delay claim is weak on prejudice: Judge Wolf found 87 months within the range for Criminal History Category I or II (ECF 130 at 21). The brief does not contest that point. It ties the waiver issues to the validity of the plea and uses ECF 122-2 ¶ 8 (the planned "later in the year" charge) to show that the charging date was a choice.
10. Declaration ¶ 9 says Kumar would have applied for an expanded COA had he received the July 1 order. ¶ 2 repeats the January 27, 2026 receipt date found in ECF 137, which came from his earlier declaration (ECF 131-1). Kumar must confirm both. ¶ 7 has a blank for the date he received the Government's motion. If that date was on or before September 24, the good-cause showing weakens. In that case Part IV should lean more on the absence of any order and the fact that no ruling has issued.
11. Kumar Declaration format. The paragraphs are numbered "1." through "9." These are numbered paragraphs, not a list. Make sure the renderer does not turn them into a list.
12. Heading levels. The markup allows only "##" and "###". Argument points I-IV and sub-points A-C both use "###". If the renderer can set off the A-C subheadings (for example by indenting them), do so.
13. Kumar's pro se opening brief. The response cites "Appellant's Br. 47-50" for his proffer argument. That cite comes from the OCR text, using the internal page numbers 47-50 (PDF pp. 54-57). Verify against the filed brief.
14. Judge Kelley is referred to by name, with "she" used once, consistent with the client's confirmation. The Government's misspelling "Kelly" is not repeated.

### Self-check against the writer skill's failures

Failure 1 (transcribing dictated argument): the directives were treated as strategy, not text. One directive was corrected: the flat "timely" argument (item 1). Rule 27.0(d)'s "on a motion" limit and the standing-instructions risk are disclosed rather than ignored.

Failure 2 (rebuild that reorders): no prior draft or memo was opened. The brief was built from the record, rules and case texts.

Failure 3 (fabricated quotations): every quotation was string-checked against the primary text. One bracketed alteration of the proffer letter ("will [not] be used") was caught and replaced with the exact sentence. Quotations from Hill and Lee were verified as text, but their pinpoints are flagged above.

Failure 4 (production process): there is no narration of how any document was obtained.

Failure 5 (rhetorical tic): short punch sentences are used about once a page. A repeated "Kumar gave up nothing" / "Kumar relinquished nothing" was caught and rewritten. There are no rhetorical questions. Antithesis appears a few times with different sentence shapes.

Failure 6 (hostile narrative as fact): the false statements are stated as fact because Kumar pleaded to Count 3. The Government's characterizations (that its letter is "standard," the COVID account, that "all indications" showed Kumar would fight) are attributed to it. The PSR offense facts from the Government's motion are not adopted.

Failure 7 (hedging): the one genuine limit on the Rule 27.0(d) argument is stated in one sentence. The concessions (Count 3; that COAs are issue-specific; Rule 11(a)) are each made once and cabined.

Overclaiming check: the preliminary statement says the truth is what the case on Counts 1 and 2 "was built on." It does not say it is what "convicted" him. It is supported by ECF 75 at 2 and ECF 130 at 20. Two claims are stated as argument with record support, not as findings: that the "unusual facts" run through every claim, and that the charging date "was a choice."
